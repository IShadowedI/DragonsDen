Tabletop Battles Reviews: Wyldwood Box and Miniatures

October 10th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Wyldwood is the newest board game from Tabletop Titans; a Stone Age era game with a unique style played on a hex-based board. One of the things that distinguishes this game from the field is its scale: Wyldwood uses 60mm display miniatures for the models. Additionally, the game eschews dice in favor of a mix of cards from a deck and individualized abilities on your character boards. If you've been looking for a hex-based game with the kinds of miniatures you'd expect to enter a painting competition with, this game might be a good fit for you.

We'd like to thank Tabletop Titans for providing us with a preview copy of the upcoming Kickstarter boxes for review.

So far, we've had the chance to see two of the factions from Wyldwood's Kickstarter campaign. In this article, we're going to explore painting the Leafsong Nomads, unpack some of the contents of the Kickstarter boxes, and look at how the game plays.

There's a lot of hype around Wyldwood, and a lot of that can be traced back to the game's Adepticon debut. Tabletop Titans are currently running a Kickstarter campaign, which is live now so make sure to check it out.

Models

Blake Law: These models are as good as it gets. Consider Wyldwood to be the new top tier standard for sculpts and casting. The sculpts are done by Creature Caster, which is widely known for their insane detail on resin models. It carries a lot of name recognition, and with good reason. These miniatures have been cast in Unicool, which is a resin/plastic hybrid. There is plenty of Unicool reviews on this site, so make sure to look them up!  In the end, you have a product that is sculpted by one of the best in the industry and cast with one of the best materials on the market. It assembles seamlessly. I did no additional work on any of these miniatures. Each piece typically has one gate to cut off. There are no mold lines on any of these models. I had no issues with any of the pieces fitting together. You have no instruction manual on how to build these, and that is because they are so intuitive and easy; you cannot get better than this in terms of assembly and ease. Unicool sometimes has an issue with bending of parts that require reheating. I had no issues at all in that regard with Wyldwood models. The larger models in the line (typically the leaders) are massive. This can be somewhat of an issue if your work space is small. I had to upgrade my lightbox space to be able to capture them in photo!

The Box Contents

Blake Law: I got access to two of the single faction starter boxes.  I will be discussing them in general terms. If you're looking for more specific details on specific cards, stay tuned: We'll be reviewing those in a separate article, soon.

I want to discuss the box that they come in first.  The art is actually spectacular.  There is even quality art lining the inside of the box.  It is a nice  touch that gives the game a high end feel.  I was scared to haul my models inside the lid (as I typically do) because I did not want to damage it.  You get three cardboard punch outs that contain several key components of the game.  The first two are terrain feature punch outs.  These make four trees and two bushes.  They match the art style of the game perfectly and are quite vibrant  (see photo).  I did not notice any issues with these punching out.  The next punch out contains the tokens for status effects.  The art is again very on par with the rest of the game.  I did notice some issues with the edges of tokens not punching quite perfect.  When I go back and finish my Leafsong Nomads, I will be using an exacto blade to clean the edges.  I also like to line the edges of cardboard with black sharpie.  It always gives these kind of games a nicer feel on tabletop.  You get a cardboard dial for each character in your faction.  They include the rivets to complete each of these dials.  As you complete parts of the character dials, you get to use the cute little spirit creatures to fill the empty spots on dials.

The Kickstarter campaign has the option to get the printed versions (versus the standard cardboard tokens).  I highly recommend this, as it adds a value of visual aesthetic to the game.  You get two decks per faction.  The print quality is very nice, and you will probably want to get these in sleeves to protect the art.  The last component is the game board itself.  It comes folded, and has a back side which is a map of the world.  It is a very nice touch.  There is a neoprene mat that you can get on the Kickstarter, and I would again go for this if you plan to play a lot of games.  While the mat is a durable paper material, it will very likely fail over time.  Also, I intended to frame the backside map over my game table!

The two starter boxes that I reviewed came with five and seven models.  I believe five is around the standard for most of the factions.  Leafsong Nomads have five base characters, and two creatures which operate off of the Wyldspeaker's abilities.  Leafsong Nomads come with Enduanna, The Wispwarden (leader), Wyldspeaker, Boneblade Alpha, Loresinger, and Wyrdbow Sentinel.  You get two creatures in addition (Sabrecat and Thistlehare), which play off the Wyldspeaker.  The Emberhold Ascended box comes with Alulim, The First King (leader), Fellbrand Woodsman, Ashbolt Marksman, Fuelwright Master, and Boughrunner.  I want to reiterate how massive these models are.  They could be easily used by someone looking to paint a competition caliber piece.  If you have not dealt with 60mm before, it is a ton of fun.  See the pictures below of all the amazing models!

Emma: So Blake's given the detailed specs here, and I'm only going to chip in to back up his comments on the aesthetics. The models absolutely rip. The Stone Age theming is subtle outside of the dwarf faction, Urscar Kindred, as the other factions either have heavy fantasy elements or are deliberately up-teched (the human Emberhold Ascended), but definitely present. If you're not a fan of painting metal, this is the game for you. 

The large scale allows for oodles of detail and character, as do the sculpted bases. As a Nightwood Covenant (orcs) player, I'm naturally biased towards their stuff, with their gorgeous nature theming and dynamic effects (the Stormwarden's lightning springs to mind), but every range has something to love. The visual identity of each faction and model is distinct, and I echo Blake's comments that this game is a painter's dream. Equally, for the non-painters among us, I reckon you could easily achieve a serviceable job with way less time than it would take you to finish a couple squads in a smaller scale game. It has a nice balance for accessibility of a low floor for entry and a high ceiling for improvement.

Paint

Blake Law: I started off by priming the models with Color Forge Spray Matt Black.  I then use my airbrush to apply the green over the models.  This was a progression of Pro Acryl Black Green, Pro Acryl Green, then Pro Acryl Bright Yellow Green.  Then, I applied skin tones with an airbrush on every model except the Sabrecat.  This was a progression of Pro Acryl Tan Flesh, Pro Acryl Bright Dark Warm Flesh, and then Pro Acryl Bright Shadow Flesh.   At this point, I went back in with a dry brush and lightly reinforced the brightest points of the green and the flesh tones.  The next step was applying the bone tone on the antlers and other antler-like parts.  This was done with Pro Acryl Green Brown, blended into Pro Acryl Bone, blended with Pro Acryl Bright Ivory.  The key is to blend the edges of the colors, but not so much that it's a true wet blend.  You want it to be a distinct color on each part, tapering to the edge.  I use Citadel Colour Contrast Skeleton Horde over that final blend.  You want to make sure you take some off the brightest points, as it will wash out the blend otherwise.

For the wooden components, I start with Pro Acryl Mahogany.  I blend that into a 50/50 mix of Mahogany and Pro Acryl Bright Pale Yellow.   I finish it off by fine lining Bright Pale Yellow over the blend.  The final step is applying Scalecolor Inktense Wood.  This gives it a blended look and brings the yellow back to a brown tone.  For my orange hair, I use a progression of Army Painter Fanatic Molten Lava, Army Painter Fanatic Lava Orange, and Army Painter Fanatic Glowing Inferno.

For my feathers, I use a stippling technique with my brush.  This is done with the colors Pro Acryl Light Umber, Pro Acryl Dark Ivory, to AK Interactive White I went back in and did some edge highlighting with the Bright Yellow Green over all the green plant parts of the models.  I applied an OSL with Reaper Master Series Clear Magenta.  This was done mostly with airbrush, but I go back in and clean up parts I want more glow on with a brush.

The final touch is the rocks on the bases.  I start with AK Interactive Black on all my bases.  I work this up with Pro Acryl Dark Neutral Grey,  Pro Acryl Neutral Grey, to Pro Acryl Bone.

Gameplay

Emma: My time to shine. 

I've been jamming game after game of this since I became aware of the Kickstarter, and would encourage anyone who wishes to give it a go to grab TTS and play around a bit. This isn't a review of the TTS mod, though, but of the game itself, and I am extremely happy with it. Rather than going into a deep dive on how the game works, which you can find on the Kickstarter itself in various forms, I'm instead going to give an overview of the mechanics, and then get into my opinions thereupon.

First things first; this is a diceless game. Variance is decided with three decks.

The first is the Location deck, a neutral fixed deck that determines terrain placement. This strikes a good middle ground between changing up the game-to-game experience, with different deployment zones and terrain locations. It's always four trees and two rocks at the time of writing, so it's not War and Peace to set up, but it does make a surprising amount of difference where things are. I like the balance struck here.

The second is the Omens deck, another neutral fixed deck that sets out where Wisps spawn (forest friends you collect to score points), and any environmental effects (such as a monster attack, flash flood, or portents of bloodshed). These have varying levels of impact, but are always relevant because of how much Wisps matter. At the time of writing, the exact rules are being tweaked, but are close to their final form.

The third is your own deck, which you construct from a faction card pool. Deck size is 30, so outcomes are reasonably consistent, but not so consistent that you can expect to do the same things every game. You pick an always-on ability for your faction from three options, like a detachment rule or formation from 40k/AoS, and then 30+ cards to form the deck itself. If you have passing familiarity with card games, the deckbuilding will make intuitive sense to you, but equally any wargamer can jump right in and know what things are doing. There's a whole bunch of depth and variation to it, and you could easily get completely different experiences each game even with the same deck, never mind if you mix it up a bit every so often.

Cards are balanced by their Fate cost, copy limit, and which models can use them. All have access to some generic faction options, while each model also has its own specific picks. For example, there's an any-model card in the Urscar Kindred dwarfs that lets a model make an extra move, while their leader model, Rurik, has access to a card that lets him move and then also maul someone with his giant bear.

The game has an online deckbuilder [link] with all the rules freely available, and I've spent a lot of time just jamming decks together. It only takes a few minutes per deck, and the cards themselves are very short - no more than 20 words per card, and most have fewer than that - so the skill expression isn't in memorising a bunch of text, it's in balancing whose activations you get to make all spicy.

As for gameplay itself, you have two objectives. Collect Wisps, the adorable little forest friends, and kill people. Do those things, and you score. The game goes for six rounds, with up to five activations per side, and speeds up fast as models die. Experienced wargamers will see the typical pattern of round 1 setup and jockeying for position, big action on rounds 2 and 3, then rounds 4 through 6 are fast paced and bloody as Fate currency access ramps up and the big cards start being played. High damage takes investment, but the health totals are in the sweet spot of not being paper thin (even the elves), and not being walking tanks (even the dwarfs).

Trust me, though, games can swing hard. Victory is determined at the end of the turn, and I've seen things flip from one person being on track to win to the other multiple times before a turn ends. There's a careful balance to strike of conservatism and aggression, particularly because you can - and will - lose points as well as gain them. You're seldom fully shut out of a game. 

Play time length, as a result, is pretty quick. After two weeks of playing, I was able to set up and finish inside of an hour and change, and that was including deckbuilding on the spot (generally, you'd come in with a prepared deck). Setting aside two hours for a game would allow plenty of time for social and chill, while potentially enabling a rematch if you fancied it. The fastest game I've ever played was about 40 minutes all told, and went to turn 4, so even rapid back-and-forth still gives you plenty of opportunities to do cool stuff.

If I'm making it sound all sunshine and rainbows, it's because it kind of... is. This is just a good game. It scratches the skirmish game itch for me perfectly, and I intend to play it a lot. That said, I have my unbiased reviewer hat on, so I want to point out a couple of issues.

Two of the Omen cards (out of 20 currently) have the potential to gotcha someone with an early game negative status effect, such as a model being locked in place or made to take minor damage over time. Nothing world ending, and the kind of thing you can trivially avoid if you know it's a possibility (basically, you just don't stand on the edge of deployment in a couple places), but I can see some people grumbling about it if they get got in their first game. I can see this changing before release, as the devs are sharpening things daily, so don't count on this being true even at the time of publication - I'm just obliged to review based on the rules as they exist right now.

The second is that, on release, every faction is model-locked. You need five in a list, everyone has five. So, while the deckbuilding is endlessly replayable, if you play a whole lot, you might want to see some new faces from time to time. That said, this is a temporary problem. By the time the Kickstarter ships, we're expecting one or two extra releases for each faction, which should vary things up. Again, though, I can't comment on what I haven't seen.

Final Thoughts

Emma: I've played a lot of wargames, and this one is easily one of my favourites. Definitely my favourite in the skirmish-level space. From what I understand of the Kickstarter, all the models are already with the factory, and they're just assessing demand levels, so the risk involved is low. I tried to decide what was my favourite part of the rules while writing this, and the number of options I wanted to put forward is a good sign. I tried asking the community, and everyone had something different to raise. So, I'll pick something minor and funny.

Witherwood Legions (the zombie fungus tree faction) has a leader that's just a giant, immobile tree that spawns tree tentacles. Its name is Yewen. Makes me laugh every time.
