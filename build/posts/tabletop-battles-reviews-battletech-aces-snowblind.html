Tabletop Battles Reviews: BattleTech Aces - Snowblind

November 27th, 2026 via Goonhammer

BattleTech Aces: Snowblind is Catalyst’s second release in the Aces series, an automated single player campaign for Alpha Strike. Aces uses behavior cards based on unit role to run the enemy mechs, mixing randomness in exactly what the units do with constraining the range of options to things that make sense for the unit. I liked the previous box, Scouring Sands (which we reviewed previously), and Snowblind is more of the same. Once again, you’re playing as a mercenary company, this time deployed on the planet Thule II in the Rasalhague Dominion. At the moment, the Rasalhague Dominion is going through a civil war - some wanted to join the ilClan, and some didn’t - there are also some opportunistic pirates on the planet that you’ll end up fighting. If you want to read more of the backstory around this campaign, check out the Dominions Divided sourcebook and the Dominion Civil War novella series.

It’s worth noting here that you will need either the Alpha Strike box set, the Alpha Strike counters pack, or other matching terrain. The missions in this campaign make use of the terrain in those products, and the way the Aces missions work you want buildings of identical size. Two tutorial missions make use of the Alpha Strike mechs, but they’re not required for the actual campaign at any point. You’ll also need a tape measure and a variety of dice, like you would for other games of Alpha Strike.

We’d like to thank Catalyst Game Labs for sending us free copies of Snowblind for this review.

What’s in Snowblind?

Ghost Bear mechs in Aces: Snowblind. Credit: Jack Hunter

Snowblind has eight ‘mechs in the box. Two are totally new, the Rime Otter and Viking IIC, and the rest are new poses or variants - from largest to smallest we have the Kingfisher C, Nova Cat B (this variant is also available in metal from Iron Wind Metals), Grizzly 3 (just a new pose, while it is a new variant the weapons are essentially identical), Grendel A, Arctic Cheetah A, and Fire Moth G. My copy of these is on-par for detail with the rest of the recent Battletech releases. They’re PVC, which is a little softer than Warhammer models, so if you’re a new player used to GW you may be a little surprised, but they do still paint up very nicely. Unlike Scouring Sands I didn’t see any minis that seemed to have any defects, they’re the same high quality as the recent Core Box release.

Clan Ghost Bear Tau Galaxy Nova Cat D and Viking IIC (3D-printed prototype). Credit: Valk.

Valk: My experience here was mostly the same as Jack's, but there are still some irritating mold lines that I found myself continuing to pick away at halfway through my painting process. The Fire Moth in particular has some really annoying flash along the top of the left torso, but it's overall no worse than any other CGL plastics I've seen (though I will agree with Jack that the Core Box is the current high water mark for quality).

Other items in the box are two rulebooks, a couple sheets of punchout tokens and terrain, all the Aces cards you need, Alpha Strike cards for the ‘mechs and vehicle tokens in the box, Battlefield Support asset cards for the elemental, vehicle, and emplacement tokens (these are just for classic BattleTech and not needed for Aces), and pilot cards and campaign record sheets for playing Scouring Sands. In the Battlefield Support cards are a couple cards for the Fulcrum hovertank in Scouring Sands - these were missing from that box, so it’s nice that they were included here. I hope that future print runs of Scouring Sands also include them. The two rulebooks are a 40 page booklet covering the core rules for Aces and one nicely spiral bound campaign book with all 20 Snowblind scenarios as well as two training scenarios to learn the system before diving in. The first training scenario is a guided tutorial that walks through everything you do in a game step-by-step. If you want to follow along and play it out at the same time, you would need to use the Aces cards from Scouring Sands, but the cards being used are re-printed in the campaign book here and you can just reference it instead of the separate cards.

The Aces rulebook is not quite identical to the one from Scouring Sands. The rules for Indirect Fire changed to have spotters declared before any other attacks by the player, which I think is mostly just a clarity-of-play adjustment and not anything for balance. There are a couple clarifications made around how to read the automation cards, but I don’t see any actual changes to gameplay. The campaign section also has some clarifications and adds a new Infantry Barracks, allowing you to field infantry and battle armor units without them dying constantly.

Clan Ghost Bear Tau Galaxy Fire Moth G. Credit: Valk.

Valk: The change to the way Aces handles infantry and battle armor for player forces is a huge improvement. I know a lot of folks play Aces with just ‘Mechs and armor, but having battle armor specifically is hugely helpful for tearing down units that would otherwise be extremely dangerous to engage head-on with ‘Mechs. When I played Scouring Sands, I immediately reached for Fa Shih (Plasma) squads to help debuff big, slow problems like the Bane 3, and they’ve thus far been super useful in Snowblind as well. By changing a “purchase” of battle armor squads to buying a barracks that you can deploy up to three squads from each turn, the system now offers a lot of extra tactical flexibility for combined arms without it weighing down your roster with fragile, difficult-to-replace infantry.

Snowblind punch-out walls and tokens.

There’s a good selection of terrain in the box - six hills in three pairs, all one inch high, two big buildings, four wall sections, and four sets of solar panels. I like that the hills are only one inch high, as they get stacked in a variety of interesting ways in the mission layouts. The walls were a little tough to push together. Not too bad, but if the dies had cut from the other side of the cardboard it would be easier. I found a flathead screwdriver helpful to punch out some of the small bits in the wall tops.

There are also quite a few tokens for vehicles, infantry, and civilian mobs, enough to cover anything you’ll need while running the campaign. If you want to expand out to use plastic minis, you’ll need:

  • 2 Manticore Heavy Tank
  • 2 Vedette Medium Tank
  • 4 Elementals
  • 2 SRM Carrier
  • 2 LRM Carrier
  • 2 Mobile Long Tom Artillery
  • A variety (up to 6) emplacements, but I can’t recommend buying the emplacements force pack.

What Is Aces?

Aces is the card system that’s used to drive the automated force’s decision making. It’s not specific to the Snowblind campaign, and was used in Scouring Sands and will be used in some more future campaigns. Each unit gets its own small deck of cards based on unit type, plus the force as a whole gets a deck of commander cards to drive overall priorities. The cards are a bit icon-dense, so in my first playthrough it did take a while to work out what to do, but by the end of the second game I was getting the hang of it and the game was moving at a decent pace, though not quite as fast as with a person on the other side of the table. There’s a handy quick reference for them on the back of the Aces rulebook. While I’d prefer it to be on a quick reference card so I can see it at the same time as I have the rulebook open, it’s still incredibly useful.

I went through the Aces system in depth in my Scouring Sands review, so if you want to see how it plays I’d jump over there.

Snowblind

Credit: Jack Hunter

Snowblind is the name of the campaign in this box, with 20 potential different missions (referred to as Sorties). You won’t play through all of them in a single campaign, as your choices and success in each sortie determine which future sorties you’ll play out. This gives you some good replayability, where you can run the campaign several times without running into identical missions (especially because of the randomness of the cards, so even playing with the exact same setup you won’t have the game play out the same).

Campaign Setup

You’re going to start the campaign by building a force of up to 400 PV with at least eight units. As long as you’re taking a range of weight classes of units that’s a pretty substantial budget. All eight Inner Sphere mechs in the Alpha strike box are at most 316 PV, so it’s pretty straightforward to take a force in the 8-12 unit range. Don’t stress too hard about hitting exactly 400 PV; for every PV you are under that cap you’ll get 40 SP, which is going to be very useful as you spend your funds on repairs. Unlike in Scouring Sands you don’t need to use the Advanced Force Creation options to get that refund, but I do think it still adds some interest to do so. Advanced force creation mostly just tells you to use the IlClan Mercenaries availability list on Master Unit List and limits your ability to take duplicates of units. Like in Scouring Sands I think you’re going to want to lean towards forces with a larger number of moderate cost units - sorties vary from 180 PV to 400 PV, so you’ll need variety to hit those sizes regardless of losses, but you don’t want to have too many small units as each one you field will cost 20 SP to re-arm.

Credit: Jack Hunter

You’re also going to create between 2 and 6 named pilots to act as heroes of your force. Each of these pilots gains experience over the course of the campaign, able to either improve their skill (ability to hit things), edge (which can be spent each game on modifying or re-rolling certain rolls or using an edge ability), and purchasing edge abilities that give them a few powerful actions they can do in game. Pilot experience is measured in SP, the same campaign currency you’ll use for repairs, purchasing new units, and unlocking certain options during each sortie. Each pilot starts with 150 free SP, but creating too many pilots is a risk – each one takes a chunk of SP from your sortie rewards, even if you don’t field them. While it does happen after you pay for repairs, having too many named pilots can make it hard to build up a warchest and purchase replacement units if needed.

Valk: Minor tangent, mostly because it’s funny: Snowblind introduces a civilian mob unit type to Alpha Strike, which can remain peaceful or turn “frenzied.” A note that I will leave for players doing force building ahead of the game arriving is that there are rules for “less-lethal” munitions to be swapped in on units with the LRM, SRM, or FLK special abilities specifically to manage these mobs. You’ll have to be careful about which units you choose for crowd control since it will reduce your overall damage output to have the option, but it’s a good idea to have at least a few units capable of using less-lethal munitions.

That said, “FLK” in this context translates to “LB-X autocannons” and “HAGs.” I’m sure you can load an LB-X with rubber ball shot and lightened powder charges, but the idea of making a HAG “less-lethal” is hilarious to me. Here’s a ‘Mech-scale Red Ryder here to shoot your eye out. Merry Christmas!

Sorties

Credit: Jack Hunter

Like in Scouring Sands, each sortie has 2 pages of information, combining some narrative about the story, how to set up the map (designed for a 48” x 48” play area), what size your force should be, the opposing force, and your objectives. This system worked well in Scouring Sands and I’m glad to see it return unchanged, though I will still criticize how trees get set up. I’d suggest getting some string to mark out each forest area and make it clearer - there are enough different shapes that I think it would be impractical to cut it out of paper.

For a more detailed look at how the sorties work, hop back over to my Scouring Sands review.

Valk: Having played a few sorties of Snowblind in a GM role, I’m going to largely agree with Jack here – if you played Scouring Sands, you’ll find this to be more of the same, for better or worse. However, there’s still a handful of little annoyances I have that I’m hoping to see resolved in later sets. The biggest headache, for me, is that there are still missions where the automated force is expected to run multiple bases of foot infantry, all using the same deck. One early mission where I encountered this had me running no less than five bases of mechanized pirate infantry, which were meant to share the same six-card deck. It feels like an unnecessary amount of overhead that players are expected to manage manually, and killing all the little infantry squads didn’t feel particularly satisfying either. If we had a companion app of some sort to reveal the action cards, it’d be much more manageable, but for that sortie in particular I was taking up nearly as much space as the game itself just to keep all the cards visible to both sides. Maybe other people have the mental bandwidth to tolerate manipulating all of these individual little dudes and their cards while also running the rest of the force, but I found it something that could probably be streamlined.

The other thing I’d like to see addressed in future products is some level of foresight about edge cases for Waypoint reveals. The first sortie my players engaged in had a mine go off when they revealed multiple waypoints, and, as best as I could tell, the fact that a VTOL at high altitude found them didn’t change the fact that it caused damage. It feels clear to me that CGL wants Aces to be played with combined arms forces, so interactions like this should be seen ahead of time, but as before, it’s more a nitpick than anything else.

Clan Ghost Bear Tau Galaxy Grizzly and Rime Otter. Credit: Valk.

Verdict

There are very very few mechanical differences between Snowblind and Scouring Sands. If you liked Scouring Sands, I’m confident that you’ll like Snowblind as well. Even if you don’t, the box has a good selection of good looking minis and is probably worth it for that alone, especially if you play Alpha Strike or know an Alpha Strike player that can make use of the terrain. I do prefer to play against other humans rather than playing solo or co-op against the cards, but the system does work well. Every game I’ve played of it feels different, with enough challenge that I’m not just beating up a computer, and as long as I keep in mind the golden rule of “just do something that makes sense” figuring out the behavior cards doesn’t take me too much out of the game.
