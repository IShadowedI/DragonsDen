Hextoricals: Hubris - Twilight of the Hellenistic World 220-165 BCE

July 9th, 2026 via Goonhammer
It is a time of crisis within the Seleucid Empire, but also of opportunity. After having crushed the rebellions in Media and Babylonia and brought those regions back into the Empire, Antiochos III sees the weakness of the Ptolemaic Kingdom to the south and starts a war with Ptolemaios IV Philopator in order to take back the satrapies of Syria. However, in the west, his general Achaios, seeing that Antiochos III’s armies are busy, takes this as an opportunity to revolt and declare an independent kingdom in Phrygia. In Macedonia and Greece, meanwhile, King Philippos V has to deal with both the unruly Aitolians, as well as intrigue within his own court as Apelles, his Chief Minister, attempts to retain as much control as possible within the Kingdom. Rome, meanwhile, is trying to deal with Illyrian pirates, but tensions are rising with Carthage once again, setting the stage for one final, apocalyptic showdown for control of the western Mediterranean Sea.

It is 220 BCE, and the fate of the remaining Alexandrian successor states is about to change forever.

Hubris is not GMT Games’ first foray in the world of the successor states, with Successors using the time-tested CDG systems to tell the story of the Alexandrian generals fighting against each other in order to set up their own kingdoms right after the death of Alexander in 323 BCE. The main difference between the two titles (apart from the fact that Hubris is set in 220 BCE) is their relative focus: while Successors is primarily a wargame where you are clashing directly with your three other opponents, Hubris aims to tell a much more detailed story, one in which you need to worry about your own internal finances, potential revolts and rebellions, treacherous ministers within your own court as well as the looming threat of Rome.

As such, it aims to be more of a simulation and, dare I say it, an experience generator than many other wargames; although some (like me) will find this extremely compelling, others will likely chafe against the randomness and volatility of the design. Still, if you are willing to explore its system, I think you will find an extremely compelling game underneath the surface, a game that will provide twists and turns at every possible occasion, allowing you and your opponents to recreate the twilight of the Hellenistic world not as it happened historically, but as it could have happened, thanks to the shared decisions that you and your friends have made.

Unboxing and Materials

One of the things that I really enjoy about GMT productions is that they provide a lot of historical background within their games; one of the first things that you notice upon opening its box is that Hubris has an entire booklet dedicated to just outlining the history of the era, including the various events and personae that are likely to affect the game. Along with this Companion Book, there is also the main Rule Book, a Learn to Play Book which includes an annotated example of play, as well as a Solo Campaign Book in case you lack friends crazy enough to play this behemoth of a game with you.

All of the components present in Hubris. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

The game also provides you with three cheat sheets, one for each player, along with a cheat sheet to run Rome, the non-player Kingdom of the game (all four sides in the game are labelled as Kingdoms for ease of reference).

The map of Hubris is centered around the Eastern Mediterranean, with a focus on Greece, Macedonia, Thrace, Asia Minor, Mesopotamia, Egypt and Syria. It is mounted, which is always welcome, and the map itself is beautiful to look at and with clear iconography, albeit with some issues that I will detail later. The map is divided into satrapies; they have a mixture of cities and tribes that you need to control in order to gain VP and tax revenue.

Each Kingdom uses many materials to represent itself; military or diplomatic control markers, victory point trackers, treasury markers, armies, individual units and the all-important leader cards that combine to make your Court. Rome is a tad different in that it only has three leaders, one for each player Kingdom, along with several different decks of cards that show at which stage the Punic War is currently at or what the current target of Rome is.

Finally, the game provides you with event cards that are the engine that drives the game, satrapy reference cards to make it easier to calculate your VPs and tax revenue, as well as other general markers to mark revolts or areas that are bereft of resources.

Overall, the production values of Hubris are stellar, like most other recent GMT productions, but it does come with a price tag of $93.

Gameplay

I’m not going to beat around the bush: Hubris is not a simple game to either teach or learn. Although the level of detail within the game is outstanding, this does come at a cost: There’s a lot of chrome and rules exceptions present within the design. It took me several read-throughs of the rulebook and the learn-to-play book in order for me to finally get a grasp on the rules. You as well as your opponents will need to put in the time and effort necessary in order to get the most out of this game and for some the investment will just not be worth it; Hubris is very much what I consider a “marmite” game.

The starting setup of the 220 BCE Scenario. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

If you are not familiar with the faintly disgusting yeast spread, marmite used to advertise itself with the slogan “Love it or hate it,” and this slogan is very applicable to Hubris as well, mostly due to how incredibly random the game can be. It is clear that the design is meant to evoke the era and create a story rather than be a tightly designed competitive system, and this clear focus is present at every stage of the design, leading to unexpected (and many times unfair) situations.

The two main gears of the game are the event deck and the court system. The former is the driving force behind the game: You shuffle the deck at the start of each turn, and then reveal and resolve the event cards one by one. There are six permanent events that remain in the deck throughout the game, and they represent situations like rebellions, pirates, natural death etcetera. They also have a turn order at the bottom, so once you have finished resolving the event, the players (and Rome) get to perform an action, in the given sequence. However, a turn can end at any time after the players have each performed a minimum of four actions, which can be devastating if your plans relied on performing just one action more.

An example of some of the event cards present in the game. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

The rest of the event cards are seeded into the deck depending on which turn it is, and are usually one-offs. These can either be devastating to a particular player, or be a small bonus for the player that decides to spend one of their limited actions to fire the event. Knowledge of what is coming up can certainly help, but events are not guaranteed to happen in the turn that they are seeded, which means that potential landmines that you could have easily dealt with in earlier turns can destroy you in later turns.

The uncertainty and randomness of the event deck is both a blessing and a curse: It will lead to exciting events that dramatically affect your Kingdom, but you could potentially end up with three or four events in a row that just crush any hopes of victory, with very little counter-play possible. Although the game will generally prevent you from being completely crushed, and there are ways to return to former glory, it is possible to be eliminated from the game, especially once Rome rears its ugly head and the legions start marching towards your capital.

The Ptolemaic Kingdom and Seleucid Empire fighting in Syria. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

So, how do you prevent your Kingdom from falling? The other main mechanism within the game is the Court system; each Kingdom has a series of important historical figures that are used in order to lead your armies, perform diplomacy or even bribe Rome, if required. Each leader has various different attributes; military ability, both in open battles and sieges, diplomatic ability, administrative ability, loyalty ratings and intrigue ratings. Many of them will also feature special abilities that give an additional sprinkling of historical flavour to the game. Leaders will enter and leave your court throughout the game, and managing your court is one of the most important aspects of the game.

The actions that are available to you are divided into three types; military, administrative and diplomatic. Every time you perform an action, you are required to select a leader to perform it, but only if they have the ability in question; a brilliant diplomat might not be able to lead an army, after all. Leaders can only be activated twice per turn (although some, like Ptolemaios IV, are lazy and can only activate once), which can lead to some brinkmanship between the players since exhausting your best general early might leave you unable to react to a potential invasion.

Macedonia and Rome coming to blows in Illyria. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

Military actions encompass things such as land campaigns, naval raids and naval invasions. The army system and combat resolution within the game is relatively simple: your leaders can be placed on the map as strategos and in order to move units around the map, you need to pay money for each unit you deploy. When two armies meet, you roll a number of dice equal to the number of units in your army, and any result lower or equal to the military ability of your leader scores a hit, which ultimately decides who wins the engagement. Administrative actions tend to be simpler: either raise more taxes, or build expensive monuments to the glory of your dynasty, although the former has the danger of creating popular revolts against your rule. Finally, diplomatic actions are aimed at either getting independents to become your military allies, gain influence in Rome or even bribe Rome to declare war on one of your rivals.

Although the above sounds rather simple, there are a multitude of auxiliary systems bolted on to this framework. Cities can revolt against your rule, eventually becoming independent nations if the revolts are not squashed. Your generals on the board can rebel against you if their loyalty is low, creating new splinter Kingdoms that can be difficult to bring back into the fold. Leaders can die of natural causes or, if your court has too many hot-heads, can be assassinated due to internal intrigues, which in the worst cases can even target your King. If you attack an opponent, you are at war with them and war expenses and weariness can rear their ugly heads, but declaring peace makes it permanent until either of your Kings die. The death of a King can lead to a succession crisis which can cripple your Kingdom with an internal schism.

The Seleucids dealing with some rebels. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

If I could use a single word to describe this game, it would be “daring”. There is an entire system to automate the actions of Rome, and Rome’s aggression greatly colours the latter stages of the game. However, it is entirely possible for Rome to lose the Punic war to Carthage, and this removes the presence of Rome from the game entirely. Leaving a decision such as this to a roll of a dice is incredibly daring, in my opinion, and really shows that the designer wanted each individual game to be its own story, with wildly different outcomes.

Historicity

My main era of interest tends to be the 19th century, so I don’t have much knowledge of the wars of Alexandrian succession or the Roman takeover of Greece and Asia Minor. The game, however, is positively champing at the bit in its earnestness to teach you about the era, something that is primarily showcased by the Companion book.

Naval squadrons clash in the Greek Archipelago. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

What I really appreciate about the game is something that I look for in many of the games that I love: The ability to tell your own stories, for better or worse. My first time playing Macedonia I fought a battle against Rome that I was pretty sure I would win, only to see my royal army driven from the field and Macedonia forced to surrender to the Romans. All of the influence that I’d carefully built in Greece was gone, and I was left with only two cities under my control, forcing me to rebuild from basically nothing: the story of my downfall and slow rebuild was exciting, even though I’d lost out of sheer bad luck.

Another time, playing the Ptolemaic Kingdom, I had a quick uneventful war with the Seleucids and turned to care for my own Kingdom and build some monuments; when Ptolemaios IV finally died, a succession crisis weakened me enough that the Seleucid player instantly declared war on me, driving their armies right into Egypt.

Hubris will never exactly mirror historical events, but it has enough verisimilitude that the stories that it creates feel correct to the era, and I think this is one of the main strengths of the design.

Critiques & Quirks

Do you ever get annoyed when some unlucky dice rolls destroy all your plans? Do you say “that’s bullshit” when three events in a row seemingly only target you and take you from a winning position to being in the bottom of the pack? Would you be unable to cope when your best general gets randomly killed and you lose an important battle due to his unexpected death? If the answer to all of the questions above is “yes”, then I would strongly recommend staying away from Hubris.

“Experience Generator” tends to be used derisively in the world of board games, and is usually reserved for games that have little in the way of actual mechanisms or interesting gameplay. I would not say that Hubris reaches such depths, but as I mentioned before, the game is fully designed with the expectation that the players will take the randomness in stride. Although wargamers tend to be more forgiving of randomness than euro gamers, this is usually with the expectation that you can minimise the effects of luck through good play; although this is still true for Hubris, I feel the design pushes the boundaries of what some players would be willing to accept.

The Romans pressure the Macedons after allying with most of the Greek city states. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

The events in the game also feel less directed than many other games; in Twilight Struggle, the events can be extremely powerful and unfair, but the only way that they get played is by a conscious choice by one of the players. On the other hand, the events in Hubris are up to the whims of fate.

Another issue with the game is that the level of chrome and discrete sub-systems within Hubris can feel overwhelming and the rulebook can struggle to make order out of the chaos; I strongly suggest reading the rules carefully both before and after your first game, because you will more than likely get a bunch of rules wrong, something that I noticed both in my initial playthroughs and the playthroughs of other groups that I observed.

Finally, there are some issues with the map design. Setting up the game can be difficult if you don’t have an encyclopedic knowledge of the ancient names of cities and locations. As well as that, some of the cities and tribes in the game cannot be swayed by diplomatic envoys, and can only be brought under your influence by direct siege or subjugation. The map shows this difference as bronze and silver coloured cities and tribes, which can be difficult to tell apart at a glance if you don’t have a direct comparison; a small icon would have served this function much better.

Final Thoughts

Personally, I love this design, but my recommendation does come with a lot of caveats. In order to enjoy Hubris, you really need to understand what the game is trying to do and accept the fact that things could go drastically wrong even if it’s not your fault. The storytelling elements in the game are extremely strong, but if you try to approach the game as a competitive exercise, you will be annoyed and disappointed by this design. It is trying to do something radically different and I really hope that they use this rules system for other eras of history; Hubris actually got me interested in ancient history for the first time, and for that alone I think it should be commended.
