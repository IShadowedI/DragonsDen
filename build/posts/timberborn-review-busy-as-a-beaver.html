Timberborn Review - Busy As A Beaver

May 20th, 2026 via Goonhammer
 

Pharaoh is one of the first games I ever owned for the PC; I remember receiving it for my birthday at the ripe old age of maybe ten or eleven. In the spring and summer that followed, I spent a parent-concerning amount of time advancing through the campaign, assembling ever-more complicated Ancient Egyptian cities (and learning a bit about Ancient Egypt along the way), listening to the incredibly evocative music and voice lines that are still branded into my memory 27 years later - the cheery Bazaar trader chirping, “I give the people what they want!”, the dismal homeless man moaning, “I’ve been kicked out of my home, and through no fault of my own!”, the hassled architect's “There are so many buildings in poor condition, I can barely keep up.” With the exception of monuments, buildings were built with money, and appeared on the map instantly, unlike the “acquire resources and have your villagers use them to build buildings” paradigm that I was familiar with from repeatedly replaying the demo of Age of Empires, houses always began at the basic mud-hut level, and through satisfying the needs of the residents they became fancier and fancier until those mud buildings, that had needed only a well to advance to the next stage, had been replaced with ornate palatial estates requiring access to pretty much everything you could possibly give them, exotic goods like carpets, oil lamps, religion (multiple flavours), police, fire department, scribal schools, mortuaries, dentists, doctors, entertainment (again, multiple flavours) and so on, in a way building a city in Pharaoh was most similar to building a watch, assembling hundreds of pieces just so. I remember how triumphant it felt to build that first pyramid, and later (in the Cleopatra expansion) to tackle the building of Abu Simbel, making tombs in the Valley of the Kings.

A few years later, I got myself a copy of Zeus: Master of Olympus, the tzatziki-flavoured cousin of Pharaoh. I played that for countless hours as well, although they’d amped up the humour; it wasn’t quite as good as Pharaoh had been, the introduction of more overt mythical elements like heroes and monsters (rather than the nebulous religious maluses inflicted by a neglected god in Pharaoh) shook loose a fair amount of the “realism.” Of all the genres, I think citybuilding is the one that defines my relationship with computer games (which is what we used to call them then). 



Citybuilding as a genre has had its ebbs and flows over the decades, falling from its turn-of-the-millennium heyday to a has-been genre for nostalgic millennials, with occasional triumphs and occasional brutal misfires, and the disastrous 2013 Sim City reboot nearly putting an axe in the genre for good. In the 2010s, the Sierra-style citybuilder gave way to the newer “colony sim” paradigm that now dominates the new “make a town” games popping up like mushrooms after a long-awaited rain. Colony sims, from their text-based grandfather Dwarf Fortress through to games like Banished, Frostpunk, Rimworld and so on, are more focused on the survival of a population clinging to the edge in some virgin landscape - an alien planet, the arctic, the new world etc. The environment is hard and unkind, and death is an ever-present threat. In the Sierra citybuilders, things could certainly go disastrously wrong, usually involving a situation where you run out of money, but there was always a flow of immigration to and from your city from the wider world, the population was represented only by a number that either went up or down. In colony sims, however, each member of the population is a specific character. The degree of characterisation certainly varies quite wildly, but at the very least each individual has a defined place where they live, a defined place where they work, and specific survival needs that have to be met. Bob lives in this house with Mary. They have a son called Tim. Bob works as a woodcutter. His hunger level is 50/100. Bob can starve to death, Bob can die of thirst, disease, cold - and if he does, who will cut the wood? Will the wood get cut at all?

Each beaver (like Tzuljin here) has its own needs that must be fulfilled, otherwise it'll die.

Unless you’ve been hiding under a pile of logs for the past five years, you’ve probably heard of Timberborn, the initial offering from Polish developers Mechanistry. If you haven’t, let me fill you in with a quick precis: Timberborn is a “lumberpunk” colony-sim style citybuilder, now out of early access. It's set in a post-apocalyptic future, wherein all of humanity has bitten the dust, and newly sentient beavers have risen to bite the wood and survive in the mangled and ruined world we left behind. Wait, really? Beavers? That’s right, the initial USP that set Timberborn apart from its rivals when it hit early access in 2021 is that the citizens are cute chompers of the Castor genus.

Gameplay will be broadly familiar to anyone who’s played a colony sim before; you start with a handful of beavers and an already-constructed town centre. From there, you’ll need to set someone to gather berries so your beavers don’t starve, cut down trees to build shelters and do research, and construct a water pump so they don’t die of thirst.

A dam with levees and floodgates

Speaking of water, the core mechanic of Timberborn and the thing that influences basically everything else your colony needs to survive, is how you control water (these are beavers, after all). Shortly after you start your colony, it will be hit by its first drought; the springs from which the water flows will all cease to function at once (typically, water rises from one of these springs and then flows following gravity along river channels until it leaves the map). The river will begin to dry up starting at the source and cascading downward. As soon as a block is more than a certain distance from a block of water, it will dry out. When that happens, all the plants growing on that block will begin to dry out, and if the block is still dry after a certain period, any plants on the block will die. Each type of plant dries out and dies at a different pace; wheat dries out faster than potatoes, which dry out faster than carrots, which dry out faster than pine trees and so on. Water pumps also cease to function when the block they’re drawing from dries out (obviously). The first drought will be relatively short and painless, but over time, they get longer and longer and considerably more dangerous for your poor beavers. Not only do they need to drink water to quench their thirst and grow food to eat to avoid dying; having dry fur is very uncomfortable for a poor little beaver, harming their ability to do their work, exacerbating problems associated with the production of survival-necessary resources even more. Beavers may be hardy little souls, but even they can’t endure deprivation for long before they start dying en masse. 



“Easy,” you might think, “I’ll just build a dam down the river to trap it, what’s so hard about that?” Well, not so fast there, friend - damming is a good start, you’re going to need to build and manage levees that block water altogether and dams that have a spillway on top. If you only build levees, during periods when the water is running, it’ll pile up behind the levee and eventually flood your settlement, but if you only build dams with a spillway, the amount of water you can retain in your lagoon is much less. Coupled with this problem, as soon as water is still and not being fed from the source, it begins to evaporate. A simple dam across the river might save your bacon in a short drought, but in a longer one… That shallow water is going to dry out as well, leaving you in the lurch and your beavers in the ground. You’ll need to create elaborate systems of waterways with levees, sluices, dams, water tanks, reservoirs, and irrigation channels to keep your settlement alive in the long-term.

Every once in a while, your regularly scheduled drought will be replaced with a “Badtide”. Rather than drying out, the water source feeding your rivers and ponds begins pumping out toxic, polluted “Badwater” (represented by red water and the nuclear symbol) that contaminates the surrounding terrain, killing all the plants growing there almost immediately. There’s usually a Badwater source or two on each map, constantly pumping out the nasty-looking red water, but during a Badtide, all water sources begin pumping out Badwater. Beavers that swim in Badwater get sick and will need medicine and a stay in a sickbed to recover fully. Interestingly, though, rather than simply being a disaster to be weathered or an environmental ill to be dealt with, Timberborn throws us a curveball - to build some of the most advanced buildings and resources you will need to harvest Badwater and turn it into the versatile resource “Essence”, requiring you to carefully manage the amount of “useful pollution” in your environment, later in your game you’ll be able to build wooden Robot Beavers that can do what real, living beavers do, but need constant infusions of food mixed with Badwater essence to keep working. 



With advanced enough beaver engineering, Badwater sources can be sealed with valves that can be opened on demand

Fluid mechanics and the way that water and Badwater behave on the map are really the lifeblood of Timberborn, Mechanistry have done a really great job in creating a simulated liquid mechanic, in an environment that is voxel-based, that behaves remarkably like real fluids, and learning to control the flow allows you to build some novel solutions to irrigation, like a high altitude aqueduct carrying water from one terrace to another.



As you can maybe tell from the picture above, the other defining feature of Timberborn is its sense of verticality. Terrain and buildings in Timberborn are based around voxels, with each tier just slightly bigger than a beaver is tall, there are some natural ramps built in that will let your beavers access a higher level, otherwise, your timber architects are going to have to build stairways, platforms, paths, and bridges to get where they need to go. Some of the buildings also have the “solid roof” trait, meaning that other things can be built on top of them with no penalty (as long as a path can reach the door), so your beaver-tropolis can have towering blocks of lodges reaching to the sky, or factories built atop storehouses to keep everything nice and compact. Our beavers are good little communists, not desiring wealth and working tirelessly for the collective, and so all the housing is the same (it doesn’t advance or grow like housing in Pharaoh etc) - the only difference between a large lodge and a small one is how many beavers you can squeeze in. 



Beaver technology marches on, and as of the latest update, sufficiently advanced beavers can move from one level to another quickly through the use of *ziplines*. Did I mention that they’re adorable? They’re so adorable.



Currently, there are two factions available to play, the work-with-nature Folktails and the work-against-nature Iron Teeth (unlocked after reaching a specific level with the Folktails); as the names suggest, the two factions play quite differently and with buildings unique to each. The Folktails focus on improved farming and “natural technology” like windmills, while the Iron Teeth focus on engineering, extracting metal from derelict human structures and using it to create engines to power their metropoles. They also reproduce very differently, while the Folktails do things the traditional, biblical way (two beavers get together, and a kit is born). The Iron Teeth reproduce in special tanks - food is added to one of the tanks over time, and after a while, a new kit pops out. Beavers are not born ready to work, though, their kits have a childhood growing period where they can’t do anything except meet their own needs by eating, drinking, having showers, playing, etc. Interestingly, the amount of time a beaver will spend as a kit is not simply determined by “how long they’ve been alive” but by their general well-being, and can be increased or decreased by how well those needs are met.

The base rate of maturity for a kit is 6 days, but a starving, thirsty kit will grow 40% slower, while kits with all of their needs well met grow up 75% faster than the baseline. Having a large number of kits that can’t produce anything, but do consume, can be really bad for a colony, especially if their maturation is slowed down by resource scarcity and in an already-stressed population. For this reason, Folktails settlements are far more susceptible to population booms that lead to overconsumption and mass die-offs, while the Iron Teeth can more carefully plan their population growth (and even put it on pause in times of stress). On the other hand, Folktails babies don’t cost anything to produce and just happen naturally. There’s something absolutely devastating in witnessing all the adults in a colony die just before the end of a badly timed drought, leaving behind some helpless kits who will also die, even though the water is running again, they can’t use the pump or harvest the berries!

Beavers work hard, but they like the good life too

Beavers are notoriously industrious, but in this brave new world, that industry requires power to function, and generating, managing, and distributing that power is another important part of Timberborn. There are four ways in which power is generated, with each faction having access to different varieties. Water, Beaver, Wind, and Steam. Water power involves putting wheels into the stream of rivers as they flow by, with the power output depending on the flow rate of the water (narrow, deep channels produce more power than broad, shallow ones, because the water moves faster through them). It is reasonably reliable, but obviously doesn’t function during drought. Beaver power has your workers run in a big hamster wheel, which doesn’t generate very much energy and prevents that worker from doing anything else, but also is not dependent on weather or drought status; Wind, which is exclusive to the Folktails, involves building windmills that generate about as much power as a water wheel but are unconnected from the drought cycle (wind doesn’t blow all the time, though!), Steam is exclusive to the Iron Tails and is generated by putting logs in a generator, reliable, but consumes quite a bit of resource to function. How does this power get distributed to the various factories? Well, by long chains of rotating logs, of course (these are beavers after all).

A barrage of water wheels and timber-based powerline

If it wasn’t already apparent from my gushing about it, I love Timberborn. I’ve loved Timberborn since early access, reinstalling it every once in a while to see what’s new with those cuddly citybuilders. In that time, it’s grown and blossomed from another Banished clone with a cute gimmick into an engagingly complex commercial and critical darling. If, like me, you love a city builder that gives you that “carefully assembling a watch” feeling, married with the anxious feeling that without your careful attention, all your adorable beavers might die of hunger when the drought goes on a bit too long, Timberborn is for you.
