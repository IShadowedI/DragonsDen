Tabletop Battles Reviews: TCG Card Shop Simulator

September 30th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Look, simulator games are tough. I don’t mean challenging; I mean the simulator genre overall has become so wildly oversaturated, and so filled with parodies of the genre, that it can be tough to trust your money when trying a new one. Yet, the simple promise of TCG Card Shop Simulator immediately enthralled my inner child and the hobby shop owner he may have grown into had he not been tied by the shackles of financial responsibility.

To be blunt, I am not a TCG player. The economy of acquiring ever-rotating cards just isn’t my jam. But, like many millennials, the Pokémon TCG was a foundational part of my childhood (and, presumably, a foundational part of my parents’ monthly budgets). That’s to say, I am the perfect, platonic ideal of the target market for TCG Card Shop Simulator, which focuses on managing a store and reproducing the dopamine hit of opening packs of cards (and even playing the game within the game).

Coolness of concept aside, the big question is this: Does TCG Card Shop Simulator have enough meat on its bones, and do the mechanics have enough depth and variety to stay fresh after the novelty has worn off? In a word, yes.

Started From the Bottom, Now We Here

As one might assume, players start TCG Simulator as a brand-new shop owner looking to turn their business into something great. Things don’t start quite at the bottom; the player gets a small storefront and 10 grand in pocket. That cash goes quickly, though, since owners need at least one shelf and some product to start slinging - not to mention having to account for rent, product delivery fees, etc.

The opening hours, while simple and small in scale, still feel exciting and introduce the game's basic elements, along with presenting a few milestones to hit. After I opened In It to Quinn It, I was directed to a virtual phone with a bunch of apps. It was overwhelming, but that’s why it’s great that things start slow. At first, all I needed to worry about was the DIY app, where I could order basic items like shelves, and a supplier app that let me grab a few boxes of basic booster packs. I set the price just a few cents above market value, flipped the CLOSED sign to OPEN, and a few people trickled in.

The game guided me through a few other tasks, and growth was fairly exponential. The money I earned turned into licenses to sell a few other types of packs, which turned into money to buy storage shelves so I didn’t have to keep product on the floor in the corner of my single room. That snowballed into small physical expansions (just a few square feet at a time), a card singles display/sales area, and eventually an employee to handle refilling the shelves while I worked the counter.

Freedom and Expansion

Fairly quickly, though, the guided objectives run out - I’m talking after an hour or less. Then, the game simply is, and the player is free to progress at whatever speed, and in whichever directions, they like. I’ll admit I would have liked to see a bit more in the way of milestones and objectives beyond that. The player earns XP by making sales, which unlock new product licenses to purchase, but it truly is a sandbox without aim. That isn’t a bad thing, but there might have been a middle ground to find or some log and reward for accomplishing different milestones - growing the store, offering new products, making new high sales, etc.

That said, there’s also something calming about not feeling a need to push on just for the sake of growth. I ended every day by restocking my shelves and storage, and most days I’d drain my account to unlock something cool to sell, buy store decor, or maybe add another play table. Other days, I just enjoyed the cash sitting in my account and progressed to the next day. In this way, TCG Simulator is an extremely easy game. I never felt any financial pressure, because (in a shocking departure from reality), my store was constantly serving a high flow of patrons willing to drop dozens to hundreds of dollars on packs/accessories and hundreds, thousands, or even tens of thousands on singles. If I spent down, I’d just leave my product as it was for a day and I’d be back up in no time. Keep an eye on when bills are due, and that’s about it.

Whether that’s a pro or a con depends on what a given player wants out of TCG Simulator. If you want a relaxing experience to work through at your own pace, this is a great system. If you want a challenge or a more guided milestone experience, this might not be the simulator for you.

How About the Card Game?

Since TCG Card Shop Simulator centers on a game, is the game itself actually interesting? Here, the phenomenon is called Tetramon, an homage both in name and design to the iconic Pokémon franchise. Beyond just selling the cards, there are a few other ways to interact with the product. First, you can just collect them. There are a few different sets, and cards have a variety of finishes - holographic, special borders, full art, etc. Moreover, players can actually play the game through tournaments in their store. I’ll be honest - this isn’t something I engaged with. I do enjoy digital card games, but I get my fix from fully fleshed-out digital TCGs. I’m here for the shop simulator mechanics, and playing the game - which wasn’t playable for much of the game’s early access run - didn’t tickle my fancy. Fortunately, given the game’s aforementioned freedom of pace and direction, this wasn’t an issue. In fact, it was probably great for my bottom line.

The Nitty Gritty

TCG Simulator gets huge props for making the nitty-gritty of monitoring and playing the market largely optional. As any TCG player knows, the value of individual cards, packs, and other products fluctuates wildly not only month to month, but sometimes day to day. For players who want to maximize profit and take advantage of market trends, an in-game app shows increases and decreases in product value (packs, not individual cards). Those who want to can order stock when the price drops (while still selling it for the same price) or hold it until the value comes back up and sell for even more. Moreover, players can look at the cards in their albums and see how a card’s current market value compares to its price history.

I didn’t personally do any of that. Every few nights I’d peruse my stock to make sure it was roughly in line with market value, but I sold my cards (after getting the most valuable graded) at a price appropriate to that moment. Some may enjoy diving into the data and min/maxing, but to me, the juice just isn’t worth the squeeze. A few more bucks don’t make or break anything, and my store kept growing just fine.

Employee management details matter a little more and, unfortunately, can be frustrating. Employees can be given a primary and secondary task, but there’s no central resource to see all employee tasks at once. While talking with individual employees, it doesn’t list their tasks, and there’s no way to simply remove a task. Employees also inconsistently prioritized the primary task over the secondary one. It’s ultimately not a huge deal, but it’s one of the very few frustrations I had with TCG Card Shop Simulator.

They say you shouldn’t mix business with pleasure, but apparently that doesn’t extend to mixing digital recreations of business with facsimiles of pleasure. Overall, TCG Card Shop Simulator is an addictive “just-one-more-day” experience that overperformed my expectations. It may not look pretty or sound particularly great, but the core loop is fun, and the freedom of action and pace make it an easy way to relax and burn some time.
