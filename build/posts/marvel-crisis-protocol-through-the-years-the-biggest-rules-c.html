Marvel Crisis Protocol Through the Years: The Biggest Rules Changes, Revisited

October 1st, 2026 via Goonhammer

Marvel Crisis Protocol hasn’t officially had a second edition, but for those who have been playing since the early days, it certainly feels as though enough has changed to call the current game “2.0.” No, we’ve never seen huge, monumental shifts drop in one go (though we’ve come close a few times), but Atomic Mass Games has delivered fairly consistent, impactful updates that, combined, have become more than the sum of their parts.

Even more impressive, these changes have been almost universally praised as positive moves for the games. For those who have been around a while, revisiting the way things used to be might feel a bit nostalgic. Newer players might be surprised by how different parts of MCP used to be. Regardless, join me as we walk through a vaguely chronological retelling of how things used to be, discuss their overall impact on the game, and talk about why the change worked (or didn’t) to make MCP a better overall experience. Note: This doesn’t include every change that’s happened, but it covers the major ones.

Early Tweaks to Roster Construction

The first rules change for MCP was small but had meaningful implications. Originally, players would include ten characters (and six Crises) but only eight Team Tactics Cards. This added consistency was simply easier to remember, but it also allowed for slightly more variety as more cards were released for the game. In hindsight, the change totally makes sense. Will Shick has mentioned that, originally, the development team didn’t expect Team Tactics Cards to be such a competitively impactful part of the game. That quickly proved wrong, and ten remains a good-feeling sweet spot for including commonly played staples and situational tech cards.

The Banned and Restricted List

The MCP banned and restricted list has sort of ebbed and flowed in importance over the years, but at the beginning, it didn’t exist at all. As one might expect, this led to a handful of generic (not locked to a specific Affiliation or character) cards dominating competitive play and pushing many other options out. To open up roster diversity and let Affiliations showcase more of their unique cards, AMG introduced Restricted cards in late 2020. Along with it, they also outright banned one TTC that was proving too powerful and prolific.

The very first B&R List was as follows:

Restricted:
  • Brace for Impact
  • Doomed Prophecy
  • Field Dressing
  • Medpack
  • Patch Up
Banned:
  • Drop Off

None of these are a huge shock. Doomed Prophecy wasn’t originally locked to Asgard, and that later change moved it off the Restricted List. Field Dressing and Medpack have since rotated out of Standard play, and Brace and Patch Up remain Restricted.

The banned list has stagnated in recent years because generic cards are much fewer and farther between, and it's largely a non-factor, replaced instead by larger-scale rotations. Cards aren’t banned anymore; they’re simply “rotated out” of the Standard format, which is functionally the same thing.

In hindsight, was introducing a banned and restricted list a good thing? Absolutely, especially paired with another widespread TTC update that we’ll get to later. It did indeed open up rosters to consider a wider variety of cards and stopped games from being dominated by the same 3-4 TTCs every time.

Crisis Selection Revamp 1.0

AMG changed Crisis selection in 2025, but they also changed it much earlier in the game’s life cycle. Originally, players included three Secures and three Extract Crises in their list. Then, after the priority roll, the players randomly discarded one of their own three and manually selected one of the two remaining. This gave players significantly more agency in what they played and functionally meant that you only really had two of each Crisis type in your list if you wanted - players could guarantee that one is never played (either discarded or not selected from the remaining two). Moreover, the priority player also chose the threat level. This gave the priority player plenty of control to “trap” the other player on a Crisis and threat level that best suits them or penalizes their opponent.

The first change shifted this so that Crises would be randomly selected from the full set of three, and the player without priority got to choose the threat. Was this a good change? Absolutely. While the system was later iterated on again, this made it much harder to lock games into the most divisive Crises or threat values (there used to be 14 threat Crises!).

The First Character Update Pass

There are, were, and always will be characters a little above or below average in MCP, but in the early days, it was unclear whether AMG would ever act to rebalance them. The answer came in 2021 when the first batch of updates hit. This pass touched 24 characters, bringing a few up and toning a few others down. The biggest changes include a completely redesigned Hulk, a brand new leadership for Star-Lord, and a threat drop for Bullseye (still the only time a model has changed threat in MCP).

At the time, these changes were billed as a one-time thing to bring earlier designs up to modern standards after the designers had found their footing with the game in players’ hands. That’s obviously proven untrue, and while updates haven’t landed with consistent regularity, the game has seen several subsequent passes, with far more hits than misses.

Infinity Gems Get a Makeover

Infinity Gems remain a powerful and popular element of MCP, but they looked quite different when they were first released. First, they didn’t all “cost” a flat one Threat. The Space Gem had the same effect it does now, but it increased its bearer's threat by 2. The Time Gem got an actual redesign. In its original form, the Time Gem granted an entire additional action! This incredible effect previously cost 2 threat, which still seemed like a bargain for such a wildly impactful bonus. Moreover, Infinity Gems didn’t used to be locked to a character in Roster construction. Instead, they took Roster slots like characters do and could be modularly assigned during Squad building.

Was the change good? Absolutely. I have some qualms about the way locking gems to characters punishes a few bearers, particularly in Black Order, but the homogenization of Threat cost makes sense, and the current Time Gem effect, while perhaps underwhelming, is better than

The First Crisis Set Update & A New Precedent

In the early years of MCP, players could only officially acquire new Crises by purchasing character packs that included them. This was understandably unpopular, as few wanted to purchase models they didn’t want for the Crises their lists needed. AMG eventually moved away from this right around the time of the first Crisis update in 2022. Instead, Crises would be available for purchase but also provided via free online PDFs.

Here, players got one new Crisis (Paranoia Pummels Populace), but it also completely removed single Extracts from the game, replacing their effect with a new one for Crises like Senators and Skrulls. It also brought minor tweaks to Crises like Hammers and Legacy Virus and more substantial ones to SWORD Base. Moreover, it was the death of 14 Threat.

While this wasn’t officially a “rotation,” it set the stage for AMG’s willingness to change Crises for balance or freshness. This didn’t happen again for several years, until the War of Kings rotation in 2025 ushered in a new era of yearly rotation, which continued with Operation: Zero Tolerance in 2026.

Though some lament the decision to remove or not remove certain Crises over the various scenario refreshes, it’s hard to argue against the general concept of rotation (and the removal of Crises from character packs).

In November 2025, AMG dropped several core rules updates at once. The first flattened collision damage. For most of the game’s life, collisions dealt 1+ the size of the terrain or character being thrown. To tone this down, all collisions moved to a flat 3 to be dodged. Did this create some awkwardness in immersion when a small trash can deals as much damage as an entire building being thrown? Sure, but it killed two birds with one stone - it made Brace for Impact much less necessary in every list, and it stopped massive early damage spikes without much counterplay outside said TTC. The downside? Some characters who relied on throw damage felt pretty pillow-fisted. A few have been updated since, but not many. Of all the core rules changes, this remains one of the most divisive.

This update also introduced the second iteration of how Crises are added to Rosters and selected for play. Instead of three Secures and 3 Extracts, the number jumped to five. Instead of randomly drawing, players draw two of their five, and their opponent picks one of those two. The result? You can no longer be forced to play on the one Crisis you like the least. The downside? You’re likely to play the Crisis you like least, and you’ll rarely play your favorite. Like the above, this has proved controversial, and the system may be further iterated in the future. Personally, I’ll take what we have over an older system.

Finally, priority changed. The initial priority roll works the same (though the priority player now chooses threat), but after deployment, a second priority roll determines who takes the first activation. Not knowing who goes first during Squad selection and deployment has gone a long way toward preventing scripted Round 1 plays, though many have been removed from the game anyway. It’s also had a hard-to-measure effect on models who feel best with or without priority. Kraven, for example, thrives when he knows he can go first, so he’s a bit tougher to justify in this new world. Overall, this change has been well received, but it’s hard to judge its effect separately from the gameplay elements that have changed around it.
