Historicals: Victrix Early Imperial Roman Range Review

October 1st, 2026 via Goonhammer
With the recent release of SAGA: Age of Caesars, there are now more options than ever to field various kinds of Roman units. With that in mind, we thought it would be a good time to take a look at the entire range of Imperial Roman miniatures available from Victrix and how to make those kits work for building forces for SAGA.

As mentioned in our recent All the Romans of SAGA article, Studio Tomahawk has finally filled what had previously been a glaring gap in the timeline of warfare in the Mediterranean, Europe, and the Near East. Now, from antiquity all the way up to the fall of Byzantium during the Crusades (and arguably longer if you count the Holy Roman Empire), you can play Romans of some stripe or other in every age the game currently covers.

But usually when people think "Roman," the immediate image that comes to mind is serried ranks of legionaries marching off to do battle with various groups of barbarians, expanding the borders of the Empire and bringing the Pax Romana to the world. That is, when the Empire was at its height, an era that ranges from 52 BCE - when Julius Caesar first led his legions across the Rubicon in defiance of the orders of the Roman Senate - to roughly 300 CE, by which time the Empire had largely split in two and began to be plagued with troubles both internal and external. SAGA: Age of Caesars covers exactly this peak era of the Empire.

Ready and waiting with an impressive range of figures, miniatures manufacturer Victrix Limited offers a number of kits that can have you up and running with a warband for this iconic era right away!

Disclaimer: While Victrix Miniatures often generously provides miniature kits to Tabeltop Battles staff for review, they were not involved with this article. With the exception of the Early Imperial Roman Generals (a spare sprue of which fellow TTB contributor Triphos kindly sent me gratis in the mail), I paid for all of these minis with my own money, largely because I was already in the middle of a massive, ongoing project that dealt with this specific era.

Romans in SAGA

Of the eight factions represented in SAGA: Age of Caesars, three of them (the Caesars' Legions, Imperial Rome, and the Palmyran Empire) can take Legionaries as a core troop type. The Rebels Against Rome faction can take Legionaries as a ruse, while their warriors may be filled with Auxilia. Dacians can take the Manuballista both as a unit and as a ruse, and as mercenary units both the Belgae and the Noble Cavalry could easily be covered by Auxilia and Roman Cavalry figures respectively. As such, unless you're going heavy into camels and cataphracts (looking at you, Bair...), you'll likely be wanting to put at least one unit of "Roman" troops on the table.

Victrix Imperial Romans

In terms of building a force for SAGA, some of the kits are almost "must have" sets, while others are going to have more utility once you are looking to branch out or add some variety to your warband. Let's look at each in more detail.

Early Imperial Roman Legionaries Advancing

These are the troops that come to mind when most people think "Roman Legions," and the kit captures this iconic look in wonderful detail. The kit consists of five infantry sprues, each with four figures. These come as one piece for legs-and-torso, with separate heads (two varieties), shield arms, and weapon arms. There are enough options on each sprue to outfit every soldier with either a leveled pilum (javelin), a shouldered pilum, a pilum used like a walking stick, or a gladius (a short, broad-bladed sword). All of the figures are in various walking/striding poses, but the variations in arm attachment planes and the ability to model a fair amount of turn to the heads can give you impressive variety even for troops who are in very similar poses.

The three options for how the soldiers are holding their pila. There are enough of each option to outfit the entire kit with any one of these, or you can mix and match for variety. (credit: Ilor)

The infantry sprues also include gladii in scabbards. These are a separate piece, so are somewhat small and fiddly. I get why they did this, though, as it gives you the ability to place them on the figure at whatever angle best fits with the arm you happen to be using (see picture above for examples). Finally, each sprue has both square and oval shields, as well as four crests in case you want to outfit your legionaries as Praetorian Guard.

Each legionary soldier is kitted out in lorica segmentata armor, a series of overlapping plates that offered exellent protection against the weapons of the time. This is reflected in the rules, as in SAGA terms legionaries sit between Warriors and Hearthguard, having an Agression of 1, but an Armor of 5. Carrying this hybrid approach even further, they are fielded in units of six for a single point (as opposed to eight or four if they were Warriors or Hearthguard respectively). As such, this kit lets you comfortably build three full points of Legionary troops. That's half of a complete warband from a single kit, which is great!

All twenty legionaries from the Victrix Early Imperial Roman Legionaries Advancing kit completed and ready for battle! Note the mix of swords and javelins, which I did mostly for the sake of variety. (credit: Ilor)

In addition to the legionary sprues, there's a single command sprue with five figures. These are designed to be a Centurion (commander), an Optio (sub-commander), Cornicen (musician), Signifer (unit standard bearer), and Aquilifer (legion standard bearer). If you're playing the Imperial Rome battle board, you're going to want the Centurion at a minimum, as having an extra Hero on the table is key to how that battle board operates. Shockingly, Age of Caesars doesn't include rules for Banners (common to many of the other ages), and aside from the "Wandering Bard" mercenary type in Age of Vikings, has no rules for musicians at all. As such, your other command figures may not see much use in SAGA. That said, with all of the extra heads and arms you certainly could kit them out as regular legionaries if you weren't in need of command figures and didn't mind slight differences in the torso armor. That would allow you to put four full points of Legionaries and a Centurion on the table. 4 1/2 points out of a single kit is a pretty good deal.

From left to right, the Cornicen, Aquilifer, Centurion, Signifer, and Optio. Even though SAGA: Age of Caesars doesn't give you anything special for a standard, multi-basing the Centurion makes him look suitably impressive and clearly marks him out as a Hero on the tabletop. (credit: Ilor)

Early Imperial Roman Legionaries Attacking

If the previous kit seems too staid or static for you, fear not - this kit gives you the same kind of legionaries, only with much more dynamic poses. They likely won't "rank up" as well in small spaces, but for SAGA that's less of a concern than it is in more traditional "rank-and-flank" ancients rules. As such, these are similarly perfect for SAGA.

Once again the kit consists of five infantry sprues, each with four figures, as well as a single command sprue. The command sprue for the attacking Legionaries kit is exactly the same as it is for the advancing Legionaries described above.

The same general method of legs-and-torso with separate heads and arms is carried through here as well, but this time the stances are much more active and dynamic. There's also one notable exception, which is one figure with the legs and torso in two pieces and the cingulum (the little studded leather dangly bits at the front of the belt that were a mark of membership in the legions) as a separate, tiny, extremely fiddly piece. Not sure why they went this particular way, but assemble with care.

Legs-and-torso along with a separate head (left) is a sensible design choice for multi-part miniatures. By contrast, whatever is happening on the right is violence. (credit: Ilor)

The weapon options are similar as well, but this time with a mix of pila and gladii in various stages of throwing/thrusting/striking. Critically, this set completely lacks the optional helmet crests, so if you want to kit out some attacking Praetorians you'll need to steal the crests from the Advancing Legionaries kit.

These miniatures are a little different from the previous kit in that each figure is wearing short trousers underneath their tunics. Once everything is painted and everybody is grouped up on the table I don't think it's overly noticeable, but it's worth mentioning in case you are a stickler for uniformity.

A comparison of the advancing Roman legionaries (left) versus the attacking Roman legionaries (right). I think both look great on the tabletop, and I plan to mix and match them across units, or maybe put the attacking ones in the front rank and the advancing ones in the rear rank. (credit: Ilor)

Early Imperial Roman Auxiliary Infantry

Serving in the Roman military was a path to citizenship in the Empire, and as the Empire expanded it found itself in constant need of soldiers. Those soldiers were formed into units of auxilia, which were often used as light infantry or skirmishers. Trained to fight in the roman style, these troops came from far-flung regions and could reasonably be from pretty much any of the conquered territories.

For SAGA: Age of Caesars, Auxilia are treated as Warriors, fielded in units of 8 men for a single point. The kit consists of seven infantry sprues, each of three men. Regular spears (as opposed to the legionaries' more iconic pila) and gladii are your weapon options, but there are a few flaming brands and trophy heads held aloft are included. That said, the tunic sleeves of the legionaries and auxilia are the same, so you could absolutely raid any legionary sprues you have for additional pila if you wanted to give some to your auxilia. Oval shields are your only option here, but if you're feeling particular savage each infantry sprue comes with sufficient heads and skins to clad each of your auxilia warriors in wolf pelts, as Germanic auxilia are famously depicted on Trajan's column. Scholars debate whether this was an actual military practice or a stylistic sculptural choice, but either way it looks pretty bad-ass.

Much more lightly armored than citizen legionaries, the Roman Auxilia were recruited from throughout the Empire. (credit: Ilor)

Like the legionary kits above, there is a separate command sprue, this time with an additional three figures. There are options for Centurion, Optio, Signifer, and Cornicen, but you'll obviously only be able to build three of them unless you want to dip into your basic soldiers. And like the Legionary kits, you can absolutely just outfit the command figures as regular troops, albeit with better armor. Doing so does let you get a full three points' worth of Warriors out of the kit, though.

Going heavy on auxilia is definitely a viable option, and only auxilia can benefit from the "Gradu Addere" advanced SAGA ability on the Imperial Rome battle board. This ability lets auxilia units perform a Maneuver within L of an enemy, which can be key for early game positioning. Even better, it can be used to allow auxilia units to move farther and (if infantry) ignore the penalties for uneven ground. Combined, this makes auxilia forces extremely speedy. Using "Gradu Addere" to get onto an objective early and then "Circle Up!" to stay there can be an unexpected surprise.

Early Imperial Roman Auxiliary Archers

In the Roman military system, missile troops were also often provided by auxilia units. This kit provides these types of troops for your warband, with 24 figures in the kit. Unlike the archer kits from later ages, this kit is specifically only archers with no option to assemble them as slingers.

Units with missile weapons are typically classed as Levies in SAGA, so having Warriors with bows is an interesting option that can provide for additional resilience and flexibility. (credit: Ilor)

Uniquely, this kit has body and head options for both western and eastern Empire archers, with each of the six sprues having two of each. As such, while the kit as a whole includes 24 archers, if you plan to stick with just one part of the Empire you only get 12.

For me, this 2-and-2 layout is a rare miss from Victrix - I feel like this would probably have been better off as two different sprues/kits. Having both mixed on the same sprue means even if you only want a couple of men (say to round out a second unit of Warriors with missile weapons), you'll need to buy twice as many sprues as you want.

Fortunately, I knew that Bair was planning on building a Palmyran force and could make use of the eastern half of the figures - after I built my half, I sent him the rest of the sprues. Now we both have archers, but it was pure luck that that worked out for us.

This is a little bit of a problem, because you are absolutely going to want at least one 8-man unit of these guys. That's right, in the Imperial Rome faction, Auxilia Archers are classed as Warriors, with an Aggression of 1 in melee and 1/2 in shooting. Without shields they are only Armor 3 and cannot close ranks, but someone charging into the unit and expecting to manhandle them in melee as though they were Levies will be in for an unwelcome surprise. Combined with "Gradu Addere," quickly getting a unit of Auxilia Archers into a terrain piece that provides them with cover is going to make them a pesky inconvenience to your opponent. The Caesars' Legions battle board has less to offer these troops specifically, but the activation efficiency of the "Trained Together" advanced SAGA ability will let you engage in good combined arms play with your archers and your legionary units. Just be aware that for the Caesars' Legions these guys are going to be classed as Levies, so you're going to want 12 of them.

Early Imperial Roman Cavalry

For the Imperial Rome faction, the final option for auxilia is as mounted Warriors, and with 12 figures in the bag this kit lets you field up to 1.5 points of them. The kit contains three rider sprues of four figures each (with a variety of head and weapon options) and three horse sprues of four figures each. While most are clad in chainmail, one of the figures on each rider sprue is wearing scale mail.

It sometimes boggles my mind when I think about it, but when these dudes were riding into battle the stirrup wouldn't be invented for another 800 years. Fighting from horseback seems like it must have been so much harder without stable footing. (credit: Ilor)In this case there is no separate command sprue, and each rider sprue has a couple of standards should you want to equip your troops with them. Again, SAGA: Age of Caesars doesn't allow banners, but sometimes looking cool is what you've got to do.

Fellow TTB Historicals contributors Bair and Mugginns always give me crap about my goofy basing scheme for horses, but it lets me use the same sabot bases for both infantry and cavalry. (credit: Ilor)

My love of painting horses is well-established, and these guys were a lot of fun to build and paint. I can't wait to field them in a game. Clever deployment, a mounted Warlord, and timely use of both "Gradu Addere" and "Rest Halt" could allow you to pull off a rare Turn 1 charge with no fatigue - surprise! These figures would also work perfectly as non-cataphract mounted Warriors for several of the other factions, or even as "Noble Cavalry" mercenaries.

Early Imperial Roman Mounted Generals

The kit gives you a bunch of different options, but I chose to do a mounted version of the Centurion from the Legionaries kit I had already finished. I think the next one will be an older, patrician-looking dude suitable for one of the command options in the Caesar's Legions warband. (credit: Ilor)

I'm going to be honest, this kit mystifies me a little bit. Like the cavalry this kit includes 12 figures, but it's four copies of the same sprue (each of which has three horses and three riders). But am I ever going to need 12 generals? The only thing I can think of is that you'd build maybe one or two generals from this kit, then raid the Early Imperial Roman Cavalry kit described above for extra heads or spears, giving you the ability to field more cavalry units. But apart from that, having a full dozen of these particular figures seems like overkill. If you have access to a "buy-by-the-sprue" service (or have some friends you can go in with together), I would probably recommend that.

That said, these figures are great. Especially if you are playing the Caesars' Legions battle board, all three of your Warlord options (Consul, Tribune, and Legate) have the option to be fielded mounted. This kit is perfect for those roles, and with a single sprue you could do a unique option for all three.

Early Imperial Roman Bolt-Shooters

When you absolutely, positively need to drive a spear through someone's chest at long range, Roman artillery perfected the process with the ballista. Harnessing the power of tension and torque, these fearsome weapons provided long range firepower capable of damaging even heavily armored targets.

This set lets you bring that terrifying artillery engine to the tabletop. The kit consists of four identical sprues, each with enough parts to build the ballista itself (with options for both uncocked and ready-to-fire configurations) and four legionary crew members. Like the attacking legionary kit, there is one guy on the sprue with the separate torso - and the separate cingulum - again. Brutal.

In terms of arm/weapon options, there are a couple of swords and pila, but also a number of axes and splitting mauls, as well as a couple of buckets. Better yet, there are arms that can be hooked to the windlass handle to model your ballista in the process of being wound/loaded.

Tension-driven weapons like the ballista deliver a terrifying amount of force. The rate of fire isn't high, but the range and potential impact they offer convinced many a Roman general that they were worth lugging into the field. (credit: Ilor)

SAGA: Age of Caesars treats these as the "Manuballista," a unique weapon whose Aggression score in shooting is based on the number of figures in the target unit. As a result the size of the weapon's crew doesn't matter, as it tracks its current "health" through Fatigue (the unit is considered a single model which has the "Resilience" rule), so a single sprue gets you a complete unit. Putting one into your warband costs just a half-point. Sadly, in this Age you can only ever field a single Manuballista (although as a ruse the Dacians can field a second one). As such, like the mounted generals you might want to split this kit with a friend.

Each sprue also contains a couple of terrain items like a barrel and a bunch of defensive stakes. These could absolutely be used to make some nice barricades, such as you might need if you use the "Tribuli" ruse from the Caesars' Legions warband.

Imperial Rome Warband

This is a recent release from Victrix and seems to be a direct response to the release of SAGA: Age of Caesars. Like the other warband kits released to support Victrix' own Pillage wargame, this set combines sprues from other kits. In this case, the warband includes four Advancing Legionary sprues, four Attacking Legionary sprues, one Legionary Command sprue, and a single Bolt Shooter sprue, a total of 41 figures and a ballista for $69. That's a hell of a bargain!

If you treat one of the command figures as your leader and another as a Centurion, that's a complete 6-point warband with:

  • Warlord
  • Centurion (0.5 points)
  • Manuballista (0.5 points)
  • 5x 6-man Legionary units (5 points)

What it lacks in variety it's going to more than make up for in brutal order efficiency and damage-soaking resilience. And the fact that it's a complete, legal, 6-point force in a single bag is pretty amazing. Fellow TTB contributor Hydriatus wrote a really nice review and "How to Paint Everything" article for this kit last week (check it out!), and it serves as a great entry point for someone wanting to get into the period.

Decisions, Decisions...

If you are looking to jump into SAGA: Age of Caesars - and you should, it's awesome! - having some legionaries or auxiliaries on hand is almost certainly worth doing. Though you'll get the most use out of them with the Caesars' Legions and Imperial Rome battle boards, they can serve as a nice way to branch out into other boards like the Palmyrans or Rebels Against Rome. Some of the kits are more niche (bolt throwers and generals in particular), but give you additional flexibility when building your list. Getting individual sprues for these would be ideal, but if your gaming group is getting into the game together then chances are good you'll be splitting them up among your buddies anyway.

Further, while by the end of the 4th century the Roman Legions and largely exchanged the large, rectangular scutum shield for a smaller, lighter oval design and moved from the short-bladed gladius to the longer spatha, you could easily put together a warband using these figures along with the Romans board from SAGA: Age of Invasions and everything would be immediately recognizable (including the manuballista, which is still an option in the later age).

If you have any interest whatsoever in wargaming during the age of antiquity - whether SAGA or some other game system - chances are good you're going to need Roman legions at some point. Now you'll have a better idea of your options!
