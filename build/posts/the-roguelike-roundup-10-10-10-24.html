The Roguelike Roundup, 10/10 - 10/24

October 24th, 2026 via Goonhammer

In the Roguelike Roundup, Jake sifts through the deluge of roguelikes and autobattlers that come out every week (or slightly longer) to shout out the ones he thinks are worth your time.

Dicevaders

Dicevaders is a charming Balatro-like (you try to beat a set score through the use of multipliers and various scoring tricks). You play as aliens invading the solar system, with each planet acting as a level to clear. This framing gives them the ability to design characters with interesting aesthetics and mechanics, and they take full advantage of that fact. If you clear a level in one go, you’re even rewarded with a cute animation unique to the character (my favorite is the blob girl who puts the planet in a jelly cube).

The game begins simply: roll 3 dice, put units in numbered lanes that score when you roll that number. Pairs and triples are good. While initially I was skeptical of the depth I was very quickly proven wrong. The artifacts you can equip offer a wide range of game-changing effects and also can level up through gameplay. In fact, there are entire archetypes revolving around generating experience for your artifacts.

Instead of standard ascension-style difficulties the game is split into chapters that have new bosses. I found this really refreshing, as playing the same content but it’s just a bit harder is dull in my opinion.

After each turn, you visit a shop which allows you to purchase generic upgrades like budges which move a die one up or down, chrono which rerolls dice, extra tiles, experience for your relics, and units. The money you don't spend doesn't stay with you and instead turns into bonus dice for the next round. Additionally, finishing a round early gives you the ability to level up the shop. This is a brilliant system which gives the player total freedom to farm units, or rush for the rarer stuff by winstreaking.

Progression in a roguelike is a tricky thing. Too much too fast and the player exhausts all the options. Too slow and the player gets bored. Dicevaders nails it, giving you a new character or artifacts after basically every run. In addition, each character unlocks specific perks that make their gameplay more interesting, so there’s lots of incentive to play each character over and over.

The score thresholds are tuned perfectly. Somehow, you go from needing 100 points round one to 150 million in the final round and it never feels like too much or too little to ask of you.

In addition, after you ‘complete’ the game, you unlock a new mode filled with unique modifiers, artifacts, and upgrades. There is so much content here and I keep coming back for more. I set aside four hours to give as thorough a review as I could and now I’m nearing nineteen hours in the game.

There's also a plethora of quality-of-life features like the ability to rewind a turn, an extensive strategy guide that unlocks more the deeper you go and speed up features. The visual scoring effects are satisfying in the same vein as the addicting Balatro cha-ching and the whole game is made in a cute retro theme like Scott Pilgrim meets Space Invaders.

This game is a complete experience made by a developer who absolutely knows what they’re doing, and it gets a strong endorsement from me.

Tower Tactics 2

I had a lovely time with Tower Tactics: Liberation so I was thrilled to see this pop up in early access.

Tower Tactics’ special sauce comes from the combination of deckbuilding and tower defense. Placing towers isn’t as simple as other tower defense games where you have a constant set of options to choose from. Here your towers are cards in the deck, and every round you’ll draw a hand of them. In typical autobattler fashion, placing three of a tower will fuse into a higher-level tower. Three of those upgrade to the next level, and so on. In lieu of towers costing money, they cost mana. Holding off on placing towers in early rounds allows you to build up your mana pool to afford bigger and better towers.

There are several game modes that return from the original game. You have your standard round-based tower defense, as well as a timer-based one where enemies constantly spawn and you gain mana cards every twenty or so seconds. Additionally, there’s a mode where you get to craft your own map, building routes for enemies to travel down. This mode is the most interesting one, as the other two tend to generate maps with a single lane, leading to stale gameplay rounds.

The initial towers you have access to aren’t going to blow your socks off. You have your bomb launcher, your default rapid fire tower, your slow hitting tower that stuns, and a few that riff off those ideas. However, as you rapidly unlock the other characters and upgrades, things start getting interesting. Towers that supercharge on certain squares, ones that teleport around the map and ricochet shots everywhere, and spell generation keeps each run interesting. Unfortunately, the trinkets are, for the most part, generic stat upgrades. Getting 10% more attack speed is good, but it’s not necessarily exciting.

Being able to freely adjust tower placement between rounds sounds nice in theory, but in practice it means there are no consequences for misplacing and thus no tension in the decision. Coupled with the fact that your mana sink is not in upgrading towers, but simply placing more of them, and most missions will end the same way: as many towers as you can place directly in the center of the map.

The feedback the towers give you feels good. The damage numbers popping up scratch that itch in the back of your mind, and the game has a solid tutorial that brings you up to pace quickly. The game never leaves you guessing on any esoteric stats, and being able to upgrade base stats that affect you and all your towers is interesting.

The other positive is the developer, who updates their game constantly and is always taking player feedback into account. Since writing this review, the game has already been updated five times with adjustments and bug fixes.

There’s also an incremental game built in, the new crystal mine mode. Incrementals are not really my thing, and it doesn’t seem to loop back into the core gameplay, but it’s there if you want it.

Burn With Me

A more story-driven roguelike than I am usually drawn to, Burn With Me has a hand-drawn style that gives an Edgar-Allen-Poe-If-He-Was-A-Furry vibe and a thematically satisfying gameplay loop. Use the demons you have to summon more demons, chasing that infernal power into the Faustian bargain at the end of the line. Beat the score for the round by enough, and you’ll bank some points that can bail you out later on.

True to its source material, every action in this game costs something. Drawing a card removes a random one in your hand from the game. Certain cards will destroy themselves after being played, or prey upon their neighbors in the pentagram.

Unless they don’t. That’s the trick the game doesn’t tell you. There’s always a way around these prices. You don’t burn a card when you draw if you don’t have any in hand. You don’t siphon power if you play the siphon card first. This subtle game design kept me going as each chapter shows you a new damned character with their own literal personal demons to go up against and accompanying bespoke mechanics. The final chapter combines all of the mechanics and characters in a really challenging and satisfying conclusion, both mechanically and thematically.

The soundtrack is a mellow, jazzy track with lots of piano. It doesn’t overstate itself, but it gives rainy-day-at-the-cafe vibes that I really enjoy. There’s also a rotoscoped hand that has a shocking amount of personality while you play. It beckons you to pick up cards, gives thumbs up when you do well, and literally swats your cursor back to the gameplay if you annoy it with clicks.

While I prefer my demons more horrific than animalistic, the art style is charming, and the little specks of colored detail allow you an easy way to understand what the demons do in broad strokes. Usually, green ones will have some sort of payoff later, while pink ones will deliver big points for big costs.

Overall, this is a game well worth its small price tag and a great way to spend an afternoon or evening. The finale was legitimately challenging, and I had to restart several times to get a run that could get me over the finish line.
