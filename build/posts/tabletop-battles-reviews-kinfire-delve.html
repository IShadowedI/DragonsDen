Tabletop Battles Reviews: Kinfire Delve

August 19th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Today, we are taking a look at Kinfire Delve, a cooperative dungeon delving card game that was published by Incredible Dream Studios in 2023 and 2024. It's shares the studio's unique setting–the World of Kinfire–with two other games that are complete separate from Kinfire Delve aside from the world. However, if you're familiar with any of them, then you'll see familiar faces as you explore the various systems. Since this is the first (and so far only) Kinfire game I've played, I'm going to focus solely on it in this review.

I am always on the lookout for cooperative two-player games to play with my lovely wife and other assorted gaming friends. Kinfire Delve was one that I saw mentioned a few times online and was also highly recommended by a friend (thanks, Bryce!), so I decided to give it a shot. The art design was a large factor in my decision, as well. It's a refreshingly bright game with colorful characters and an art style I really vibed with. Seriously, just check out the box and card art throughout this article. The physical cards are also of exceptional quality cardstock and have gold foiling. It gave me premium boardgame material vibes at a surprisingly low price point.

Something else that was readily apparent after breaking into the game was just how easy it is to pick up and play. I'm fairly deep into the weeds of gaming, as you might guess given that I write for a website dedicated to tabletop games, but I've taught the game to people who run the whole spectrum of gaming knowledge. From people with boardgame libraries that would rival what you'd see at a major convention to more casual dabblers to people who are brand new to the boardgame or tabletop world, everyone has been able to quickly pick up the mechanics. More importantly, I've seen every player have some kind of "ah-ha!" moment while playing which is such a positive thing to experience as a player. This game feels like a great fit for the dedicated boardgame crowd, but also for families with differing game skill levels or a group of friends who can vibe with the fantasy theme but might not know the difference between a d8 and a d12.

Kinfire Delve supports anywhere from 1-4 players, provided you have picked up enough of the box sets. This is as good a time as any to go into how Kinfire Delve is packaged. There are three distinct boxes you can pick up for Kinfire Delve and each of them will contain the cards to play as two of the heroes (known as Seekers in the setting), all the cards for one of the big bad guys and their lair (referred to as a Well and the Well Master), and all the relevant tokens and dice you'll need to play. This means if you want to play with only one or two players, you can get by with a single box. If you want to play with more players or mix-and-match the Seekers, you can do so by grabbing two or three boxes. Considering these range around $20-$25 per box, that was an easy pill for me to swallow so I promptly snagged all three of them.

Releases

Vainglory's Grotto, the first Kinfire Delve box set. Credit: Jake Bennington

The first of the three releases for Kinfire Delve, Vainglory's Grotto, was released in 2023. In this box, you'll find Vainglory herself. There is a very strong otherworldly fae court theme to this Well Master which I always enjoy seeing. The first of the Seekers in this box is Khor (they/them), a sort of reborn soul that is housed in a mechanical body. They are accompanied by a cute little moth critter named Flickers that shows up on a bunch of their cards. Mechanically, Khor has methods of reducing incoming damage which is quite helpful given the relatively small shared health pool among the Seekers. The other Seeker is Asha (she/they), an elf assassin who is more effective when going after challenges that already have some progress on them. Asha has tricks including a few more ways to outright discard challenges than the other Seekers -- this comes in handy!

Scorn's Stockade, the second Kinfire Delve box set. Credit: Jake Bennington

The following release was Scorn's Stockade in early 2024. The titular Scorn is a vengeful jailor spirit who is also imprisoned at the bottom of his Well along with a host of other prisoners. His art features scales and he's wearing a blindfold like the iconic image of Justice, but he also has a host of floating eyes around him and a big ol' enormous eye in his chest which indicates how he's subverting the "justice is blind" ideal. One of the Seekers available is Naz (she/her), a talented orc strategist with a big sword. Her tactical acumen is represented by being able to shuffle around progress tokens on challenges currently in play. The second Seeker is a lucky human bard named Feyn (he/him) who has a mysterious past, upbeat outlook, and a sweet lute. As is typical of bards in these party-based games, Feyn excels at supporting his teammates by granting re-rolls and has cards that can enable card draw. Drawing cards is always good in these kinds of games and becomes even more appreciated when you find out how difficult it is to normally refill your hand.

Callous's Lab, the third Kinfire Delve box set. Credit: Jake Bennington

The final of the trilogy is Callous' Lab. Callous is an immortal being afflicted by a malady that is slowly killing him, leaving him to wrestle with his newfound mortality through alchemical experimentations that he's more than willing to perform on the Seekers who come to stop him. Bundled in this box are Roland and Valora. Roland (he/him) is a dwarven scholar who is adept at fire magic. He is uniquely skilled at boosting his fellow Seekers' attempts at challenges, both because he can help them draw cards and because every single card in his deck is functionally a wild card for boosts meaning he doesn't have to match colors. He can also hit incredibly hard. Valora (she/her) is a runaway human noble-turned-archer who, by leveraging her bow, is adept at applying progress to multiple challenges and sports a few tools that can allow her to bypass a typically required roll of the dice.

The game is designed so that any of the Seekers can be played against any of the Well Masters. Mix and match between the sets to your heart's content!

Overview

An example of a game in progress. Credit: Jake Bennington

During the game, each player will take control of one of the Seekers mentioned above. This is represented by a card with your character's special passive ability, a lantern card that's essentially an enhanced attack that needs to be charged up during gameplay, and a deck of 18 cards that are each an attack that you can play to attempt a challenge. These challenges are found in the Well deck of 57 cards which serves double duty in that it not only holds the various encounters your Seekers will have to overcome, but it also serves as an indicator for how far your party has managed to delve. I quite enjoy this mechanic because a common reward of completing challenges is to delve a number of cards. Delving cards just means taking the top X cards of the Well deck and placing them into a face down pile off to the side which gets you ever closer to the boss residing at the bottom of the Well. It allows for more variation in each game since you'll be seeing different encounters based on the luck of the draw while still progressing through the whole deck.

On the subject of variation between games, each of the Well Masters actually has three different versions. Part of setup has you shuffling the three cards together and choosing one at random which resides face-down in the middle of the table. Even after becoming familiar with the game, there's no telling which iteration of the Master you're going to be up against so players are kept on their toes. Four Well cards are also dealt face up to surround the Master; these are the first four options a player has on each turn to try to defeat. As the challenges are defeated or discarded, another will take its place from the top of the deck. This process repeats until the Well deck runs dry. At this point, the Seekers have reached the bottom of the Well and proceed to fight the Master! I'll speak more on the Well Master fights later because they shake things up just enough to give a climactic end to a game.

A sample hand for Asha and a sample Well setup for delving into Vainglory's Grotto. Credit: Jake Bennington

Taking a look at the cards themselves, you'll notice that both the Seeker cards and the Well cards are divided up into three colors – blue, green, and red. A nice touch is that they each also have distinct shapes (square for blue, diamond for green, triangle for red) shown underneath the big number value in the top left corner which will allow anyone with color blindness still be able to discern between the three. Some Seeker cards can have two colors on them, which means you can treat them as either of the two colors shown. There are also a limited number of white Seeker cards (including all of the potent lanterns) which are functionally wild cards that can be played as either blue, green, or red depending on your needs at the time.

Gameplay

A turn follows a pretty simple structure that can be broken down into five steps.

First, the active player selects the challenge they want to attempt. This would be one of the four face-up cards surrounding the Well Master. The goal in attempting a challenge is to meet or beat the difficulty number in the top left of the card. Challenges each have a reward and penalty, shown on the card as the symbols next to the trophy and skull icons respectively. The reward is granted if the challenge is completed and the penalty is applied if it isn't. Even if the challenge is failed, a Seeker is typically putting some progress tokens on it which will make it easier to accomplish in the next turn. Most challenges have unique rules on them which makes it even more difficult to choose what to prioritize. Some even have bolded text which are effects that are in play even if you're attempting a different challenge.

Asha attempts the King's Ransom trap with the Defeat Trap card. There's even a boost from her teammate, Khor, represented by the card tucked up under Defeat Trap. Credit: Jake Bennington

Second, the player can choose a skill card from their hand (or their lantern if it is charged) and place it out on the board to show the other players. This skill card has to match the color of the challenge being attempted. Skill cards have a value that serves as your starting point for meeting the difficulty of the challenge, but most of them also have card text that gives them a variety of special rules and modifiers. This can range from ignoring penalties from failing the challenge, charging your Seeker's lantern, or allowing you to discard the card before attempting a challenge to gain some kind of powerful effect. It's worth mentioning that playing a skill card is optional in case a player wants to save their precious cards in hand and believes they can get by without spending one.

If the player does play a skill card, the next step is allowing the other Seekers to play their own cards as boosts. Boosting is achieved by looking at the bottom of a skill card. You'll see a little strip on the very bottom that contains the relevant color(s) and a value. When boosting, this is the only relevant part of a card – in fact, the booster can just slip it underneath the active player's skill card to make it even more obvious. Boosting can be done up to two times per skill card used, but it can only be done by other Seekers. Boosting your own cards isn't allowed. You also cannot boost if the player decides to not play a skill card in the previous step.

Not a particularly great roll here -- only one die face matches the color of the challenge, so Asha only gains +1 to her attempt. Fortunately, that's all they needed! Credit: Jake Bennington

Rolling the dice is the following step. There are four six-sided dice included in each box of Kinfire Delve. Three of the dice have two red, two blue, and two green faces each while the fourth has three white and three black results. After players have decided whether or not to boost, the active Seeker rolls all four dice. If any results come up that match the color of the challenge being attempted, they each add one to the Seeker's total. If the white face shows on that last die, it also counts as a success since white is wild. That might make you think the black result is bad and you'd be sort of right and sort of wrong. By itself, rolling the black result doesn't do anything. However, there are a variety of cards that can apply penalties if that result is rolled.

This Hunting Cat has some progress already on it from a previous attempt, meaning the next Seeker only needs to get 4 more to complete it. Credit: Jake Bennington

Finally, check to see if the challenge has been completed. You add up the value of the skill played, the value of any boosts applied, and the number of correct dice results rolled to get the total amount of progress that can be placed on the challenge this turn. If that amount meets or beats the difficulty of the challenge, great! It is completed and the active player gets the reward represented by the little trophy icon. The most common reward is delving some number of cards, but there are also fancier effects like drawing cards, charging your lantern, healing, or removing exhaustion. If the progress placed on the challenge doesn't get past its difficulty, the challenge is failed and the penalty next to the skull icon is applied. Often, this is damage suffered by the party, but just like the rewards, there are other unpleasant surprises awaiting on some cards. Things like forcing you to discard cards, ascend back up levels (putting previously delved cards back into the deck), or even attaching to the Seeker to cause negative effects for the rest of the game. Progress applied will stick around, though!

The end of the turn has some basic clean-up effects. Players take the cards they played this turn, either as skills or boosts, and put them in their discard piles. If there are any vacant slots surrounding the Well Master, they get refilled by the top card(s) of the Well deck. After that, play passes to the next Seeker and it all starts from the top.

Turns are pretty snappy in the game, so even with four player games I've never had to sit around impatiently waiting to take my turn again. The boost mechanic also provides for some interaction opportunities on other players' turns, and the cooperative nature of the game has always lead to discussions about who is planning what attack and how terrified we all were of some particularly nasty penalty.

Fighting the Well Master

Vainglory is surrounded by her Gauntlet cards. Players can't begin progress on Vainglory until at least one of these gauntlets have been defeated. Normally, Vainglory would have flipped here but I didn't want to ruin the surprise for anyone's first encounters with her! Credit: Jake Bennington

If at any point you need to draw a card from the Well deck but there aren't any, you've reached the bottom and are ready to fight the Well Master. All the remaining challenges are removed, the face-down Master card is flipped over, and the four gauntlet cards set aside at the beginning of the game are laid out around the Master. These gauntlet cards are tricky challenges that bar the way from attacking the Master until they're dealt with. In fact, the Well Master card is always a black challenge and not one of the colors associated with cards. This means it's impossible to pick a matching skill from your hand to attempt them (since even the white skills are really just treated as red, blue, or green). Defeating the gauntlet cards here will flip them face-down instead of removing them, and the back sides of most of these have rules as well. In most cases, you will unlock a vulnerability to that color to go after the Master. For instance, defeating the red Reprisal card from Scorn will allow all the Seekers to start attacking Scorn with red skill cards.

The Well Master fight follows all the normal flow of the rest of the game, but adding the gauntlet cards and the unique effect of whichever form of the Master was randomly chosen makes it just different enough to feel the shift. It reminds me of the mentality of a video game where you hear the boss music kick in right before a tough fight. The penalties start to really ramp up which means that one way or another, the game is going to come to a close pretty shortly after reaching the bottom of the Well.

Perils of Delving

The Exhausted deck is full of nasty surprises for the party who also has to contend with a shared health pool that only goes up to 10. Credit: Jake Bennington

Eagle-eyed readers may have noted that at no point did I mention drawing new cards in a turn. That's because unlike many other games, you don't refill your hand at the beginning or end of your turn. Card draw is an effect available from a few sources, but it's quite rare. Instead, if a Seeker wants more cards, they have to engage with the exhaustion mechanic. At the start of a player's turn, they can discard the remaining cards in their hand to draw a new hand or if they have no cards in hand, they must draw a new hand. Each time this is done, one of the exhausted cards is flipped up, the player draws their hand of cards, and then takes their turn as normal. Each of the Well Masters have a unique deck of six exhausted cards and they're genuinely all terrible. They include effects like "each time you suffer a damage, suffer another damage" or "if you have three copies of this card in play, lose the game" or "if a player plays a certain card name, lose the game." There's a lot of "lose the game" involved there, so managing exhaustion is pretty important.

Taking damage has also been mentioned a few times and it's another route to losing the game. At the start of the game, the party has 10 health. Yes, it's the party as a whole. Health is a shared resource and there's only 10 of it. While healing and damage reduction effects do exist, they're not exactly plentiful and in my dozen or so games I have almost always felt like we were in danger of running out at some point.

At this point, I've won more games than I have lost, but I've still definitely lost my fair share. I enjoy that it's not a guarantee to win the game every time you sit down so there's always some tension present. It also feels like some of the Well Masters are easier (e.g. Vainglory has become my go-to for teaching the game) and I'm sure some of the Seekers are more potent than others. Synergy among the Seekers is also lurking, with potential combos like Valora spreading progress around multiple challenges which then enables Naz to shift it onto a primary target. This means there are plenty of dials for the players to adjust to get the game to their desired challenge level. The rulebook does also offer a "hard mode" but all that entails is starting at 8 health instead of 10, which I guess does achieve the desired result but it feels a little bland given how much character and pizazz the rest of the game offers.

Final Thoughts

I'm one of those people who has a board game shelf with a few dozen various games, some of which I am embarrassed to say I've owned for years and still haven't played. Kinfire Delve definitely isn't one of those games languishing on the shelf. My wife and I have played it many times, I have taken it with me to conventions and tournaments to play after rounds, and we have shared it with my wife's D&D group who took to it with ease and enthusiasm. It's portable, teaches and plays fast, is relatively affordable for what can be an expensive hobby, and is just a genuinely solid game that I have to give it my endorsement. If you think it sounds remotely interesting, I suggest you give it a try!
