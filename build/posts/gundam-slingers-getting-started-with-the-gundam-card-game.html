Gundam Slingers: Getting Started with the Gundam Card Game

August 5th, 2026 via Goonhammer

The world of trading card games is currently going through something of a boom period, with lots of new games and ideas vying for your hard earned attention and money; while Magic the Gathering and Pokémon still dominate much of the conversation, there are a lot of other games out there that have engaging mechanics, competitive play, and unique ways of interacting with the expectations of a card game. Quite a few of these games come from the same place lately: Bandai, a company that has access to numerous anime based IPs and the ability to leverage them into games, such as Digimon, One Piece, Dragon Ball Z, Gundam, and the newly announced Naruto card game. Some of these games are beholden to the “investor” market, namely One Piece, but almost all of them are quite alive and have a thriving competitive scene; personally, we’re pretty fond of Gundams here at Tabletop Battles, and my recent ability to interact with and learn the Gundam Card Game made it an easy fit to bring to our site. Today, I want to do something of a combination review and Getting Started piece, going over what makes the game tick, how it is unique and fun, why you might want to take a look at it, and then how to best start your journey into Gundam Card Game play!

A Newtype of Game

Depending on your familiarity with trading card games, the Gundam Card Game can be remarkably easy to pick up once the basics are explained, but even with total unfamiliarity with card games in general, the system at play here is fairly simple, making it quite beginner friendly and having the “low skill floor, high skill ceiling” type of gameplay that can make a card game fun to continue coming back to. In Gundam, each player has a deck of 50 cards, 10 Resources, and a starting Base card that comprises what they have to work with. The Resource mechanic is very similar to other Bandai card games, in that it is a separate pile of cards that allow you to pay for the things you’d like to do in the game; they also are somewhat similar to Lands in Magic, although Lands don’t exist outside of their main deck and the drawing (or not drawing) of them can become part of the fun (or frustration) of Magic’s playstyle.

The general layout of a Gundam Card Game match: Your base and shields to the left, the action area in the top middle, your resources and resource deck at the bottom, your deck top right, and trash to the bottom right.

Within a deck, players also have a few different card types: Mobile Suits, which make up the units you’ll use to attack and defend with, Pilots, who can be equipped to Mobile Suits and provide various benefits and abilities when they do so, Command cards, which generally feature immediate actions such as dealing direct damage, drawing cards, searching your deck, or more, and Base cards, which can be played to replace your starting Base and provide you with some extra defense. Decks have pretty basic construction rules, with a 50 card limit that can include 2 colors and 4 types of the same card (there are some more restrictions with the most recent Banned and Restricted changes, but that’s a little more granular than an initial overview can get into). Once you have a deck and have started playing, players place 6 cards from the tops of their decks next to their Base, creating a set of Shields. Again, these are very similar set up to games like One Piece, and functions very much the same way: when you take damage to your Shield, you flip the card over; if it has a Burst action, the card does what it says, and otherwise, it goes to your Trash. Once you’ve lost your Base and all 6 Shields, any damage dealt to “you” ends the game.

What we're all here for: Gundams. Mobile suits and some other vehicles make up the Units in GGC.

Gameflow is fairly quick and easy to get into: players draw 5, and then may Mulligan once. Play starts once a first player is determined, and players have “levels” that determine what cards they can play. Player 1 will always start at level 1, and level is decided by your Resources available; Player 2 is given an “EX Resource” card that, when used, is expended, but also functions as a level, meaning that going 2nd is not inherently a death sentence and that the extra level can help the second player stay on tempo. Level determines what cards you can play, but Resources then determine what you can pay for, which gave us nice flexibility during our turns during playtesting.

Pilots like Machu attach to Units, and can even allow them to act the same turn you play them if they're Linked.

Units are unable to attack upon being deployed (unless the card says otherwise, of course), so this can also help with turn order play and not feeling bad about going second; units also have choices of what they wish to attack, being able to choose between a player’s Base or Shield, or Rested units; in playing, we really enjoyed the choices available, and some cards even make it lucrative to trade Units in combat with various effects, rather than simply ignoring each other and seeing who can slam into the other’s Shields faster.  During our playtesting, going full out hard aggro against Shields also didn’t often pay off, instead resulting in leaving the over aggressive player often at a disadvantage, generally running out of steam quickly and having very little ability to recover.

Command cards such as Overflowing Affection allow you to draw cards, do direct damage, or other effects, and help break up the flow of the game.

That, perhaps, is what we found most enjoyable with the game: Tempo. Playing smart and playing in a timing focused method resulted in deeper gameplay and much more interesting matches, but it also tended to make our games fairly evenly matched. The current competitive meta does tend to focus heavily on aggression, but generally through decks that enable aggressive play and have cards that then allow that aggression to pay off. There are some various styles of play, too, often designated by keywords; for example, Gundam GQuuuuuuX tends to be tagged “Clan”, and feature numerous cards that search for, play off of, or work together with that keyword. This also extends to Pilots, who have the ability to “Link” with specific Units. Although Pilots can be attached to any Unit, when a Pilot is attached to a Unit that they are “Linked” to, they enable that Unit to activate immediately, as well as sometimes giving them specific powers or abilities.

If you're interested in trying the multiplayer variants, this starter deck is a good place to look for that, but can be a little odd otherwise.

Overall, games of Gundam lasted about 25~35 minutes in our play, and generally rewarded 2 out of 3 sessions to get a good sense of variability and feeling while playing. We also tended to have fairly equal and tied wins and losses, and the game generally feels very well balanced. We haven’t had much of a chance to try the other play variants, a 2-4 player free for all or 2 v 2 team battles, but one interesting aspect of this mode is that a set, Eternal Nexus, even features specific and unique cards that make those game modes more interesting and provide cards that work differently when there are multiple players at the table; in an upcoming article, we’ll take a look at these modes in more detail, as knowing what you can do with a game is always a helpful way to inform whether it is right for your table; currently, we appreciate that it exists, but have no hands-on experience yet!

How to get Started with Gundam

There are a few options available to get in to the game at the moment: buying Starter decks, or buying Singles. I’ve mentioned in previous articles that I’m not always the biggest fan of having to rely on Singles as the best way to get into a game, mostly just in that it can sometimes remove the sense of community or camaraderie from a game that you can’t “buy” easily, but like many card games right now, Gundam is going through a bit of growing pains with the “investment” market trying to make the game a new cash cow. The recent release of Freedom Ascension, the newest set, saw this as booster boxes and a Deck Building Kit sell out almost immediately and start to be resold at above average prices on the market. In general, though, Gundam is a very affordable game that benefits from the fact that “playable” cards are not the cards “collectors” want, and so to play the game, decks will often run around 40-60 dollars, with some being slightly more if they are the current top meta decks in the game.

There are a few more Starters coming in September, and some older Starters that were packed with Gundam Assemble kits, but right now you might be best off looking for some variety of these to get started with.

However, one good note here is that the starter decks available for the game all provide very balanced and fun ways to get into the game, and finding them tends to be fairly easy compared to buying the cards in bulk via booster boxes. Clan Unity, Iron Bloom, Flash of Radiance and Celestial Drive are the 4 current decks that you’ll often see floating around, and we were able to pick them up and test them out during our initial learning phase of the game. They all tend to focus on specific keywords, which does bring us to one vexing aspect of the game: If you’re looking to play a specific Gundam series, you’ll have to figure out what their keyword is, and then figuring out what card work best with those. For example, Clan tends to be so far attached only to GQuuuuuuX, meaning that Cland decks really only want GQuuuuuuX focused cards. But most competitive decks tend to focus on deck synergy over Gundam series, and there are some choices to be made about playing the game for fun and playing the game competitively; this is often the odd part of licensed games, so if you want to play as your favorite Gundam series, you’ll need to figure out the deck that correlates to them, or otherwise, enjoy playing within the various Gundam Universes by finding a deck you like to play instead.

Destiny Ignition gives you 22 extra cards to start customizing and deck building in the box, which is great for practice and learning.

There are two other Starter Deck options around: Destiny Ignition, which is an interesting deck that allows a player to take the cards provided and customize the deck with a provided amount of extra cards; I actually really enjoyed this product and would love to see more things like this in other card games, because it provides a pre-made deck that then immediately gives the player access to new ideas and tinker with the deck. The Deck Builder Box from Freedom Ascension works very similar, providing players with a set of different color options and then allowing them to build a deck from that; if you can find that box for a price close to MSRP, it can be the best box to start with. The last current starter deck, Generation Pulse, features cards from Eternal Nexus that makes the deck ready to be used for Multiplayer Variants, and while it does work for 1 on 1 play, it did feel slightly lopsided against decks that were specifically made to play 1v1.

Sadly this box can be difficult to find at the moment, but it contains a slew of reprinted staple cards from the original starters, and allows a player to build a custom deck from what's included. If you can find it for around MSRP, it is absolutely the best place to start.

Otherwise, your best bet is to look up various decks Bandai notes for competitive play wins, and try to build one off of those from singles. Ironically, this isn’t exactly that much more expensive than buying in with starter decks, as they tend to run 20 dollars for most basic decks, 40 for Destiny Ignition, and the Build Box tends to float around 60~80 dollars at fair market values. A single deck made of singles bought off the market will run around 40~80 dollars at max, especially if you avoid premium alternate art cards, so consider what you’d like to play and how you’d like to get into it; it may also be worth seeing if your LGS carries the game and has a community, as I was able to source some singles from there as well as trade with players and get some access to staples like extra Resources and commonly used common cards far below what I’d normally pay for it with taxes and shipping.

What’s Next?

Shout out to Calcifercurse, who made this amazing playmat! Not sponsored, we just loved the design and its what we use to play on now.

Hopefully, our primer has given you some encouragement to try out Gundam Card Game and see if it is for you; we think it is a great game and has a lot of playability and life, and since it just celebrated a year on the market in English, there is likely a lot more to come from Bandai as there are 4 new Starter Decks and booster sets on the horizon. The tie-ins with Gundam Assemble also hint at the fact that Gundam has a lot of room to grow from here; in our next feature, we’ll be taking the Starter Decks and discussing how to build off of them, their strengths and weaknesses, and how various deck synergies, strategies, and more can work for players looking to get started with the game. We hope you’ll enjoy it with us as we move towards more discussions of competitive deckbuilding and the world of the Gundam Card Game!
