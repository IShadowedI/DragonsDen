Games from the Crypt #26 - Bohn Again

November 11th, 2026 via Goonhammer

"It's about farming beans."

That was the pitch the vendor gave when I was looking at some imported card games at a convention back in 1997. Of course I furrowed my brow. Of course I looked at the dorky cartoon bean farmer on the cover and smirked. Of course I thought "how dumb is that." And of course I bought it.

Now, almost 30 years on, Uwe Rosenberg's Bohnanza remains an absolute crypt classic - a brilliantly designed card game with a particularly unusual hand management concept and intense player interaction by way of bean trading. Trust me, it is much more exciting than it sounds, and although the novelty of introducing it to a group and fielding the "WTF is this crap" reaction when you tell everyone they will be playing a game about harvesting legumes has worn off, the singularity and uniqueness of it have not.

Version 1.0.0

I recall reading the printed out English translation of the German rules - this was before Rio Grande Games had picked it up for US release - and having to explain and re-explain to my friends that once cards were dealt, you absolutely could not change or sort the order of the cards. This simple restriction broke many brains back in the day, but in order the game to work as it does it absolutely requires players to play the cards they have been given in the order they are given to them. This game is all about restrictions, working around them, and using limitations as a catalyst for tough decisions and trade incentives.

Once cards are dealt (and absolutely not rearranged), each player must play - or plant as the case may be -  the first card in their hand. This card will be one of many different bean types, which have varying rarity in the deck. The second card they may choose to play if they want. Then they flip over the top two cards of the deck and an open trading session begins, whereby players can trade cards from their hands with the active player for these face-up cards. Once trading is concluded, anything traded for must be planted immediately and the active player draws three cards. Again, not changing their order in any way.

You see, each player can only have two fields (i.e. rows or stacks), each containing a single type of bean. You can buy a third with gold if you so choose. The more beans that you have in a field, the more exponentially their value grows when it's time to harvest them and convert the cards into gold coins, shown on the back of the card. The rarer beans have higher values and need fewer cards to get into good money. So you might find yourself absolutely sweating over getting rid of a cheap and plentiful Weinbrandbohne through trade in order to keep your more lucrative Rote Bohne and Kakaobohne fields active and earning more money. You can also gift beans to clear out the trades, but it's much smarter to work out deals and fill your fields to max value. Because of course, the most gold at the end (once the deck has been run through twice) is the winner.

It's such a ingenious system that creates the impetus for high stakes trading as well as careful strategic planning. The only way you can manipulate your hand order and what you are going to be forced to play is by trading, and it is only through the trading that you can effectively get into the higher tiers of bean payouts. I love that it is such a silly theme, yet it's a game that engenders plenty of tension, bickering, debates, deals, and schadenfreude when someone is forced to plow up a valuable and growing field with lots of potential to plant garbage beans. There's a little bit of the classic card game Pit in its DNA, but it is much more structured and, well, Germanic.

Uwe Rosenberg would of course emerge as one of the premier game designers of the early 2000s with big, massive, and complex titles like Agricola and A Feast for Odin but I maintain that Bohnanza is still his finest design. It's a simple family card game released at the absolute peak of the golden age for German games It has a timeless that not even several frankly misguided and overwrought expansions that added pirates, a mini-wargame, mutants, a western theme, and too many bean-related puns have diminished. It is still in print, with a recent US edition published by Pandasaurus. It's same great game - mostly. The original artwork was so iconic that I can't imagine playing this game with "Jalapeno Joe" and not the Saubohne.
