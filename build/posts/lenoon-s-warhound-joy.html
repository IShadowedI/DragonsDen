Lenoon’s Warhound: Joy

November 25th, 2026 via Goonhammer

I should start with a confession. The whole point here was to journey together into process. To talk to artists, visionaries, geniuses, the creative and the profound (and ideally the infinite and the divine, not to mention the wicked and the warped), but then life took over and the Warhound built its own momentum, charging forward as the calendar ticked on and on. The discussions were the best bit, so when I could, I talked. Everyone I talked to - from passing chats to proper interviews - was interesting, fascinating people with valued perspectives and mastery in their systems. I learnt a lot about painting, creativity, and process - fulfilling, albeit internally, the central pitch of the series: What can we learn from outside the boundaries of miniature painting.

Here on the page, though, not so much. After talking to everyone the dialectic took over:

  • Thesis: There is so, so much to be learned.
  • Antithesis: I do not have time to learn everything.
  • Synthesis: Here is Joy.

Where I have ended up is perhaps inevitable.

Through intent, structure and finding, this has been a wonderful process, and I have found a great happiness in many stages of it. Contemplating a build not as a task or a hobby but as a compliment to life, a guide to it, a song in the background that swells and breaks (or drops the beat - pay your money and take your choice of genre) when you need it to, has felt so profoundly different to other, hurried, projects that I want to talk about where that joy has been, and to invite you to take nearly eleven months to not even finish a single model. Because, in short, it feels good. Very good.

Joy in the Concept

The concept of the series - and of the Warhound - still, just about, stands. I did learn, mostly about the process of creation from a psychological, philosophical standpoint which perhaps suits me better than technique anyway. The concept of the model though is a real joy. I am no sculptor or artist, I can’t - and never will - do the Jes Goodwin thing (who could? Only the man himself) where a model stands on the page absolutely as it will be in the flesh. I can’t do the Blanche thing (who could? No-one, now) of a drawing that inspires a model where the thread of genius is passed and transmuted between hands until it ends up on the table. What I can do is an idea that I can’t possibly execute, then work joyously within my limits and the materiality of the resin to arrive at an approximation of it. Ultimately, the learning from everyone has been that this process is relational, from start to finish. We engage with material and the world to bring it into ourselves, the object biography that was always there. So I create copy of a copy of a copy, in the way that Celtic coins, when you squint, really do look like a crap version of those of the Macedonian. It’s whisper telephone with myself, a process I have previously found immensely frustrating.

Here, in there, somewhere

Here though I have found joy in it. I have a dim and third hand memory of being mesmerised by a saying tossed out by my grandfather, only to find years later that it was one of the most famous lines of mystic poetry: Your own voice echoing off the walls of God, or, as Google Translate and certainly not my grandfather puts it: Dy lais dy hun yn atseinio o derfynau Duw. There’s no self-aggrandisement here, if my creative process is the walls of god it’s the poorly built, shoddily constructed walls of a crap god (Crap God! The New GrimDark Game from the Makers of….), but letting the concept bounce around as the resin dried in epoxy and sap and what was imagined became what was possible, then what was doable, then just what I was doing in the moment, was a mystical experience. It mutated and flowed, feathers turning from real into wood, then carved, painted and finally merely suggested with angled sponge on metal trim, the body reaching down, the head staring up. The transmutation from Carrion Crow, all sadness and omen, into scrap-hart, renewal and urbanised folkmyth. The failed experiments with bioplastics, and the successful experiments with cyanotype.

A skull changes much

I'd love to say "the most common question I'm asked about working with organic material is their longevity", but no one particularly asks. I've tried to use more organic material than before here - bone, lichen, bark, wool, cotton, wood, mycelium, dyes, shell - and it's come off looking like it usually does. Unusually, though, I've tried to plan for decay. It is already falling apart, the fabrics treated with rusting iron and caustic ashes and crumbling as they are applied, dragging themselves into whisps with the weight of glue and paint pulling fibres apart. The antlers were first mounted with copper rod, then replaced with wood and iron; the monster will shed them, one day, in a cacophonic thunder that shatters everything around it. Feathers, untreated, are fraying and curling. Already, it is different to how it was; once alive, now dying, never dead. Every day it looks different and new. What can we leave behind, what can we rot, what can we let go of? Our models are nigh on infinite, with lives that potentially stretch far, far beyond our own. They are metal and resin and plastic, and we are simply meat. Why not inflict upon them what they inflict upon us?

When I look back at the concept, so little of it remains in substance, but in feeling - those echoes from the walls - it is redolent of it, a beautiful little celebration of my strange, suspended little path. I feel as if it was always there, and all I had to do was chip away at the blessed crow, expel the Bendigiedifran of it all to find what it should have been, amalgam and wild, chaotic refusal of material to behave as it should, already colonised by arachnids and red spider mites (Christ knows what they’re feeding on in there) and wonderfully, solidly present in and of itself. The talking certainly helped that process. Flow, materiality, art, intention, the imagined conversations with mudlarks, the comments, questions, suggestions you’ve given me in various formats, all contributed to switching off the frustration of not being able to pull off exactly what you imagined, to realise that what you create is better - an answer to imagination. An echo. A detail in a wider picture.

Joy in the Detail

There is no cohesive whole here, so that wider picture is just a succession of detail upon detail. There is a broad - very broad - theme of organics, and my paint scheme more or less holds across the titan and the knights that preceded it. But this is a mélange, a mismatch. All detail, or, in another way, all filler no killer. While I enjoy a good unified scheme, I find myself drawn to detail on its own, for the sake of the hour or so spent on each patch. There’s a lot of what I think of as my usual detail here; multilayered rust, chequerboards, the spiraling vines that I’ve been doodling for 30 years in notebooks and essay margins, little birds (and little bits of big ones), leaves, lichen, candles - of course the candles.

Hands, lichen, scribbles

Each section where I’ve added, sculpted, cut, or painted detail is its own project in and of itself, making the Warhound a series of ratty canvases and sketches. I enjoy detail as it suits what I can do these days - half an hour, an hour, stolen time or earned time; the pomodoro method via childcare. As opposed to batch painting or hurrying to get an army done (my goths are languishing as I write this), detail like this completely occupies my focus. Outside of the couple of square inches that expands to occupy all but the most peripheral vision, nothing else exists. There’s both metaphorical and literal elements to that, as hearing drops away and the glasses come off, and as other projects fade into the background. Horizontal lines, vertical lines, fill with black ink, fill with ivory. Breathe, remember to breathe.

Never been a giant fan of oasis

Increasingly I turn to banners and birds, always birds now, the dream of flight filtered through that of Macsen Wledig, as I am torn free of my mid century anchor-ghosts and fall further and further into the past. Kelp takes paint well, inks take the t-shirt scraps, and over time I dress the Warhound in tatters dyed with avocado, nettle, Tumeric, cyanotyping liquid, oak gall and Indian ink.

Dream of Macsen the Bird

Eventually, life breaks through the reverie enough to call me away. An alarm goes off, a child wakes, a lunch break ends, or perhaps the meat calls through one of its many inevitable appetites. The spell breaks, and, hey presto! The detail is done. It’s an approach I’ve been trying to hold on to. Focusing in on an armour panel and deciding it’s done when the time finishes is deeply satisfying. When are models finished? When we say they are. Painting is potentially endless, there will always be something to improve or retouch or redo. But stop! Walk away! It belongs to the ages when your time runs out. Smile and put down the brush, re-enter life renewed. It works, every time.

I've been scribbling this for maybe 30 years

Imagine that process again and again and again. Perhaps a few times a day, or once a week, or, latterly, only when inspiration strikes and a neutrino scything through the matter of the world sparks a neuron in exactly the right way. What is important is the process, and the dance of it, the how of an endless range of potentialities being whittled down into concrete reality. What the detail is - what it looks like, the contribution to the whole, is unimportant. What matters is the movement of my hands across the model, the way that we grip the brush, pin, scalpel or glue, how the pieces are twisted, turned, and affixed. Hands skitter across the workbench for the very fine brush, the neat black, the off white. Too fast, too nervous, we cascade flock, or leaves, or static grass and snow across a blue/white cutting mat, ignored - time to create, but not to improve. It all stays as it is.

Joy in the Accidents

I love that creative impulse in Warhammer that tells us improvement is not necessarily our aim, or even a desirable end. We create! That is enough. It’s a childlike impulse, and I think we lose something in our adulthood with our search for improvement, especially, but not exclusively, with our models. It’s a sense of possibility, I suppose, the imaginative gap that fills better than sprue glue, that turns conversions, those that by any objective sense look like shite, into masterpieces. It’s something that lets everything be joyous. Perhaps you came into this place as an adult - in which case I can only commiserate, because there really is something unique about childhood conversions, no finishing or blending, no additional step: cut, stick.

A child, wanting a model to hold a bigger gun (to shoot better), doesn’t worry about balancing it, or keeping it in proportion, or faction technology, or anything at all, really. They get the bigger gun and they stick it on. Isn’t there a huge and almost forgotten joy in asking What If This Goes There? I remember (hazily, for reasons that will become clear), heating a kitchen knife over the hob until it glowed, so I could cutmelt Space Marine helmets and replace them with skulls - chaos marines straight out of the 2nd edition box set, and cutting all the horse out of Bretonnians to make the first of several dozen mounted wights. Both, of course, look terrible to the eye of adulthood, but I think it’s important to think of these first steps along the way as the start of a journey and the result of one. In and of themselves, they are wonderful, important.

First steps

The horse is something I’d only do without the knowledge of how to do it better. Why is the barding so thick around the eye? Why is it resting on the base? Why did I cut the crest off the helmet and replace it with what I think must be the hair from a tester model of Dark Eldar I got one Games Day long ago? Armed with the knowledge I have now, I wouldn’t do it that way at all - there are many routes to the effect I was after, but carving the living horse out of a box of Bretonnians isn’t one of them. In fact, put those models in my hands again and I’d end up keeping them on the sprue, those vintage ToW guys pay big money for old plastic. Then, I did it regardless, of course, and I played with that phantom Knight army for many, many years. It was impossibly joyous to play it against the conventional armies of my friends and not-so-friendly adults. That was joy, the intersection of teenage diversion and the last shreds of full on childish play.

Behold! The Brain-Breakers. Solar Auxilia, Credit: Lenoon

That’s a place I’ve lost - perhaps you have too - but the Warhound now preens itself in that space, straddling the line between the two on its impossibly weighty base, and gods bless it for that. There is immense satisfaction in all we do here, but it is work. We all find joy in different places - the writing, the painting, the playing, the building, the analysis - but even the most joyous bits sometimes just… aren’t. This is work. Work is hard.

I’d be surprised if it were universal, but I know that some of us - if not many - have at some point hit a burnout with writing, building and painting for review. I know I have, when the sixth Veletaris broke my brain to the extent I gave up Heresy, and however beautiful the models, however lovely the work, you can rapidly alienate yourself from this thing (hobby, site, practice, whatever) that we love. Accidents help with that. You can’t really make mistakes, try out entirely new things or just give it a fucking go when you’re writing for review. You do your painting to your established style, most often because that’s what you know you can pull off quickly. You might try out a technique from one of our HTPEs, but speed and clarity is (usually) the aim of the game.

Not so, of course, here. Instead, I made endless mistakes.

I overcooked the fox femur, making it impossibly brittle. The rawer ends stayed on the Warhound, but the rest was ground into powder and mixed into paint. The paint was scratchy and raw, both aggressive and scared, disobedient and cunning. There’s an animus that drew the warhound more mammalian, that replaced the beak with antlers.

I spilled oak gall ink over the carapace when filling up my fountain pen (which, incidentally, really hates the gall ink), and found it dried to a beautiful translucent sheen, deep in purple and brown tones, as if it were well-loved, ancient wood. Out came the linocutting tools to take best advantage of it, and it ended so beautifully - of all of it, I am most proud of this.

Oak Gall ink, I can't get enough of it

I built the whole torso before remembering I’d intended to fill it full of clockwork to meet and make sense of the treadmill wheel, so now the titan is not powered and moved by manpower, but adorned by servitors attending to a task even more Sisyphean than I’d intended. If we must imagine him happy, I can imagine these miserable, because someone has to be, and it isn’t me - not today.

cut, stick, until the work is done

I tried to carve out the resin of the shoulders smoothly, and royally fucked it, sending a hacksaw spinning into slivers, melting resin via friction of pin and blade, narrowly escaping losing a finger before slamming my hand down (accidentally) on an upturned scalpel blade that earned me 7 stitches. Dried blood makes a thankfully poor primer, so yes, I did wash it off - before anyone asks.  You can fit so much stuff in there, though - wires and gubbins and skulls and birds - and that was joyous, that was the child impulse.

The whole thing, the scaled immensity rocks on its base, a good centimeter of movement at the top. The pins are rigid and well sited, but they’re already working their way through the mdf base. It is precarious, held in place with a sliver of bone under a rear toe that I have not (and will not) paint to match. Measure (never), cut (twice), stick (until it’s full).

Joy in Chasing that High Ever Since

Of course the modelling is to the writing as the heartbeat is to breathing, so there was joy here too. My hobby is here, really, not out there on the board. The writing was, and is, and will be, exultant. That first time I stood twenty feet tall, buoyant on two thousand words lightly edited (knight army, never played). Sometimes my hands burn hot enough to turn iron to vapour sometimes they adhere in frost and for moving them the blood-wreathed bones crack and push through, but however I’m feeling, they can always hit a keyboard at around seventy words a minute. Whatever I’ve written in this process though, the best words came from someone else: make a knight about it.

That's become a mantra, to Make a knight about it. make a knight about it make a knight about it I love it I love it I would make a sticker of it make a t shirt make a banner make a website make a manifesto make a make a make identity about it my thing is that guy who does the weird things makes the toes makes you feel sick makes you feel (mainly tired, and bored, and you roll your eyes) and I love it it is enabling and freeing it is the guy I want to be out in woad and clay with ash dyed hair as an omen of visiting bacchanal but instead I writhe write burn on the bus the 38 the 103 the 4 the SL2 in shirt and jumper but never a tie as if that matters with work shoes on my feet wrapped in the trappings of the weather conscious (always sensibly dressed)

I would like to be the man you read irl, because I love him - and you, thank you for your time. I should get another tattoo.

I step forward with eyes closed, it’s not what I want to do and as fear for my future mounts the model becomes a certainty I can anchor myself around it wobbles on the base with brass rod i can’t remember and I hunt for baize on the streets with the boy I think I am falling apart or coming together I should be able to tell which is which

There is lichen on the floor on fallen bark white birch paper catches my eye and the boy shouts catkin catkin amongst the smoking wreckage the cans and broken glass there is life (comrade ant toils her days away) and there are bones for the pot: One million chickens a day I think, one million avian lives snuffed out to keep the streets coated in bones and the Thames choked with the votive offerings of the last remaining pillars of our nation, the chicken shop, the vape shop. That is the ground I walk on, that is the land my sons and daughter were born into, and this is the place the titan stalks, 28mm scaled with glossy shots in the national psychogeographic.

I am so conscious in this moment of this model as a gift given, and a gift gratefully received. Gifting is many things to many people, isn’t love, but this, this is being seen and understood, enabled, freed, Yes, Yes, Yes, to all my bullshit, to this and to the lack of grammar, and to be given a space to do all this has been a gift, a blessing, i am blessed beyond the ken of mortal and immortal alike, so reach for it and I’ll do the same as we wrench our arms out of sockets to pat each other on the back in this tiny burning moment as the sparrow flies through the window and out again into the darkness. What we leave behind is memory, legacy - and for us, plastic, resin and metal.  When I am gone, what will they do with the Warhound?

Joy in the Repudiation of the False Self

I have taken great, immense, almost painful joy in telling you all these stories over the years. What we’ve spun together has created a new, antagonistic, spikey person, a piece of shit, really, crowing over a hoard of pine and bone. Not the same as, but equally not a million miles from, the author. It has been magical, but I have grown too attached to this particular way of writing and thinking, too self serious, too contrived, too much bullshit, too true (for a given value of truth) so it is time to send him on his journey, and what better time?

I carve a shitty skull out of found bone and bundle it together with rodent bones and broken shards - at this scale not far off a human, albeit a gargantuan, mutated one (but what else are we?). This is a kind of death, a shadow of a requiem for a sliver of a psyche. Aaron has lived, made, written, and now we send him out to sea, coins on his eyes, we watch the coracle buck and turn amidst tossing wave until it falls out of sight. I’m still here - still alive, still writing and building and thinking - but that me isn’t. I make him out of cow, sheep and pig, I dress him in scraps and wrap him in a silken shroud, anointed with lighter fluid and gas. It is kissed and wept over and serenaded.

Then burned on a pyre. Scattered to the winds, to the barrows, on the Warhound, over my keyboard, my brushes. I work it into the cracks of my hands, and smear it across my tools. It is a dark, corrosive dye, a grey and black smear of all that is not but once was. I reek of it, I am indelibly stained with it, by and with all we’ve done together.
