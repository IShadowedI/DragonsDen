Games Industry News Roundup- August 4th, 2026

August 4th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Here at Tabletop Battles, we know that it’s hard to keep track of all the news happening all the time in the games industry. So much is always going on, all over the place, all the time. With games of all sorts, and their related media, being an informed gamer can feel impossible some days. 

That's why every week, we help you stay in the know by rounding up five of the biggest recent stories in the gaming and gaming-related pop culture sphere in the Games Industry News Roundup. Our trusty news boy, Dan “Swiftblade” Richardson, is right here at the very real, and cool TTB newsdesk with the scoop.

Leading Company in Card Grading Facing Giant Class Action Lawsuit

Credit: PSA

The Professional Sports Authenticator (PSA), the largest service in the world for card grading of all kinds, is the subject of a large class-action lawsuit accusing the card grading company of engaging in widespread fraud with its business practices. 

The lawsuit was filed in Baltimore last week by Nicholas Funk on behalf of thousands of people all over America who have used the service in the past. Funk accuses PSA of defrauding the public by engaging in “subjective” behavior while grading cards submitted for quality and value, despite advertising themselves as wholly impartial judges. 

“PSA sells card grading as an expert driven, criteria-based, unbiased, and reliable credential that the market treats as authoritative,” the lawsuit states. “It delivers something materially different. Its grading involves a subjective, opaque, inconsistent, financially conflicted system backed by an obscure online ‘contract’ that purportedly disclaims reliability, caps remedies, and forces arbitration. Consumers paid for expertise and reliability, but PSA sold them manufactured uncertainty and then charged them for it.”

Luck accuses PSA of engaging in this fraudulent grading practice since the very beginning of its existence in 1991 with the very first card ever submitted. The company gave out a "Near Mint-Mint 8" grade to a vintage Honus Wagner baseball card. The card would become the most famous and valuable card in the world, selling for $2.8 million in 2013. This is despite the fact the card was tampered with prior to its submission to PSA, which was later admitted by the owner of the card and, according to Luck, should have been clear during the grading process and greatly diminished the value of the card. The fact PSA still gave the card such a high grade only proves that the fraudulent grading practices of the company are systemic, and have been around from the very beginning.

The lawsuit alleges that since then, the PSA has focused on corporate profit over proper grading practices, engaging in anti-consumer activities like hiring contract temp labor without proper grading training during periods of high volume, giving preferable grades to recognizable players or characters, and artificially limiting the number of perfect 10 grades to increase their sell value.

While PSA hasn’t commented on Funk’s lawsuit, it did release a statement over the weekend regarding a different lawsuit from Steve Lichtmann, who sued the company over a 1952 Topps Mickey Mantle card he submitted for grading. 

“A grade is an expert opinion.” PSA stated in regard to Lichtmann’s lawsuit. “Its value to the hobby rests on the fact that specific grades cannot be bought, pressured, or litigated into existence. A grade obtained only by threatening a lawsuit would be worthless — to the market, and to the millions of collectors who trust PSA to treat every submission the same, whether it comes from a first-time hobbyist or a seasoned collector submitting a rare and sought-after item.”

While card grading initially focused on sports trading cards, its popularity with TCGs has exploded in the post-pandemic years, particularly in the world of Pokémon, and has been a large contributing factor to card shortages and secondary market price spikes.    

Dungeons & Dragons Set to Release “Mature Content” Expansion

Credit: Wizards of the Coast

It was Christmas in July for Dungeons & Dragons fans last week, as Wizards of the Coast revealed multiple new expansions for the hugely popular game, including officially acknowledging the leaked World of Warcraft expansion and announcing a Dark Sun setting book aimed at “mature audiences only.”

The World of Warcraft expansion was accidentally leaked by digital tabletop platform Roll20 in July with a pre-order announcement that was swiftly taken down. Following this, fans suspected that the full reveal for the book would arrive during GenCon last week, and Wizards didn’t disappoint. 

The new sourcebook, which is set to release later this fall, will let players explore the central setting of the WarCraft series, Azeroth. Alongside providing background for the iconic setting, the new book will also feature a litany of new content aimed directly at emulating characters, creatures, or concepts from the hit series. From what was revealed during the keynote, Dungeons & Dragons: World of Warcraft will include 13 new classes, 16 new playable races, over 50 new spells, and over 60 new creatures all based on the established lore of Azeroth.

While this isn’t the first time Wizards has released a crossover sourcebook in modern memory, the company is doing something very new with another one of its upcoming expansions. The long-running grim spinoff world, Dark Sun, will be receiving two new 5.5e books in early 2027, with one focused on the Dark Sun setting and the other focused on gameplay. These books will be “mature rated” books, a first for the current edition of the game, due to their graphic depictions of violence, nudity, and slavery. According to the Keynote announcement, these books will be shrink-wrapped on store shelves to “protect” audiences from their adult content.

While no specific release date was announced for Dark Sun, it’s slated as the first major D&D product for release in 2027. D&D: World of Warcraft is scheduled to release on November 17th, 2026.    

Missile Strike Destroys Ukrainian Board Game Distribution Center

Credit: Catan

The Ukrainian games publisher and distributor, Rozum, is in dire straits after a Russian missile struck its warehouse, destroying its entire stock of over 200,000 games.

Rozum is the largest publishing company for Ukrainian board game localizations since its founding in 2018, including multiple massively popular titles such as Catan, Exploding Kittens, and Power Grid. It has remained in continuous operation since, despite the ongoing invasion of Ukraine by the Russian military. 

The main warehouse for Rozum, located just outside the Ukrainian capital of Kyiv, was struck by a Russian ballistic missile on August 1st. According to Rozum’s parent company, Factor Group, there were fortunately no casualties suffered from the attack, but the stock of games stored in the building was completely lost, with estimated damages around $670,000.

“This blow is not only to cardboard boxes, but also to a cultural product that promotes the Ukrainian language, fosters a culture of shared leisure, brings people together in such difficult times, and educates children through play,” Factor Group wrote regarding the attacks. “Rozum Publishing is currently assessing the damage and planning its next steps. The company’s management assure us that they will definitely resume operations and continue to create popular board games for Ukrainians.”

Factor Group and Rozum launched a fundraising appeal for supporters to donate funds to help the company financially recover from the attack, and to reprint all the games destroyed. 

Last Sentinel Dev Team Hit with Layoffs; Game Stuck in Development Limbo

Credit: Lightspeed LA/Tencent

Remember The Last Sentinel? You know, from that one trailer at The Game Awards back in 2023? Of course you do, how could anyone forget? After a long silence following the game’s announcement, we finally have an update about the planned open world cyberpunk game, but unfortunately it's bad news. Despite some additional promotional videos last year, the game seems no closer to release, and the development team was hit with a large-scale layoff by its owner, Tencent.

According to reporting from Bloomberg, Last Sentinel, which was pitched as Tencent’s competitor to Grand Theft Auto, has been in development hell for six years, going through multiple builds and starting from scratch several times over, costing hundreds of millions of dollars in the process. In a recent statement from Lightspeed LA, the developers behind Last Sentinel, the most recent test build failed to impress players, forcing the team to once again start from square one with the project. 

“Because of this shift, some roles at the studio will be affected,” Lightspeed wrote on their LinkedIn. “We are treating affected team members with compassion and respect, offering comprehensive support and where possible the opportunity to transfer internally.”

While no official word on Last Sentinel’s fate has come from Lightspeed or Tencent, sources close to the issue told Bloomberg that Tencent informed Lightspeed LA leadership that it decided to not move forward with the project following the recent negative reactions to the test build.

While it’s possible we may still see a scaled-down version of Last Sentinel released in the future, or for its assets to be used in other games, its dream of becoming a competitor to Grand Theft Auto is very dead. 

EA Acquisition by Saudi Public Investment Fund Approved 

Credit: Electronic Arts

It’s even more bad news for the video games industry, which seems like the only news coming out of it nowadays. The final approvals by international regulatory bodies for the purchase of EA by the Saudi Public Investment Fund went through last week, with the deal set to close today, August 4th. 

The massive purchase of one of the largest publishers in gaming was announced in September of last year. With this deal, EA would be purchased for $55 billion by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund (PIF), Silver Lake Capital, and Affinity Partners, with the Saudi PIF owning a little over 90% of the company shares. 

The deal was reviewed by regulatory bodies from all over the globe, including the SEC and EU Commission. The SEC would go on to approve the deal, as well as the EU Commission, with the EU writing that the acquisition “"would not raise competition concerns, given its limited impact on competition in the markets where the companies are active.”

Despite the assurances from EA leadership that the purchase wouldn’t cost EA employees their jobs, the company has already gone through several rounds of large layoffs this year in the lead up to the finalization of this deal, including a massive cut to the teams behind the commercial and critical success, Battlefield 6. While we will have to wait and see how things settle following the closure of this deal, it seems near certain that many more jobs will be lost as the publisher transitions to becoming privately owned.
