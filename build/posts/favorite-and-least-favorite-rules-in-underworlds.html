Favorite and Least Favorite Rules in Underworlds

July 15th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Welcome to another installment of Starting Hex, a series about Warhammer Underworlds. A few months ago, I was listening to an episode of the Tabletop Battles Patron exclusive podcast Hams Radio when Rob and Norman discussed some of their favorite and least favorite rules in Warhammer 40k. Being the creative genius I am, I'm stealing that idea wholesale and going over my own hits and misses for the current edition of Warhammer Underworlds. As part of my research for this, I dug into the rules preview articles I wrote when second edition was first being teased as well as some of the review articles for the Embergard and Spitewood releases. That was an amusing trip down memory lane, particularly the coverage of the various previews leading up to second edition Underworlds.

Favorite Rule Changes

Credit: Jake Bennington

Critical Dice Results

The way critical results worked in first edition was that a crit would trump every other success, regardless of the number of successes. For example, an attack looking for hammer and rolling five dice could wind up with five hammer results and still be defeated by a defender rolling a single crit on their single save dice. This was really swingy and lead to some pretty big "feels bad" situations popping up.

By instead treating it as a normal success and attaching special benefits to it via Overrun and Stand Fast, crits are still a highly desirable result to roll but no longer invalidate everything else your opponent does. An unexpected crit on defense can still throw a wrench in an attacker's plans thanks to the damage reduction from Stand Fast, but "reduce damage by one" is a heck of a lot better of a consolation prize than "your attack does nothing."

Credit: Jake Bennington

Warscrolls

Warscrolls are a rad tool that was introduced and immediately helps each warband to have some unique flavor and mechanics.

Previously, this role was addressed by the warband decks. Warscrolls obviously can't offer as many unique options as an entire deck of cards, but one benefit they bring is that you're guaranteed to see them every game. There's no misfortune of having your key faction cards being stuck at the bottom of your deck. It allows players to more reliably leverage unique warband functions to the point that you can build around them.

Warscroll abilities often times serve as a couple of pseudo power cards that you always start the game with. Other times, these warscrolls serve kind of like plot cards that introduce a slew of new rules. Just look at Grandfather's Gardeners and their plague cycle or the Knives of the Crone with the prophecy table to see things that wouldn't fit on a standard sized plot card.

I've been able to do things like building my Hold Treasure focused Morgok's Krushas thanks to always having access to their useful tools of going on guard, pushing, and countering enemy movement ploys.

Warscrolls also needed to exist because fighter cards, where previously a few of these types of rules would be found, no longer have any text on them aside from the fighter names. If you've been playing second edition Underworlds for a while, going back and looking at some of the first edition fighter cards feels pretty weird. They look quite cluttered to me, for instance.

The weapon runemarks as seen in the digital rules of Warhammer Underworlds. Credit: Games Workshop

Weapon Runemarks

The implementation of runemarks are another success in my opinion. Previously, runemarks were just rules shorthand. Notably, this meant you could stack as many runemarks as you wanted on an attack. This resulted in situations like attacks having both ensnare and cleave along with (first edition's version of) grievous or other nonsense. Fighters could very easily be juiced up to become insane death monsters.

Now, however, attacks can only ever choose to use one runemark at a time regardless of how many are available to pick from. This allows effects like Warp-charged from Zikkit's Tunnelpack to grant cleave, ensnare, and brutal all at once without being completely insane.

Another huge change related to runemarks is how grievous is implemented. It wasn't uncommon to find various effects in first edition that would add +1 damage to an attack which would all stack, again leading to the issue of overly enhanced fighters scaling out of control. Since those effects have all (mostly – looking at you, Ylthari's Guardians) been turned into granting grievous, they can now exist side by side without creating something unhinged like a Spinefin that hits for five damage.

Credit: Jake Bennington

Focus Action

This is a somewhat minor change compared to the above. The focus action was added in second edition and merely served to combine the previously two unique actions of pitching power or objective cards with Strategize and Venture. By the way, I had to go look those two up because I sure as heck didn't remember their names. Did any other first edition players recall them before you read them?

Focus is a substantially more powerful version of the two separate Strategize or Venture options because it's more flexible and removes the requirement to decide which type of card you need to cycle through more because you can do both. It's still not a super common action in my experience; I see it in maybe every four or five games, but when it does come up it feels less like a desperation move and more like a solid strategic option available.

Least Favorite Rule Changes

Battle of the dogs. Credit: Jake Bennington

Underdog

It's not uncommon to see Games Workshop introduce a rule in one of their games and suddenly start seeing it in various other systems they produce. Underdog was debuted in Age of Sigmar before also showing up in Underworlds with the release of Embergard.

I understand why Games Workshop wanted to include this mechanic in their games. Its nature is to allow a player who is behind to have an easier time to catch up and, in theory, result in fewer non-games or blowouts. It's natural to think this would also lead to both players having more fun. Not only is "having fun" a pretty important goal of a game, but it also means players are generally happier and presumably spend more money on playing the game. So far so good.

What's weird is how it just doesn't really do much of anything in Underworlds.

There are 22 cards that behave slightly differently when played by the underdog player. Of those, maybe half are ever worth playing in a game. The design problem a lot of them fall into is the baseline effect being so weak that it requires being the underdog to be playable, and that's not something that you can reliably count on so the card never makes the cut in a Nemesis deck. Most of these powered-up effects aren't even all that wild, so it feels like wasted design space in my opinion.

Arguably, the most useful effect underdog has is serving as a tie breaker for roll-offs at the start of each round and that isn't terribly high praise.

The boards in Warhammer Underworlds Embergard. Credit: Games Workshop

Single Board

I've gone back and forth on the change to having a single board as opposed to first edition's paired boards. For those unfamiliar, previously each player would bring a smaller board to the game and the two boards would be arranged adjacent to each other to create a larger play space. They could be long edges adjacent, offset slightly, or short edges adjacent and each of these created a unique playing area.

Aside from just being thematically cool and fairly unique, there were a lot of benefits to this system. Each player had some influence over the shape of the gameplay area and could pick options that favor their gameplan the most. Whether this was something like a Lady Harrow's Mournflight player taking boards with plentiful blocked hexes to fly through with spooky ghosts or a Hold focused warband taking a board that allowed for a carefully planned feature token layout, it gave agency to the players.

It also leads to more variety, helping keep the game feeling fresh compared to a single static board. Not only is each battlefield made up of two boards instead of one, there are also multiple arrangements that can be created based on how they are rotated or lined up.

The shift to a single board isn't fully negative, however. I still maintain that the hurdle for a new player in learning board placement was a brutal one. It's wasn't uncommon for board placement from an inexperienced player to cost them the game before it ever started and that's hardly a great feeling. It's one thing to play a few turns and find out you're getting demolished, but it's a lot less appealing to hand your opponent one of your boards and witness them set up the gameplay area to realize you screwed up and are substantially handicapped before even starting play.

Also, there were a freaking ton of boards in first edition. Currently, we have a single double sided Embergard board and double sided Spitewood board giving us four options. At the tail end of first edition, there were 20(!) boards legal for the Nemesis format, each of which could be combined in eight different orientations with your opponent's board. That's a staggering mental burden to try and familiarize oneself with, not to mention how unwieldy and heavy carrying a bunch of extra boards is. In reality, players trying to optimize would often just bring 2-3 boards with them to events but that's still a lot of stuff to wade through to get to that point.

In the end, I think my ideal solution here would be a reintroduction of the first edition style boards but definitely slow down the amount of them that are released. If we had two double sided boards in Embergard and two more double sided boards in Spitewood, we'd be about two-and-a-half years into the game with a reasonable amount of boards by now.

The Order of Azyr purge the devoted of Slaanesh. Featuring very scary lethal hexes. Credit: Marquis of Peaches

Removal of Lethal Hexes

This is probably my most minor quibble, but I miss hexes that damage fighters when they enter them. Stagger hexes are great and all, but hexes that can outright kill fighters emphasize fighter positioning and treasure token placement even more.

There are a few warbands that can emulate lethal hexes and every time I run into them, I miss the wider access to these damaging spaces on the board. Zarbag's Gitz are notorious with the shenanigans they can pull off using Snirk's deadly effect, but other warbands have a more limited access to similar damaging abilities including Mollog's Mob, Borgit's Beastgrabbaz, and Thundrik's Profiteers. If you've ever played against a skilled player piloting these warbands, you definitely feel the impact of pseudo lethal hexes.

There's nothing stopping lethal hexes from being added again, either as printed hexes on the board or as the placeable feature tokens in the style of the Aqua Ghyranis tokens. The other things mentioned here are less likely to change without a major edition overhaul, but maybe there's a chance for painful hexes in the future.

Credit: Jake Bennington

Removal of Faction Decks

I'm reluctant to include this entry because I don't necessarily have the same viewpoint as what I've heard other players make. I don't think the change to warscrolls from faction decks is innately a downgrade.

Before the creation of warscrolls, warbands instead had a deck of (mostly) unique power and objective cards. In Rivals, you could simply play with one of these decks. In Nemesis, you could pair the warband's deck with any of the universal decks. This meant that no matter what deck you paired with in Nemesis, each warband would feel pretty unique in their execution… usually.

The downside is that some faction decks were just weak and dragged their warband down. If a warband's deck didn't have at least a few solid objectives, they were fully reliant on leveraging another universal deck to have any chance at scoring in a game. This meant warbands with weaker decks wound up mostly just running a universal deck with a couple of their own cards which didn't do a ton to differentiate them from another warband doing the same, especially because those three or so unique cards could just never be seen during the course of a game. At least warscrolls guarantee you'll get to use your warband's unique tools every game.

With those caveats noted, I do miss how much flavor was packed into these decks. A premier example was the Headsmen's Curse deck. Not only were the majority of the cards in there of a playable power level, they also did a lot to push the flavor of the cursed ghosts cleaving heads from enemies.

I think in the end, my issue is that of the 60 or so warbands we have, it's difficult for each of them to feel unique when they just have stripped down fighter cards and a very load bearing warscroll. I don't really see this ever changing for a variety of reasons. In addition to what I've touched on already, it's a massive balance ask for the designers to also have to worry about the existence of nearly 2,000 extra cards in the card pool (32 cards per warband x 60 warbands). Likewise, it's a steep learning curve for anyone new to the game to need to familiarize themselves with the same number of cards.

Perhaps a slightly expanded warscroll is a middle ground that could work. Including an objective to score on the warscroll as well as the current abilities and rules modifications feels like a compromise that would go a long way to helping each warband feel a little more special.

On the whole, I think the changes made in second edition were pretty solid. It's not perfect, but I acknowledge the reasoning behind a lot of the changes and imagine they were made with good intentions. Incrementally marching toward improvement doesn't sound like a bad approach to me.
