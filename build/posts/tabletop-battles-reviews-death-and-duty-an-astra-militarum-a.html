Tabletop Battles Reviews: Death and Duty, an Astra Militarum Anthology

July 17th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Games Workshop

Today's anthology, Death and Duty, has a rather curious distinction that's puzzled me since it arrived. It hit the shelves last November, on the same day as another Black Library anthology, No Peace Among Stars (review). That in itself isn't unusual- Death and Duty had an Imperial Guard theme while No Peace Among Stars was devoted to putting short story Theme Weeks into print, meaning they each seemed to carve out their own space.

But the problem here was that one of the three Theme Weeks selected was October 2024's Astra Militarum Week. As a result, two anthologies released on the same day ended up having four stories in common. That's a full quarter of No Peace Among Stars, and a third of Death and Duty, which is far from ideal.

I don't know if this was a case of the left hand not knowing what the right was doing, with two different editors developing their anthologies independently, or something else. Regardless, there's still plenty to look at in Death and Duty- not least because three of the stories are brand new!

Here's the rundown of all twelve stories, with excerpts from my previous review for those stories already previously examined.

Image credit: Games Workshop

Less Than Human, by Steve Lyons

The anthology opens with the action-filled Less than Human. For weeks amidst an offensive against the t'au, Krieg forces have been waging war the Krieg way: artillery and skirmishing to fix the enemy in their position, then using sappers to build a tunnel right under their noses.

Close to completion, a Mordian colonel decides to pull rank, using them as a forward shock element to soften up the entrenched enemy before her Mordians punch through any gaps bought at a high price from Krieg lives. Outranked and with no ability to refuse, the Krieg commander has little choice but to go along... or does he?

Make no mistake, this story is pure Krieg fan service, but fortunately I'm amongst those fans. The Krieg were right, the Mordians wrong, and it all comes out on the battlefield. But Steve Lyons writes crisp action scenes with a terrific sense of what makes the Krieg, well, the Krieg. The selfless duty, the fatalistic stoicism, the marrow-deep sense of purpose and cohesion. A solid opener!

Image credit: Games Workshop

Blood Sands, by Victoria Hayward

For the next one, we get a tale featuring Sentinel pilots, which isn't something you see a great deal of. Deployed in the desert to stalk and destroy Genestealer Cult cells, their enemy always seems to be one step ahead of them. Fighting through harsh terrain and the threat of ambush, it soon becomes apparent that their enemy seems to have a source of intelligence on the inside. Can they sniff out the mole before the Cults destroy everything?

Victoria Hayward's Deathworlder was my Book of the Year in 2024, though this story originally predates that one by a year. This one's a solid story, but I found the end a bit of a letdown as the climactic moment pivots on a slip admission from the mole, what TVTropes refers to as, I Never Said it Was Poison.

That's the thing with short stories, they're short. In a novel you have a lot more room for reveals, the give and take of plot and momentum, and if a plot element might be a little underwhelming that's okay, because there's plenty there to offset it. Blood Sands has all this great build-up, but being a short story it's got just the one trick up its sleeve.

Image credit: Games Workshop

Hell Fist, by Justin Woolley

Justin Woolley's Hell Fist is a frequent flyer, having seen inclusion in three anthologies over the last four years, starting with 2023's Galaxy of Horrors. It's also one of the stories that was in both this collection as well as Carnage Unending.

The title refers to Colonel Haskell "Hell Fist" Aldalon, who was in Woolley's 2022 novel Catachan Devil, and this story mixes it up by viewing Aldalon through the eyes of the orks. It's an amusing take with the orks seeing "Hell Fist" as some sinister bogeyman stalking them in the jungle, a reversal of the point of you you'd usually get in an Astra Militarum story.

It also- and I'll admit this is just a peeve of mine- has the main character understanding Orkish without acknowledging it in the text. In Leontus: Lord Solar (review), Rob Young also has his protagonist able to speak the Orks' tongue, but goes into the why of it.

All the same, this is a serviceable action story that isn't out of place here.

Image credit: Games Workshop

The Sum of its Parts, by Rhuairidh James

Rather than following the soldiers, Rhuairidh James- who saw his novel debut earlier this year with Death Rider (review), centers this story instead on a single Leman Russ tank, one which changes hands over the years of its service. Born on a forge world under siege, Sebastian's Lance will in its time be crewed by teams as diverse as Whiteshields to Jungle Fighters, and ultimately even a Genestealer cult, with each leaving a small but indelible mark on the vehicle.

This technique reminded me of Finest Hour, by Sam Ryan (which, as luck would have it, is also in this anthology!), with its fixation on an object passing hands. James does terrific work here, managing to make you care about the rotating cast of crew a little at a time as they add to the legacy of the Lance. While there are some great stories in this anthology, this one is my "best in show" winner.

Image credit: Games Workshop

The Strength of Symbols, by Carrie Harris

This is one of the 'overlap' stories from No Peace Among Stars, noting in that review:

"While like many of this collection's authors Carrie Harris only has a couple of short stories under her belt for the Black Library (so far!), she has a number of other writing credits for properties ranging from Marvel Super-Heroes to Arkham Horror (as well as her own). She brings this polish to her tale of a Valhallan medic transplanted to a Cadian squad who struggles with feeling like an outsider.

When she has the opportunity to recover a regimental banner stolen by Orks, Cathris Korr finds that being Cadian isn't just about what planet you happen to have been born on. This story didn't quite land for me. It's a solid enough action tale against a largely anonymous horde of Orks, but the emotional arc didn't carry enough of the weight to really set it apart from a combat writeup. That's not necessarily bad, but if a story is mostly combat I'd like to see it either have a little more emotional resonance or a clever twist on the usual, such as with the story I awarded 'Best in Show' for 2025's Black Library Celebration Subscription (review), Finest Hour by Sam Ryan. That story also focused on a battle standard as an essential part of the story, but saw multiple POV changes as the standard kept changing hands."

Image credit: Games Workshop

The Redoubt, by John Sollitto

One of the book's trio of new stories, this one comes to us by John Sollitto, who we saw recently with the short Rok Crackers in the terrific World Ablaze anthology (review).

In The Redoubt, a squad of Guard troops are tasked with holding on to an outpost under constant threat from the t'au. Sometimes the best way to keep something is to give it away, however, and the increasingly desperate Guardsmen lay a trap by giving the t'au exactly what they want.

This one's a solid action story that almost feels like a snapshot from a bigger story. As with Rok Crackers a little more depth on our characters would not have been unwelcome, and a couple times I couldn't quite place the dimensions of the battlefield in my head, but Sollitto continues to show a flair for crafting engaging combat scenarios.

Image credit: Games Workshop

Finest Hour, by Sam Ryan

I initially reviewed this one for the Black Library Celebration 2025 eShort subscription, and here's what I wrote then:

"The fourth entry in the Subscription is my pick for Best in Show. Sam Ryan’s Astra Militarum tale is one of the best shorts I’ve read in some time.

It’s a straight up war story, but one with a riveting twist as it centers not on any one person, but rather on a Cadian battle standard. A flag that gets passed from one set of hands to the next as the battle continues to unfold, this 'wandering point of view' allows us glimpses of moments of heroism (and cowardice) from a number of different characters.

The gamble Ryan takes here is that the reader will care much about any of them. That’s already a challenge in the short story format, investing the reader with an emotional stake in far less space than a novel. But to try that with multiple characters who enter (and then just as quickly exit, either returning to the background or disappearing altogether) is laudably ambitious.

Ryan not only pulls it off, but he sticks the landing too. Finest Hour goes far beyond a simple action story and only the hardest of hearts will remain unmoved by the nobility of character revealed time and again under the very worst conditions. It’s the small notes that Ryan weaves through his characters, from the Vostroyan tank commander ("forty years' service and eight evaded promotions had left her with countless hours under fire") to the Tech-Adept’s impassioned line "the Song of Iron has come to her coda, but her requiem will belong to you!" to the Krieg Colonel’s determined stoicism against impossible odds.

If you’re not already a fan of Imperial Guard stories, this one stands a very good chance of making you one. It is exceptional, and a very promising entry in the portfolio of another new Black Library writer (like the previous author, his first, the Warhammer Crime story Call of Oblivion, was part of last year’s Advent Calendar subscription)."

Image credit: Games Workshop

Resounding, by Russell Zimmerman

Usually when it comes to character depth my criticism is that there isn't enough of it, but Zimmerman here seems to be the rare case of perhaps giving a little too much. The sin here isn't one of excess so much as that it all felt a bit front-loaded, and thus conspicuous by its quantity rather than being woven through a little more naturally. The good news, though, is that very quickly the story evens out and it's another fine battle story that pits the Astra Militarum against overwhelming odds, this time the inexorable Death Guard.

Zimmerman is no stranger to writing the sons of Mortarion, which he did to terrific effect in Seven Ships, a story that saw him win Best Black Library Debutant (along with Avalon Irons) in 2025 for the Goonhammer Book Awards. He also had a story in the most recent issue of White Dwarf, a tale of a Steel Legion learning that sometimes getting what you asked for isn't the blessing you'd expect it to be (reviewed here).

While the tale of Colonel Zava Heerde and her Laessians is another fine slice of a perilous life in the Guard, the same eye for detail that made Seven Ships so good really shines here. It's the little observations that give the story such a feeling of place, whether it be the miserable environment to the ever-present spectre of illness and infirmity that comes with proximity to the favored of Nurgle. I'd love to see Zimmerman get a chance at a novel featuring the Death Guard at some point, as he has a real knack for the legion.

Image credit: Games Workshop

Those Without Mercy, by Callum Davis

When I reviewed this story for No Peace Among Stars, it was right on the eve of the release of Chem Dog, the novel to which this story served as a prelude (and reviewed here).

As I noted in my original review, 'I've found that the further away from a POV character a Commissar is in a story, the more likely they are to be a miserable bastard and that certainly seems to apply here.' Consider, for instance, this passage:

Behind her chem-inhaler, she had no teeth. Commissar Hasp had taken one for each time she’d sworn at a Cadian officer who said her boots needed polishing. The ramrod-backed man had run out of teeth to yank out, and so had moved on to her fingernails. When it was pointed out to him that that might make handling her lasgun more difficult for her, he ordered her tied to a post and lashed. A dozen scars now criss-crossed her back.

Those Without Mercy, which as a story involves Hasp investigating the murder of a fellow commissar by a unit of Chem-Dogs, lay the groundwork for both the character and his relationship with the regiment he's attached to. He is unrepentantly biased, seeing the Savlar troops as little more than petty criminals, and the story's end leads directly into the novel that follows.

I enjoyed this one quite a bit, and it certainly whetted the appetite for what followed.

Image credit: Games Workshop

The Last Psyker, by Shauna Lawless

From the No Peace Among Stars review:

"If you were looking for an allegory of the tragedy of the Imperium writ small, you could do worse than Shauna Lawless's lone Black Library tale. We've seen plenty of times where the Imperium's bitter soil has nurtured the seeds of its undoing, and all of the same ingredients are present in this tale of a bullied psyker.

With his squad's prejudices on full display, there's little Lieutenant-Psyker Karleth can do to earn the respect of his squad. Isolated and ostracized by his fellow Guardsman, Karleth finds validation and acceptance from an unlikely source- but one that isn't everything it seems.

Chaos seduction stories are a bit like roller coasters-the more time you spend going up the more fun you're likely to have on the way down. With Karleth fighting other characters for POV time (Lawless uses an alternating-chapter-POV structure) in an already-short story, there wasn't enough of Karleth for readers deeply engaged. Still, a solid effort."

Image credit: Games Workshop

Exterminator, by Mike Vincent

Again, from No Peace Among Stars:

"If Mike Vincent has a through-line in hos small but growing body of work in the Black Library, it's the 8th Legion. In both of the first two stories he wrote in 2024 (this one as well as The Vengeful Dead), the Night Lords are present as the tale's antagonists. This changed the following year with Blades of Atrocity, the short story that served as prelude to his debut novel The Remnant Blade and gave us our first taste of Dalchian Rassaq, the "Skin Taker."

Of course, we're getting ahead of ourselves a bit there, but I always like to see how each piece contributes to a writer's own canon. Here for Astra Militarum Week, Vincent gives us the story of a group of Guardsmen involved in transporting a cargo-8 of "priority supplies." As we quickly learn, however, this is a case of "mushroom management" (as in, kept in the dark and fed shit). When the convoy is caught in a raging blizzard and the hauler overturns and ruptures, the crew discovers what they've really been transporting all along- and it's not happy.

From the intriguing premise to its gripping execution (no pun intended), this is one of the anthology's standouts.

Ave Dominus Nox, baby."

Image credit: Games Workshop

Worm of Valhalla, by Tom R Pike

Finally, we end the anthology with the second Black Library story from Tom R. Pike, following behind Dream of the Locust (collected in No Peace Among Stars). Like The Sum of its Parts earlier, a tank plays a central role in the story but this time it's the crew that carry the tale.

The "Worm" referred to in the title is the name of the tank, a fact which offers some lighthearted moments in the story as we come to learn that it was clearly named by a non-Valhallan given that the worms are miniscule things. Here a new officer, chafing at an unwanted promotion that puts him in the crosshairs of danger, decides the fastest way to alienate the crew and demonstrate that he's a poor fit for command.

Of course, as the saying goes, no plan survives first contact with the enemy, and things take a turn once the crew finds themselves under fire. It's a tale with terrific characterization and no small amount of heart, and Pike delivered one of the anthology's standout stories here.

Final Thoughts

I still find the overlap between Death and Duty and No Peace Among Stars to be baffling, given they released at the same time. But all the same, while I didn't find this to be a standout collection of stories on that basis, it's still worth a grab for the other stories- particularly the trio of new tales from Sollitto, Zimmerman, and Pike. Bonus points as well for having an identifiable theme here, which many of the other anthologies don't seem to trifle with.

As a paperback-only release, where collections like this shine is in the value-for-dollar department. With this collection selling for USD $18, the bar is fairly low for expectation, and it easily clears it.
