Tabletop Battles Reviews: Eldfall Chronicles: Apocrypha

November 9th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Tomorrow, November 10, is the first day of the Eldfall Chronicles: Apocrypha Gamefound project. This is a cooperative game allowing 1-4 players to explore the archives of a magical, shifting library. These sealed archives have been opened and adventurers are sent down to the depths to recover long lost knowledge. Of course, things won't be easy given that there are a variety of monsters and mysteries to be overcome.

As of the time of writing, it's already picked up over 11,000 followers and the number keeps growing. Freecompany was gracious enough to provide us with a prototype sample of the game which I eagerly dove into. If you've been following any of the Northern Wind coverage here, some of the game might look a little familiar. It follows a similar approach to cooperative (or solo) gameplay as seen in that game -- there are tiles, miniatures, cards, tokens, dice, standees, and a collection of various rulebooks. There are a lot of things in the box! There are various levels to back the project at which provide different amounts of miniatures and other goodies, but this is a good representation of the basic level.

A spread of the components from the prototype review copy of Apocrypha. Credit: Jake Bennington
  • Four miniatures representing the four heroes. There's the one-armed Chronomancer with a floating greatsword, a Kitsune spell-archer with ember magic, a shapeshifting half-Lamia assassin, and a frontline berserker paladin.
  • Standees representing not only the enemies you encounter, but also younger versions of the protagonists who you wind up using during the prologue/tutorial section.
  • 30+ double-sided tiles to build the dungeon maps.
  • Multiple handfuls of cardboard tokens.
  • Multiple decks of cards, from upgrades to monster behaviors to special chaos cards which make the game more challenging and unpredictable.
  • Larger, tarot-sized profile cards with the stats for each of the heroes and hostiles.
  • Multiple books for the game rules, the quests, secret information revealed during gameplay, and spells used by spellcasting models.

The Miniatures

Given that this is the baseline product, it only had the four heroes as miniatures but rest assured all the creatures present as standees will also be available in miniature form as well. Everyone I have talked to who has gotten their hands on Eldfall Chronicles miniatures has talked about how great the sculpts are. These certainly continue the trend of being cool looking characterful models. Cyrus, the one-armed mage, is quite eye-catching and it's no mystery why Tabletop Battles' own Blake Law painted him up first. Look at this guy!

Cyrus from Apocrypha. Credit: Blake Law

The other three models are just as striking. Rien, the snake lady, is a tad more cheesecake than my personal preference, but if nothing else it does fit the semi-anime vibes that Eldfall Chronicles puts off from time to time. We'll be sure to showcase these heroes later on along with some painting guides. Stay tuned!

They weren't included in this sample, but the monsters in particular are some fantastic designs that I can't wait to get my hands on. There are two different sculpts of mimics that look like the kind of thing painters could have some real fun with. My favorites are probably the Ushtabi which were originally golems which tended to the vast halls but the ones we encounter in the lower levels of the archives have been warped by the strange magic down there. Not only do they look imposing and otherworldly, but as a former librarian I know I would have loved to have something like an Ushabti to keep me company while wandering the stacks.

One of the incredibly cool Ushabti sculpts and concept art. There's another one that is carrying mummies like duffle bags! Credit: Freecompany

Changes from Northern Wind

If you have any familiarity with Eldfall Chronicles in general or Northern Wind in particular, none of the core rules of Apocrypha will come as much of a surprise. I went page-by-page with the rulebooks from both Apocrypha and Northern Wind, making notes of any changes between the two and didn't come up with more than a few bullets. If you're coming into the game with less familiarity, feel free to hop down past this list. I just thought it'd be helpful to returning players to see what's different.

  • One potentially huge change is that your heroes automatically heal after each quest. They'll still keep all their other ailments and Weak Points, but the rulebook specifically says they are healed of wounds after every mission. Very helpful.
  • True Line of Sight is listed as the default. Assisted Line of Sight is still present, but it's listed as an optional rule. NW had both listed as possible options but neither (as far as I can tell) was the default way of playing.
  • Rules have been added for multiple opposing groups of hostiles. Presumably there will be some in-fighting or multiple factions showing up in missions, so this spells out how two groups of hostiles can beat each other up.
  • The Deathblow action has been removed. This doesn't seem to be very impactful given my experience in both of these dungeon crawl games, honestly.
  • Probably one or two other things that I missed! I saw, for instance, that the Attack of Opportunity advanced rule is worded slightly differently but I couldn't parse how it functionally changed.

Gameplay

For those of you who aren't familiar with Eldfall Chronicles, let me break down the rules in a very brief and high level summary. If you want to dig into them more, I have good news -- the rules are all available online for free! Scroll down to the "Dungeoncrawl" section to look at the Northern Wind rules which, as I mentioned, are nearly identical to the rulebook for Apocrypha. This is a crunchy ruleset for sure. It reminds me in the best way of playing some older editions of Dungeons & Dragons with random miniatures and a wet erase battlemat with my friends.

The four heroes of Apocrypha. On the left are their younger forms that you control during a prologue/tutorial, then after 20 years of game time you pick them up again on the right. Credit: Jake Bennington

The core resolution mechanic is rolling some number of d20 dice and trying to get equal to or below your target number. Yes, we want to roll 1s here and that's why the Eldfall Chronicles dice have symbols on the 1 instead of the 20. Each model has a set of statistics that govern different actions and determine how good they are at them. For instance, Rien the assassin has an offense (OFF) value of 15, meaning she'll be successful at stabbing stuff if you can roll 15 or lower. Kimiko, the Kitsune spellmaiden, is much less adept at up-close violence and would need to roll equal to or under her OFF of 8 to land melee blows. There are, of course, various modifiers that increase or decrease your chances of success as well as reroll mechanics to help fish for favorable results.

The other half of what I'd say makes games of Eldfall Chronicles feel unique is the action/reaction system. One of the stats that ever model has is their stamina (STA). This is used for a few things, but the most prominent by far is how many Action Points (AP) the model will get each turn. The average by far is 2, but there are exceptions for unusually potent beings such as bosses. At the start of a turn, every model gains their full allotment of AP. The first turn (usually) belongs to the heroes and lasts until they have all spent all of their AP. You can decide which order to activate models and don't have to spend all of one model's AP before activating another. After all the heroes are done, the turn ends and it becomes the hostiles' turn where everyone's AP refreshes.

Cyrus has a Spectral Blades token that moves around the battlefield and he can use as the point of origin for his melee attacks. Credit: Jake Bennington

However, since the hostiles also have AP during the hero turn, they're going to be reacting to things that the heroes do. Monster behavior is governed by double-sided behavior cards that are unique for each type of model. One side lists the movement and actions they'll take on their own turn, and the other side of the card lists the reactions they'll take when it's the heroes' turn. Each model will have their own target priority and a set of types of movement and actions to perform based on whatever circumstance it is in.

Ferron is a fun archetype of a paladin berserker. He is remarkably tanky, but also no slouch in melee. Doubly so because he gets powered up by anyone in the party taking damage. Credit: Jake Bennington

Turns keep going back and forth between the heroes and the hostiles, with each side fully replenishing their AP in each turn. This means that even though it might not be your turn, you still usually have just as many opportunities to take actions and make choices that impact the game. It can also lead to baiting out certain reactions to burn the enemy AP which leaves them vulnerable. This is how it works in the skirmish game as well, for those who are curious.

So, models spend AP and do things. What kind of things can they do? The basics are pretty simple, but as always the devil is in the details. First up is the move, then the action. You can forgo the normal version of either of these to do a special version of the other.

Kimiko has the ability to place Ember Spirit tokens on the board that grant her various benefits. She can also shoot spells out of her Ember Bow! Credit: Jake Bennington

Your choices for Normal Movement are to walk (move equal to the model's speed) or to idle (only rotate but otherwise remain stationary, but this is the only way to do a Special Action). A Special Movement can be Run (move twice the model's speed), Climb (move the model's speed over elevated ground), or Jump (move the model's speed across a space) but if you do any of those last three then you won't get to do any kind of action.

Actions have many more options. The most common Normal Actions include Attack (whether melee or ranged; offensive spells also fall into this action) and Dodge (an agility check to avoid incoming damage and also move half the model's speed), but there are also other things like Assist, Interact, Perceive, and Trade. There's only one Special Action here and it is simply called Ritual and is used for a very few powerful spells or special mechanics within the quests themselves.

Rien dealt monstrous amounts of damage in my games but also sports a mechanic where she slowly charges up and transforms into her fierce Perfected Form.

It's worth noting that any Reaction performed has to choose from the list of Normal Actions. This does mean that even when it isn't your turn, you are quite likely to be repositioning due to Dodge.

Specific Quests

Let's get into the specifics of the missions that I was given to play. The prototype kit that Freecompany sent over had four-ish missions. Why "four-ish" instead of four? Much like Northern Wind, the first few missions are very stripped down tutorial missions intended to help get familiar with the game mechanics. After those, there's a bit of a jump into a mission from later on in the story to get a feel for a full, "real" mission. Finally, I was provided with a taste of the modular Location Quests.

The very beginning of the first tutorial mission in Apocrypha. Credit: Jake Bennington

The first tutorial, The Sealed Archives, introduces the basic concepts of the game. One really fun touch is that you're playing as the main heroes of the game, just 20 years in the past. There are even standees and unique unit cards for them featuring some de-aged artwork. In this mission, you'll learn to lay out the tiles to build a gameplay area and place Encounter and Intrigue tokens. The former trigger when any of your heroes enter the same space as the Encounter token and typically sets up the next leg of the mission. Intrigue tokens are various things that your heroes will interact with that vary wildly with each quest. This time around, the encounters are traps. Fitting for delving into a magical and spooky library, these are some basic traps that you'd find anywhere from Indiana Jones to Dungeons & Dragons. We have spears stabbing out from walls and a bolt shooter firing down a long hallway. Both of these have switches to disable them beyond the point where the trap triggers.

The traps do an admirable job of introducing the dice rolling involved in attacks, but also the importance of dodging and baiting out reactions from enemies to keep some of your fighters safer from retaliation when activating later in the turn. I had a streak of my typical luck when Ferron walked up and triggered the first one. I figured he was the tankiest member of the group, so he could be the guy stepping on all the pressure plates. The spears shoot out and I react with a Dodge, needing a 10 or lower. I failed. That's okay, they're relatively weak attacks and he has a ton of armor, meaning they'll only damage on a 2 or less. I rolled a 1. That's still okay! Ferron is a berserker and can, once per turn, roll against his toughness to ignore incoming damage. I just need to roll a 12 or lower. I rolled a 17.

After this, I realized it would be incredibly embarrassing to have a character die in a tutorial level so I took it more cautiously. It did mean the gameplay kind of dragged on where one party member went forward, triggered a trap, and then kept going forward to get to the switch to turn it off. The rest of the party just twiddled their thumbs until it was clear so they could move up and repeat it again.

The second tutorial mission in Apocrypha drops you straight into combat. Credit: Jake Bennington

The second tutorial mission, Those Who Remain, has a nice touch of just using the same layout as the previous mission except you're going through it backwards. There are story reasons but I'll leave it vague for now. You finally get to start fighting stuff here starting off with the very first turn of being dropped right onto a big baddie. However, your party is fairly weak at this point so it's hardly a walk in the park. At one point, there is a tip in the quest book to use weak points on enemies to take them out easier. I was kind of expecting the spawning enemies to come in with a few weak points like they did in early Northern Wind missions, but that wasn't the case.

After this, it jumps into a later part of the story somewhere in Arc II. This is a much larger, complete mission that involves multiple Ushabti, Archive Fiends (flying bird demon looking things), Forsaken Scholars and Spellblades (magic spooky undead), and a surprise insane boss encounter at the very end. There's even another party of adventurers that you can swoop in to save.

This was a much more enjoyable experience compared to the tutorials. The finale has a frankly absurd Devourer of the End burst out of the wall like the Kool-Aid Man in between your party and the exit. It had a staggering STA of 4, twice as much as any of my heroes, and hit like a truck while being built like a brick house. It definitely felt like the idea was to just push past and run for the exit. I decided to take advantage of one of Cyrus's tools with a spell that grants Stagger, which drains AP from the target. I swung at the beast, connected, dealt two damage and drained away an AP while it missed. Then I did it again. Suddenly it couldn't react to any of my other six activations and it was 40% dead. "I don't think this is how it's supposed to play out," I thought to myself but I then went ham and beat the unholy crap out of it. The Devourer of the End never even harmed my party and I wound up taking it out due to some insane dice on my part. I'm talking this thing only getting 18-20 results on dice that needed 17 or lower for success 4 times in a row and me getting multiple critical hits to ignore its high armor.

This guy is absolutely insane. Credit: Jake Bennington

That felt a little anticlimactic so I set it back up and tried again. The dice were much more average this time around, and it was a frantic race to the end. There were multiple points on the map to interact with in order to slow the behemoth down and distract it (including the option to send it after the rescued but injured other party!). Ferron made a valiant stand to let my squishier models get further down the hall only to be slammed by the Devourer of the End and sent flying... right into the injured Rien who went down in a heap. Fortunately Ferron is also the healer, so he just booped the snake lady on the nose and she felt better. Everyone ran away and made it to live another day.

The final mission wasn't exactly a mission, but rather a framework of what are called Location Quests. These are new and function as a semi-random set of missions with different parameters that get determined via dice rolls. There's a table for Sites, one for Challenges, and one for Side Quests and after rolling on all of these, you combine the results into a mission. I'm very interested in how many options are in each of these tables in the final version -- the sample we were given only had one site, three challenges, and two side quests but that still provides a decent amount of variety. One of the quests involves you tracking down a magic lamp to free a djinn and the other has a hidden pomegranate scented hookah lounge.

What I imagine are very stripped down versions of the Location Quest tables that will appear in the final product. Also, yes, there's a typo for Chased and it should be 1-7. Credit: Jake Bennington

I feel like these Location Quests can be a handy framework for people making their own missions as well. I imagine there will be plenty of ideas in the final product that can be lifted for organized events like at conventions or game nights at the FLGS.

The sample Site layout for the Location Quests. Credit: Jake Bennington

Final Thoughts

On the whole, I enjoyed my time playing the prototype missions of Apocrypha and fully plan to back the Gamefound project once it goes live on the 10th. The game is definitely crunchy -- I feel like it might be right on the threshold of what I enjoy in a game, but it's hitting all the right buttons for me right now. I'll admit it's also somewhat of a novelty for me because this is my first solo board game experience and I think I've found my niche. It's a lot easier scheduling wise to sit down after my kid goes to bed and play a mission than trying to round up 1-3 friends to play whatever other games I've been trying to get into!

One of the biggest downsides I've run into is that there are just so many keywords and they're scattered all throughout the rulebook. If I come across something new or that I don't remember, I flip between the sections for States, Skills, Combat Arts, Class Abilities, Traits and Environments because sometimes I can't quite tell what goes in which category. I have noticed that over my dozen or so games between Northern Wind and Apocrypha that things are starting to pick up pace because I'm not having to reference the rulebook nearly as often. It's one of those things that will get easier with repetition and practice, just like almost everything else in life. Shocking, right?

There have been a few places where I ran into a weird situation that I couldn't quite puzzle out on my own, and fortunately the Eldfall Chronicles Discord server has channels for rules questions and I've never gone more than a few minutes without getting insightful replies that helped me out of whatever pickle I found myself in. There are also a lot of gorgeous models and terrain shared in there, so hop in and check it out. Tell them I said hi!
