Black Library Readers’ Hall of Fame: The Winners of 2018, and Class of 2019

October 10th, 2026 via Goonhammer
The Black Library Readers’ Hall of Fame is Tabletop Battles’s long-term, reader-participation initiative to recognize those novels that have “cultural, historical, or aesthetic significance” to the Black Library. Through the votes of readers as well as our community panel of community voices, we’ll examine the entire history of the Black Library, induct the latest winners, and vote for the next round’s candidates every other week.

Welcome back to the Class of 2018 Edition of the Black Library Readers’ Hall of Fame! At this point in Black Library history, the original Horus Heresy is winding down, the Age of Sigmar is heating up, and 40K is firing on all cylinders. An impressive seven books from that year have been selected by the community for induction to the Hall, as well as one author getting in for the first time!

As a reminder, if you're really enjoying this project and want to see it from a different angle, the companion piece, The Hall Monitor, runs over at my Substack, The Warhammer Wordforge. A cross between a director's commentary and behind-the-scenes look, each installment we dive a little deeper into a particular element of the Readers' Hall of Fame project. Hope to see you there!

The Envelope Please

Now, let's get to the stars of the show, the books inducted for 2018!

Keri, The WH40K Book Club: "I really don't like the Death Guard. A lot about them creeps me out and, quite frankly, grosses me out. The Lords of Silence did nothing to change my perspective, and yet, Chris Wraight captivated me with his Lords of Silence. It was gross, it was funny, it was intriguing, and it brought me a whole new view on the Traitor Marines in general. I'm eagerly awaiting a sequel, because there's no way we're done with Vorx's story. I'm not done with Vorx's story."

Jay, Tabletop Battles and the Warhammer Wordforge: "With The Lords of Silence, Wraight helped rewrite the book on the Death Guard similar to how Aaron Dembski-Bowden with the Night Lords. These were hardly one-note rot fiends, but rather deeply developed characters that balanced their physical horror with a pious and philosophical perspective. Beyond being a rightly-deserved fan favorite, the book helped expand the way the Traitor Legions are understood and portrayed."

Jay, Tabletop Battles and the Warhammer Wordforge: "The Magos not only served as a sort of connective bridge between his trio of Inquisition trilogies, but it stood as a terrific story in its own right. A detective tale with those elements of cosmic horror and Imperial intrigue that Abnett has mastered over the course of his career, it showcased one of the canon's legends at the top of his game. The original Eisenhorn trilogy was told in the first person, and by switching to the third-person here Abnett forced readers to reckon with Gregor Eisenhorn not as how he saw himself, but how the rest of the universe did."

Jay, Tabletop Battles and the Warhammer Wordforge: "Throughout the Readers' Hall of Fame project, we've typically seen the first book of a trilogy stand in for the entire series when being assessed for Hall inclusion. Because the middle book is built off the first one, the bar is higher. But sometimes that middle book manages to stand on its own feet too, as is the case with Guy Haley's Plague War. There weren't a lot of books that brought the lore "forward" like this, but Haley rose to the challenge with some of the most memorable Primarch confrontation writing we've seen to date. And given that we're at the tail end of the Horus Heresy, that's saying something!"

Jay, Tabletop Battles and the Warhammer Wordforge: "Though their adventures began in the novella City of Secrets, this was the first novel appearance of gritty duo Armand Callis and Hanniver Toll. While they'd go on to return in 2024's Callis & Toll by David Annandale, Horth's swashbuckling, pulpy adventure through the jungles of Ghur was filled with terrific action and characterization. A story on an "ordinary" human scale (much as Imperial Guard stories tend to be in the 40K setting), this one was a standout success for early Age of Sigmar fiction."

Gus, Black Library Subreddit: "Well deserved. This is Callis and Toll’s first full length adventure after meeting in City of Secrets. What’s cool about this one is we get to travel the Realm of Beasts, rather than being stuck in and around Excelsis. Nick North understands the setting of Age of Sigmar well and writes a great catch me if you can/treasure hunting adventure where we get to see a growing friendship between the titular characters that personally endeared me to them and made me a lifelong fan. Add an elf tomb raider and you get one of the best adventure novels in the Black Library.

There’s pirates- Khorn Pirates!!! And Seraphon!!! And Kharadron!!! Some of the character interactions are hilarious. Truly a deserving hall of famer."

Jay, Tabletop Battles and the Warhammer Wordforge: "As the end of the Heresy approaches the era of the Siege of Terra, John French delivered a masterpiece ensemble work that pulled together a number of different narrative threads. A story with tremendous narrative weight as well as French's attention to psychological detail, there's a reason it's the final book in the ongoing Horus Heresy Saga reprint series."

Aasa Timonen, The Warhammer Scholar: "Soul Wars is one of those Black Library books that were published as tie-in for the then current new edition of the game, sharing name with the 2nd Edition starter set for Age of Sigmar. This does not diminish the book in slightest - in fact, it is one of the best introduction books into the Mortal Realms. Josh Reynolds makes the immortal Stormcast Eternals feel achingly human with their doubts, fears, and lingering memories of what they used to be. He does that while also writing a thrilling siege story which starts as massive clash of armies, and ends as almost private and personal fight over the soul of a one person."

JordanSorcery: "The early years of the Age of Sigmar featured plenty of controversial moments, especially with regards to the lore of the Mortal Realms. It’s only mildly contentious to suggest that it was not until Black Library’s fiction writers had been given room to explore that the shape of this new universe, and a strong narrative thread, really began to emerge.

"Published in 2018 Josh Reynolds’ Soul Wars might be amongst the first almost universally agreed upon successes in the setting. A story centred on the machinations of everyone’s favourite mean-spirited former silent movie star ready for his close-up, Nagash, and his long brewing war against chopped unc Sigmar, Soul Wars would introduce a completely new cast of characters and put them through their paces in dynamic fashion. Fortunately though it wasn’t just mindless action, instead offering insights into the rival powers at play in this war of gods and monsters.

"For many this was the strongest outing in the setting since Reynolds’ own early work in the Realm Gate Wars. It put a deeper, darker spin on the battles that would follow in the game itself, and set the stage for what is still one of the most memorable conflicts in Age of Sigmar’s real-time history."

Gus, Black Library Subreddit: "Probably one of the best Black Library novels. ADB gave us a different kind of Space Marine tale, one told from a human perspective that really drives down how far from base line humanity the emperor’s angels are, and at the same time, how much humanity they have in them. I know, sounds confusing, but believe me when I say, it’s what makes this book so brilliant. It is a very intense, very graphic, very emotional novel. For me it was one of those that have you staring at the wall in silence after putting the book down.

"Dembski-Bowden always does a great job of writing interactions between humans and Astartes, and showing the emotional and psychological toll of the scenarios where these two groups must bands together to avoid annihilation. The book also continues the story of the Celestial Lions from ADB’s novella Blood and Fire, itself a follow up to Grimaldus’s story from Helsreach."

Jay, Tabletop Battles and the Warhammer Wordforge: "While Dembski-Bowden has a strong reputation for writing sympathetically deep portrayals of the Traitor Legions, here he gives us his take on a Loyalist one- with a twist. With the Cicatrix Maledictum rending the universe in twain, the Emperor's Spears must discover what it means to be a light in the darkness, when you don't know how much light is even left. Dembski-Bowden's emotional resonance and masterful worldbuilding are on full display here, with one of the era's truly outstanding offerings."

Congratulations to all the worthy winners, and especially first-time winner Nick Horth!
