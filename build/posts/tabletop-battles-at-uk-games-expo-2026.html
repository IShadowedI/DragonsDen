Tabletop Battles at UK Games Expo 2026

June 19th, 2026 via Goonhammer
We recently sent Mike over to check out what was happening at the 2026 UK Games Expo, wandering the wasteland with Modiphius and taking a look at some upcoming releases. We’d like to thank the UK Games Expo team for providing a press pass as part of this.

In case you’re not familiar with UK Games Expo, it’s one of the world's largest tabletop gaming conventions, covering everything in the hobby from mead to drink while playing D&D right through to the latest releases from the industries largest publishers. They hit over 87000 attendees over the weekend this year, with people coming from across the globe to embrace and celebrate the hobby.

This was my first time being on assignment at UKGE, and while I was going to be in attendance for longer than my first trip to UKGE, I still wasn’t going to be there for the full event unfortunately. I did however make the most of my time there, catching up with friends, family, and other members of the Tabletop Battles team.

Lining up to collect my lanyard gave me a chance to have a chat with another attendee who was meeting up with his virtual D&D group in a rare chance to get everyone together. This might be one of my favourite things about tournaments and expos; it’s an opportunity to catch up with friends immersed in the hobby you all love.

Award Show and Preview

I will say the layout for this one was interesting, and I had expected them to make use of the stage which was central in the room. It was only once I’d taken my seat next to Alex and Wingshan of Warp Miniatures and Twisted Spire that I realised I was half a room away from the award area. Knowing I had a hectic weekend ahead of me, I took the chance to relax a little and just support the winners of the awards, including Warp Miniatures and Forbidden Psalm.

Loading...

The preview was a great chance to get a taste for what the expo itself held, and offered an opportunity to chat with publishers and developers in a slightly more relaxed setting. I know many of the exhibitors had come straight across from building their stands in the main event area to then showcase their games for the preview event, and they were all very well composed and excited for the weekend. Aside from catching up with friends from Atikin Games, the highlight for me was finding Willow of Neon Rot. A fluorescent wizard with games inspired by eldritch abominations and the manga of Junji Ito, he took me through his catalogue which ranged from a solo journaling experience where you take the role of an archaeologist unearthing ancient horrors in the early 20th century right through to Meat Kaiju, a 2-4 player card battler. The game that had me sit down for a chat was Fukuzatsu or “what if you could play a physical game of Balatro with your friends using a tarot deck”.

New Adventures in the Wasteland

Friday morning was an early start as I had a full schedule ahead of me and my first stop was the Bandai Namco area to check out Gundam Assemble, and take part in the paint and take. You can check out our initial thoughts on Gundam Assemble here. Bandai had set up an array of demo tables, a painting area, and stores from around the country had provided painted Zakus as part of the display. I’m excited to see what people do with greater access to even smaller scale Gundam models, and I’m looking forward to getting more paint on my own.

After getting some paint on a Zaku, and a couple of quick conversations to book on to TCG demos later in the day, I made my way over to meet Evie Moriarty (she/her), Lead Game Designer at Modiphius Entertainment. She had very kindly agreed to an interview so we could talk all things Wasteland Warfare Second Edition and Wolfenstein. Evie was obviously excited to talk about Wasteland Warfare, and ahead of our interview I’d sourced questions from across our team.

Which parts of the Fallout universe does Wasteland Warfare 2 best capture?

Stepping out of the vault and picking a destination on the horizon. Venturing across the wasteland, discovering more of the map and narrative locations. That initial experience from the video games, the magic of moving from prologue to the first act, was hard to capture but Evie feels she’s achieved it.

The team took lots of cues from solo RPGs in working towards this, along with the map system from Fallout 1 and 2. The campaign map is procedurally generated, with quests laced through as you explore locations during your adventures. Some of these quests may result in chains, which flow around the campaign map.

UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

What do you see as the largest improvement between editions?

Even fans of the first edition recognised there were challenges, the game wasn’t designed with a seven year life span in mind, and as expansions layered additional complexity the number of tokens and cards became unwieldy.

The goal with the new edition was to capture everything people loved, while sanding off the rough edges and addressing some of the pain points that stopped people from continuing to play after picking up the starter box.

The second edition is an exercise in refinement and clarity, allowing new and returning players to explore the Fallout universe without being over-encumbered.

What was the largest challenge in realising that improvement?

Understanding what the game was supposed to be. Finding what the core proposition should be, and then maintaining the freedom of expression that players loved. Maintaining the freedom of expression which players loved from the first edition was a real challenge.

What was the one thing about the previous edition that bothered you most?

The overall accessibility and on ramp for going from being a new player to a long term player. The first edition had become overwhelming, with a vast array of choices and boxes to understand and potentially buy.

On a personal note, Evie shared that she was unhappy with the distribution of the values on white dice. It made the game hard to predict with spiky results distributions.

UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

What were the teams favourite and least favourite moments during playtesting?

Evie shared she hadn’t play tested as much as some of the other devs in the team. Most members of the team have played hundreds of hours through development.

The best moments of testing were made up by the story sharing that happened after a campaign. A wanderer might head into a mission thinking it was going to be some easy caps and maybe some nice salvage, only to discover an angry Super Mutant and his trusty hound.

How will that sharing be encouraged and facilitated?

Modiphius plan to have an app ready day one to track the campaign and warband, with the possibility for joint campaigns with shared progress. They’ll also be encouraging players to share progress in their official discord, and via social media.

And the least favourite moments?

“The Day of Crushing Defeats” or always having to pick the worst option. Having to make deliberately bad choices just to see what happens and make sure the game remains balanced doesn’t really make for a fun experience, but it’s a necessary one. The goal of this type of testing is to ensure that even if a player hits an all time run of bad luck, they can still have a somewhat enjoyable experience.

Our conversation moved onto something a little less structured as Evie started to share some of the core mechanics of Wasteland Warfare with me. When players move across their map it is done in cardinal directions, and this determines which board edge you arrive from for your encounters. When you leave an encounter, the board edge also determines your direction of travel.

During encounters, you’ll use an innovative branching decision-tree system (what used to be called "AI") to the play the opposing force, with a clean set of tables defining how the enemy interacts with the player and their surroundings. Certain actions like movement will increase a player's priority, and directly attacking an enemy generates threat. Enemies then select a target based on priority and threat, spending threat on their actions for the turn. Broken into six types, each enemy has a behaviour table that defines the actions available to them. Cunning use of distractions by the player to generate priority in other areas of the table can buy vital breathing room or offer a chance to set up for a punishing attack.

During key moments of your campaign you’ll encounter scripted enemy events. For these types of encounters, the events defined for them manipulate enemy priority and threat levels to influence behaviours. There’s enough here for players to still feel like they can outwit enemies, without things becoming trivial.

If all of this has piqued your interest in Wasteland Warfare Second Edition, there are going to be two starters available at launch:

  • Into the Wasteland (£50) which is aimed at existing players and collectors. It’ll contain tokens, dice, terrain, and a Hero/Survivor sprue that allows for a couple different versions of your hero as they progress. It’ll also include a solo hero mode, for fans of the Lone Wanderer perk.
  • Warband (£120) will contain hard plastic minis, tape measure, along with the tokens, cards, dice, and terrain you’ll find in the Into the Wasteland set. Enemies from the box will include a Super Mutant Ravager and Hound in PVC, with resin versions available at a later date.
UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

The foundational experience for the new edition is built around the four core factions of Raiders, Survivors, Super Mutants, and the Brotherhood of Steel. The unit reference book will cover all of the major factions and the goal is to have everyone’s existing collections covered in the next 6-9 months.

For those looking to grow their collections Modiphius will be introducing a new series of Encounter Boxes. They’ll contain PVC miniatures which are ready to slot straight into existing campaigns.

Jumping timelines, we moved to a discussion of a different vision of mid century America, as Evie took me through what’s in-store for the Wolfenstein RPG. This RPG adaptation of the franchise is set during the events of New Order and New Colossus, taking place in 1960s Nazi-occupied America. Using an updated version of the 2D20 system, Evie has taken the opportunity to overhaul how it functions for the game. The goal was once again to address what people enjoyed from the system, while understanding what could be improved and changed to offer a new experience.

One of the key areas for improvement was the management of the meta currencies used in core mechanics, which often created hard to manage dice pools. These meta currencies have been pared back for Wolfenstein, with a reworked currency pair for players and GMs.

Players and GMs alike often found it hard to judge and balance when to spend meta currencies in other 2D20 systems, so this has been simplified for Wolfenstein. In the updated 2D20 system, a critical roll for a player will always give them their cool and impactful action, while also allowing them to bank Momentum to spend later. Previously they had to decide between an immediate impactful action, or saving for an unknown future benefit. GMs now have a reworked Threat system as part of their toolbox, with a trio of well-defined effects when Threat is spent. During an encounter a GM can spend Threat to call in reinforcements, worsen a player's failure, or activate the special ability of an enemy. If the GM is already at five Threat and they would go to six, a crisis is automatically triggered. This empties the entire Threat pool and creates a major event or plot twist for the party.

GMs aren’t the only ones with shiny new toys, and during combat players will be able to pick from three stances as they murder their way through hordes of Nazis. Evie and the team have worked to capture the fluid and sometimes chaotic combat of the FPS with Mayhem, Tactical, and Stealth stances available. Mayhem stance encourages multi-target attacks, dual wielding weapons when players need to become Death Incarnate. When there’s a crucial target to be taken down, Tactical stance favours single target attacks, and if for some reason you don’t immediately just want to go loud there’s always Stealth stance which encourages sneaking to get to that perfect position for a vicious opener.

TCG Previews

Miniature games weren’t the only thing on my list to check out at UKGE, and having previously played the PC versions of Palworld and Cyberpunk 2077, I was curious about their Official and Trading Card Game adaptations.

Palworld has had its share of interesting press, and while I haven’t played the game since the Early Access days, the OCG left me curious to revisit. For those unfamiliar, Palworld is an open world crafting and survival game, with a land populated by Pals which the player can capture, farm, and put to work. And of course you can also have them do battle on your behalf. The mechanics on display during the Palworld demo were fairly straight forward. During your turn you spend Souls to deploy Pals, gear, and structures, and select Pals to Battle with the opposing player. Your deployed cards have various abilities reflecting those found in the video game, offering synergies and additional effects. My main game is Magic: The Gathering, and I definitely have a soft spot for card games generally so I might grab another deck or two of this down the road to play with friends between longer sessions of Commander.

UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

In the Cyberpunk 2077 TCG you build a crew of runners, taking on jobs with a goal of seven total jobs to win the game. Jobs are represented by a set of polyhedral dice, and as you take them on you roll to check their value.

Players use runners to either claim jobs from the middle of the play area, or attack opposing exhausted runners. Not every runner can defend, so there’s a tension between taking out the opposing crew and claiming jobs. This might have been a limitation of the demo, but it wasn’t clear how impactful the rolled value of the jobs would be. The game created some fun moments, and the aesthetics are on point for the adaptation. I think I’d want to see a little more of this before going too hard on it. Speaking with the community team, they also have plans for limited formats and I’m very curious to see how those work.

UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

Handmade Games

While my time at Salute was filled with historical, or near historical wargaming, UKGE was an adventure in the weird. Starting with Thursday evening, and a conversation with Willow of Neon Rot. I haven’t had a chance to get their Tarot powered physical version of Balatro to the table yet, but the concept of Fukuzatsu was enough to sell me on the boxed edition which came with a hardback rulebook and extra stickers.

Loading...

Dotted around the expo floor, many stalls had taken to displaying “Handmade, No AI” signs on their stands. Across RPGs, boardgames, and miniatures, designers and publishers were at pains to make it clear when their products were created without the assistance of AI. An A4 tri-fold can’t really capture the nuance of the statement, however the hobby and community itself have broadly pushed back against the proliferation of Generative AI use in the creative aspects of the hobby, this distinction is deliberate, and was made very clear during the Generative AI and Tabletop panel.

Loading...

Chaired by Evie Moriarty (she/her), Lead Designer at Modiphius games, she was joined by Dr Grace Feehan (she/her), Research Assistant in AI Skills, CJ Shearwood (they/them), a lead API developer for a large UK based retailer, Corey Davies (she/her), tabletop activist, and Union Head of the Games Worker branch for the Independent Workers’ of Great Britain, Per Janka (he/him), a freelance artist and designer, and Matt Baca (he/him), Community Manager for My MiniFactory.

UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

The panel came together to discuss the impact and perception of Generative AI usage in the tabletop games industry, covering both the impact to creatives, the supply chain, and consumers. Hiring has become a particular challenge, as contractors will lie about AI usage and CJ shared that they had recently declined a large commission after discovering photos had been upscaled instead of sourced and scanned. Even when Gen AI isn’t used during the creative process, accusations of usage lead to enormous amounts of additional effort to demonstrate the workflow and its absence. The proliferation of its usage has led to a continued suppression of wages within the industry, however it hasn’t impacted or changed the behaviour of businesses who were already reluctant to pay artists or writers. MyMiniFactory has an enormous catalogue of models, and Matt and the team have taken the approach of working with artists and sculptors to certify where AI has been used in any part of the process to celebrate what they’re calling Soul Crafted creations. Consumers have commented that the volume of items on the marketplace now makes it incredibly difficult to find what they want, and the Soul Crafted label goes some way to helping them differentiate. This challenge isn’t unique to the tabletop gaming space, and other platforms like Etsy, have also struggled with a large increase in volume of low quality submissions.

When talking about accessibility and AI usage, there is some confusion amongst consumers with definitions blurred between additional support for an individual with a disability, and making goods and services more accessible via lower costs. This could be the difference between more advanced speech to text software, and generating your character portrait using an AI tool. The argument the panelists put forth is that only one of these applications is ethical.

UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

During the discussion, it became clear that the community was deeply polarised, but also not fully appreciative of the impact and implications of AI usage in the industry. Some of this was driven by a perception that tasks like coding are not creative acts, as broadly the audience were much more resistant to AI usage for art and other game assets, when compared to tasks which they perceived as lacking creativity such as accountancy and other back office functions. Corey highlighted that the impact is still the same, an individual who was previously paid to do the work was being paid less, or had lost their job as a result of these tools.

UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

On the deep polarisation of the community, Grace offered some words of caution regarding AI usage, as an inability to discuss the usage of the tools can lead to shadow AI usage. Shadow usage can encourage unsafe or irresponsible applications of the tool, with no opportunity to discuss what ethical or sustainable usage may look like when people are hiding their usage out of fear or guilt. With many AI models now small enough to run on home computing hardware, they have become ubiquitous, and Matt shared his view that claiming AI usage is bad across the board is not helpful. There are many applications of AI which have been beneficial to society, with decades of use across many industries before the explosion in popularity of generative AI. CJ has been using machine learning in their role for a decade, and also uses it for rapid iterative testing during the design phase of their games, acknowledging that the usage should still be responsible.

UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

When exploring the concept of responsible, and avoidable usage, the panel acknowledged how ubiquitous AI tooling is now. The question of where the line exists on AI tool usage was one discussed frequently at MyMiniFactory by Matt and the team. He shared the example of artists using AI to generate the poses after creating their wireframes, and initially not appreciating that this was still AI usage in their workflow. The artist was asked if it was really required or necessary, as they certainly had the ability to create those wireframes themselves. When considering this type of responsible usage, Grace also shared some of the outcomes of the discussion inspired by the initial survey. Within the tabletop community Generative AI is broadly demonised, but when people are asked to consider the power requirements sustaining YouTubes datacentre, and reflect upon the overall value of the media hosted on those servers the conversation shifts. It goes from being one about Generative AI as something that must be stopped, to a question of what ethical usage of technology looks like across society. For those curious about what this might look like, Grace recommended a lecture from The Alan Turing Institute “Making AI (truly) sustainable - from environmental costs to social impacts”.

UK Games Expo 2026. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

To close out the panel, Evie asked what the ongoing impact looks like for the industry. Matt felt that the genie was out of the bottle, and was unsure what impact future regulation may have on broad usage. His concern was large businesses experimenting with AI DMs, eroding an important part of the tabletop RPG experience by removing the human connection. Doubling down on soul crafted and hand made creative works is going to be the centre of his own approach in the current climate. Per agreed, and while many creatives felt doubtful about the future he also acknowledged how innovative the community are, and hoped that the desire to spend on handmade products would continue to sustain creators. CJ and Corey both shared the view that the current low operating costs of Generative AI were not sustainable, and expected another industry adjustment as costs increased. Grace closed out the answers to this section by highlighting that local usage is possible, and there’s growing interest in smaller models but the most important thing was to continue discussing how usage can be sustainable and responsible, avoiding polarisation.

Everyone on the panel was passionate about games, and about creating, and the current industry climate was not going to stop them from following their passions and sharing them with others.

With the panel concluded my time at UKGE was almost up, and it was another incredible experience. I’m always left revitalised and at the same time exhausted by these events. Burning with a renewed energy and buzzing with ideas for projects and games to play. Continuing to share that energy, and my own passion for the hobby isn’t going to stop anytime soon and I’m already looking forward to seeing many of the same faces again at the next convention.
