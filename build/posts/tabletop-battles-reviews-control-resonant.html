Tabletop Battles Reviews: Control Resonant

October 3rd, 2026 via Goonhammer
This review will discuss mild thematic spoilers for Control Resonant.



Remedy Entertainment has been a studio with a strange but now somewhat predictable lifecycle: The great hit, first Max Payne (we’ll count the first two Max Payne games together here, they were released within two years of each other and form a single story) and then nearly two decades later, Control. Then, a niche, ambitious single-player follow-up project -- both times, the Alan Wake games. Remedy would get into strange cross-brand IP boondoggles with outside publishers -- Quantum Break in 2016 with Microsoft Games, for whom things have not improved; CrossfireX in 2022 with Smilegate Entertainment, as the ringers brought in to do the campaign for a failed attempt to bring South Korea’s biggest multiplayer shooter west. Both are remembered poorly when they’re remembered at all. (Every now and again they make a Death Rally game; we’re overdue for another one of those.) That brings us back to the possible start of a third cycle, or its breaking: Control Resonant. I think it’s a masterpiece, but I also thought Alan Wake 2 was a masterpiece, and the market was significantly softer on that game than I was.

It is, perhaps, a game better suited for wide audiences than Alan Wake 2. That game has a couple things working against it -- or for it, if you’re a long time fan of the real sicko Remedy shared universe stuff. First, it’s a survival horror game; those titles build very passionate audiences, but not huge ones unless you’re a brand name like Resident Evil or…no, Silent Hill is fairly niche these days; it’s just Resident Evil now. Second, in addition to heavily referencing the world and characters of Control, Alan Wake 2 is a crosswise stealth sequel-continuation to two games (Max Payne and Quantum Break) that Remedy no longer had the IP rights to, having to rename all the characters they wanted to bring over -- Martin Hatch becoming Warlin Door, Max Payne becoming Alex Casey, and so on; Saga Anderson was completely recast between her appearance in a Quantum Break teaser and when she resurfaced as the main playable character in Alan Wake 2. Heavily invested Remedy fans are fine with and excited by all this; frankly, most of the changes work out for the better. “Alex Casey” is more suitably generic for the kind of airport horror-thrillers that Alan writes than the knowingly, ain't-I-a-stinker goofy “Max Payne,” and “Mr. Door” is much more forward-facingly ominous than “Mr. Hatch.” For those with less investment, it was a whole lot of multiverse crap to be throwing at the player at once. “Remedy is doing their own Dark Tower!” is only interesting to people who are already in the room.



Control Resonant, by contrast, is a direct sequel to Control and only Control. Yes, there are still a few spots out there in the world where a light starts to flicker and the shadows darken and it’s time for Alan Wake to get his shit in, but the tie-in material is relegated to easter eggs, at least through the first half of the game. More importantly, Control Resonant is a melee-and-superpowers third person character action game of the specific kind we used to get a lot of back on the PlayStation 3 -- it’s like someone threw Gravity Rush, Infamous, and DmC Devil May Cry (the Ninja Theory one that Alex Garland did script supervision on, where you fought your way through energy drink factories and a barely-disguised Fox News to defeat Demon King Jeff Bezos) in a blender, and then filtered out all the Iraq-War-Three-Doors-Down-music-video America particles from its writing and protagonist. It is, in short, much easier to pick up and play even than its predecessor, which was much slower and more cumbersome to move around in, with the tradeoff that you had a gun.

To the extent that I have mixed feelings about the game, it’s about the combat -- or specifically, expectations about the combat. Max Payne and Max Payne 2 (essentially identical in terms of gameplay) are the only titles Remedy has ever made where the combat gameplay loop itself carries the experience. (I haven’t played Quantum Break, but I’ve seen someone play Quantum Break, and I’m fairly confident about including it in this blanket statement.) Even in the original Control, I found fighting to be the third-most compelling thing I could be doing with my time; it wasn’t bad -- it was often quite fun -- but exploring the space and doing little puzzles to learn about the world were the main things that drew me to Control, as with every Remedy title since the Max Payne games. While I think the combat feels much better in Resonant, it is still the third-most compelling thing to me about the game, healthily outpaced by traversal/exploration and learning about the world they’ve designed, whether that’s through huge story setpieces or the ever-present little puzzles.



One thing which definitely informs why it feels like a love letter to early 2010s mainstream character action gaming and possibly informs why some players are bouncing off the combat system for reasons they have trouble articulating: This game has no default parry. There is a Shield power you can use in combat -- personally I’ve never bothered; I never used that power in the first Control either -- but that’s not the same thing. LB on your controller is your interact button, rather than what it’s come to do in basically every game like this since Dark Souls design began to rule the roost. All you’re given is a dodge button, and the point of perfect dodging in the combat system is to apply status effects and buffs to make your return offense done through the regular combat system more powerful, rather than giving you a direct riposte.

The combat can be quite easy if left on the default settings; when I die, I generally die doing runs in the arcade cabinets that return from the first Control as optional mastery challenges. Those cabinets will remind you that no, these combat systems can in fact be quite lethal; by default, they're just tuned pretty low. In a way, this is pretty representative of main character Dylan Faden; the ease with which he's able to hurt people is both why the FBC needs him and why he terrifies them. Combat balance in Control Resonant both indulges the power fantasy inherent in the genre -- every time they throw red barriers over the doors out of the room, it’s the Hiss that are, in fact, trapped in there with you -- and permits the player to kind of cruise through most of the action if they pay even cursory attention to how status effects like Burn and Mold work. (You can change this in the perhaps-deceptively-named Assist Mode menu under the Gameplay tab in Options; yes, this is where the cheats are, and there is literally a “turn on god mode” toggle here, but it’s also the place where you can make the enemies smarter and more aggressive.)



This brings us to Dylan himself, the subject I expected to be the most controversial thing about the game and am pleasantly surprised to find that, once people actually play it, they’re mostly cool with. I wasn’t as immediately and stridently against (temporarily?) moving on from Jesse as the franchise’s protagonist as some, mainly because I very much enjoyed Sean Durrie’s performance as Hiss Dylan in the first game when I replayed it over the summer and I figured that Remedy had earned some buy in from me; with Jesse in Control and Saga being the actual main character of Alan Wake 2, it wasn’t like they were running from female protagonists. There was, I figured, something going on here. The actual execution of Protagonist Dylan has exceeded all my expectations (and, anecdotally, those of my friend group, who were much more stridently pro-Jesse than I was), mainly by making him nothing like both the sinister menace from the first game and, more importantly, consciously rejecting in both writing and performance the Big Budget Video Game Coolguy Male Action Protagonist character voice that's developed over recent years, primarily but not exclusively in the work of Nolan North and Troy Baker. This game might not be an outright failure if Dylan were running around hitting one-liners and smirking his way through conversations with people who have deeply hurt him (and who he in return, and more recently, deeply hurt right back), but it would certainly be worse.

It also wouldn’t really make any sense. This is a guy who was kidnapped when he was ten after witnessing the violent deaths of everyone he’d ever known (except his sister), raised in a secret federal black site by an incredibly unethical and abusive scientist who disliked children and was just winging it the entire time, then kept in solitary confinement once that scientist got bored and afraid of what he’d misparented into existence. And all that was before the extradimensional intruder slipped in and turned him into the Evil Dead version of himself. He shouldn’t be throwing around jokes! He should be precisely what he is in Control Resonant: A victim of emotional and social arrested development who (depending on the answers you choose in dialogue) swings between kicked-puppy cringing, smarmy petulance, and disarming earnestness. They’re not afraid to lay it on thick to make a point: One of the little activities you can do out in the world is constructing make-believe dioramas of things for Dylan to sketch in his notebook, all of which are things ten or twelve-year-old Dylan really wanted to do, but never got the chance. I appreciate the sketches themselves here, too -- a lot of games turn their protagonists into incredibly talented scratch and margin illustrators because they’ve got a bunch of incredibly talented concept artists on staff and it’s a good way to use their talents in the actual work; it’s how you get the minor character detail that Red Dead Redemption 2’s Arthur Morgan is one of the most important naturalists of his generation (or would be, if science recovered his notebooks of incredibly skilled flora and fauna drawings). Dylan’s sketches, on the other hand, are clearly done to communicate emotion while being the work of an amateur.



The main pop culture influence on Control Resonant, then, was completely unanticipated going in but perfectly obvious once you see it in action: Evangelion. Parts of this game are as much a love letter to it as parts of Alan Wake were to Twin Peaks. Once the concept of Dylan as a physically but not emotionally older Shinji Ikari enters your head, you’ll see it in a lot of the decisions they make about the character. He’s got his own quasi-Gendo in Dr. Casper Darling; he’s the reluctant and inferior second choice to pilot a living weapon to a favored daughter who is no longer available. (It’s very important that you understand this is the only way in which Jesse is like Rei.) Remedy is so intent on you getting the parallels between the FBC and NERV that they give the FBC front company Verconn a spoofed NERV logo which they prominently guide your eye to within the first hour of gameplay, and plot developments around the Resonants and what the FBC wants to do with them will eventually click, “Oh, yeah, they’re the Angels here,” into place. We can kind of stretch and make Zoe the game’s Misato? Emily Pope is a very surface level Ritsuko. The direct comps there are less important than the number of important people in his life who are women, and how they stand in contrast to his father figure. Sadly no Kaworu analogue has shown up yet, at least not as far as I’ve played. They’re not actually just doing Evangelion in New York, obviously, but they’re wearing their influences on their sleeve.

The last really big picture thing I have to say in Resonant’s favor is: This is a game about big, big setpieces in service of pure audiovisual spectacle, and they’ve knocked every single one of these out of the park. In the abstract I’ve gotten a bit concerned they might be overdoing the “music on top of a big gameplay traversal moment” formula that made Control's Ashtray Maze so successful (Alan Wake 2 had one as well which didn’t really work for me much at all); they’ve already gone to that well three times in my playthrough and I suspect, given the size of the Zoe’s Mixtape section in the Files, they’ll be going to it three more. So far all them have worked, though, especially the most recent one; there is a sequence in this game that in my opinion puts the Ashtray Maze to shame, that is so good in its combination of visuals, gameplay, and audio design -- not just the music, but how it’s deployed, what’s happening over top of it, how it exists in the game space diegetically -- that after it was done, I had to put the game down for a little bit and take a little walk before coming back and, you know, poking around for health upgrades for another hour or two.



I still have a ways to go in the game; this review would have been much different if I’d started to write it at the 15 hour mark in my save file instead of around the 25 hour mark. I wasn’t kidding when I said I love just running around the game world doing minor sidequest stuff, and that there’s a lot of that stuff to do. There’s probably around 10 hours of main story content in here if you stay on the plot-critical path. But I love finding evil stunlock cameras, locating lost toys for eerily-unbothered rooftop dogs, finding a secret organization’s bunkers hidden behind switch puzzles across the city, and otherwise doing all the non-essential content. So for a player like me, Remedy has delivered their best Remedy experience yet.

Final Verdict: I preordered this game without really paying attention to what it cost, which is not something I do more than once every couple years; I am obviously very high on the title and was predisposed to be. One of the target audiences for this review is people who love the kind of stuff Remedy does in their games but are skeptical of the move away from Jesse; the main takeaway there for me is that I think Control Resonant will win you back. The game runs like a dream on my PC, but it had better given I have a 4070 TI Super in there, so your mileage may vary; that said the Northlight engine, which they’ve used for Control and Alan Wake 2 previous to this, seems to scale very well. The base game is $60 on Steam and the $10 upsell is for some cosmetics that even if you love playing dress-up you’ll probably find non-essential (it’s actually a pretty good bit to throw the NPC Office Guy haircut in the deluxe pack); I find it a very worthwhile use of that amount of money for this amount of fun.
