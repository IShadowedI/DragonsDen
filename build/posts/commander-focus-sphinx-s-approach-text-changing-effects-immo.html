Commander Focus: Sphinx's Approach, Text-Changing Effects, & Immortus, Master of Eternity

September 22nd, 2026 via Goonhammer

Folks, they got me. I never thought I’d be extolling the virtues of a card ‘you can play any number of copies’ of, but here I am, reporting in after having purchased all pieces necessary for paper aside from those arriving in Reality Fracture. The culprit in question? Sphinx’s Approach, a color-shifted version of Dragon’s Approach appearing at Common in Magic’s newest set. As a bit of background, I tend not to enjoy cards like the Approach—historically, they homogenize your list and tend to have a fairly strict power ceiling, with the best thing they can do being the tutoring of some powerful Creature you’d have preferred to Poly/Scramble into. A lot of the panache of these cards is stripped away when you realize they’re just worse Polymorphs with harder deckbuilding restrictions, and at first glance Sphinx’s Approach appears to be of a similar kind. You’d need your targets to make the fact you can play any number of the card matter, and moreover, want to play more than one potential Poly option in a desired order. A wide enough pool, with a specific tutor chain, and ideally alongside a Commander that supplements the playstyle in a truly unique way. Done, done, and most definitely done. I mentioned it during our review of Marvel Super Heroes, but I’ve had my eye on Immortus, Master of Eternity since he was first previewed, and Sphinx’s Approach not only makes him worth exploring, but vice versa as well. You’ve probably seen that Sphinx of the Chimes does some heinous stuff in a list like this, but let’s talk about the card that pushed me to spring for this list in paper: Exchange of Words.

"A deck can have any number of cards named Sphinx's Approach."

First, though, a bit of background. For a deck based around what I’ll term ‘Basic nonlands’, like Sphinx’s Approach, a few considerations must be made: How many are you playing, to what end, and what can you do to squeeze the most from your remaining limited slots? To answer the first question, we’ve got to jump into the hypergeometric calculator. What a calculator like this does is measure the rate at which you’ll see a given card(s) in a certain number of cards drawn or milled, based on how many like cards that are played in your list; as an example, we need to see 5 copies of Sphinx’s Approach to get the festivities going, and if we play 29 copies, we’ll be able to see 5 or more Approaches in our first 20 cards 75% of the time. If that jumps up to 39 copies, that same 75% chance to see 5+ Approaches exists in our first 15 cards, instead. To that end, most lists playing Basic nonlands settle at around ~35 copies of their chosen build-around, give or take. It’s enough to the extent that you’ll be seeing copies throughout the game, and hit key metrics like an 82% chance to resolve a Ripple trigger successfully from Thrumming Stone (a key card for these strategies). Sphinx’s Approach is different, though, because unlike Dragon’s Approach or Slime Against Humanity, the card itself pushes you to see more copies, by being a double-pip Quick Study. The more Sphinx’s Approaches that you cast, the more likely you are to see additional copies, and so on—this means that, unlike its peers, we’re actually incentivized to cast these Spells rather than just mill or Ripple through a deck stuffed full of redundant pieces.

Appropriate, with JoJo Fridays returning this week.

Enter Immortus. Coming in at 4 mana, he has the ability to tap for {U} equal to the number of cards you’ve drawn in a given turn, with the caveat it can only be spent to cast noncreature Spells. We’ll talk about his Power-Up later, when we start covering win conditions, but suffice to say this gentleman was tailor-made for Sphinx’s Approach. Unlike its peers, the Blue Approach is double-pip, meaning it’s far less easy to cheat on its cost compared to something like Dragon’s Approach. Immortus, however, makes Blue mana. There are also 29-39 cards in your deck which draw cards, and at a rate of 3 mana per 2 cards alongside your draw during the draw step, Immortus at his very worst allows one Sphinx’s Approach to pay for the next. That’s the power floor, and it’s a decent one, but where things get silly is when you’ve drawn 4-8 cards in a given turn, and start playing cards like Cerulean Wisps to repeatedly add more and more mana. Immortus is, between his floor and ceiling (power...room?) a Storm Commander, and a reasonably compelling one! Especially if you have any sort of discount on your Approaches, chaining between draw spells and untap effects can tear through your Library, and get the right pieces in hand to pop off. At his utmost, however, the ceiling of the strategy, Immortus is a win condition. It’s time to talk about the Sphinges we’re Approaching.

Riddle Me This

The three Sphinx cards we’ve included in this deck are not the ones which lead to any sort of deterministic endgame; that is possible, by combining Eligeth, Crossroads Augur, Prognostic Sphinx, & Curator of Mysteries, in order to discard and draw through your entire Library purely via Sphinges. If you play Eligeth in the Command Zone, alongside Kydele, Chosen of Kruphix, you get a more deterministic endgame than with Immortus, in exchange for not adding colored mana. It’s a notable tradeoff, but the real defining factor in my choosing Immortus is the fact that we don’t need to play any bad Sphinx Creatures. Sorry Prognostic Sphinx, but you don’t make the cut. Instead, we’re on the trio of Sphinx of the Chimes, Sphinx of the Second Sun, & Consecrated Sphinx—these three do everything, and each makes a compelling case to be the option you tutor for after resolving your fifth Approach. In order of your usual access chain, let’s start with Chimes: This card has been rightfully hyped for its interaction with the Approach, being able to pitch two and draw 4, effectively shortcutting 6 mana worth of spells. You do of course need two Approaches in hand to make this work, which is where our handy dandy hypergeometric calculator once again comes into play. At our current count of 29 copies, there’s a ~33% chance to draw 2+ Approaches out of our 4 cards, allowing us to continue the chain. On 39, this goes up to ~51%, although Chimes as your sole wincon would want even more. That said, filling your deck with an obnoxious amount of Quick Studys is a recipe for a list you play once and put down, which is why Sphinx of the Chimes is more our means than our end. That brings us to Sphinx #2, Sphinx of the Second Sun: Coming in at a whopping {6}{U}{U}, this behemoth gives us an additional beginning step after our combat step (meaning untap, upkeep, & draw). We’re huge fans of this card for a number of reasons, least of which is the fact that it’s one of the only Sphinges to untap our Commander, usually netting 4+ mana and a card in hand. Crucially, it’s worth reminding ourselves that summoning sickness doesn’t fall off at Upkeep and instead occurs at start of turn, which means Second Sun doesn’t solve all our problems when rebuilding. That mana cost though, it does do something special—our strategy’s core win condition hinges around some niche interactions with Power-Up, and a card from Unfinity that most folks have forgotten about, Exchange of Words.

Draw like an Egyptian.

Exchange is one of only two Commander-legal cards to switch textboxes, and the only one that doesn’t make your opponents tear out their hair. When it enters, similar to an O-Ring effect, you swap the textboxes of two target Creatures for as long as it’s on the battlefield; this can in theory be used to rob opposing Commanders of their text, but we quite like the text on our Creatures. So much so, in fact, that this deck’s primary win condition is swapping the textboxes between our Commander, and Sphinx of the Second Sun. Under the hood, Exchange uses Layer 3, text-changing effects, where each targeted Creature loses their rules text, and gains the others' abilities. Copiable values aren't transferred, it's strictly what's written, and moreover this effect persists even if Exchange itself loses its abilities while on the board. This is all covered in the Unfinity Release Notes, but one thing to keep in mind is that the text boxes aren't literally swapping, but instead that new instances of each target's text overwrite the other's after the trigger resolves. This requires a bit of explanation, when it comes to Power-Up as a mechanic—it’s a deeply odd one, because the ability’s cost reduction fluctuates with the card it appears on, with colored pips beyond the required cost overflowing into the colorless reduction.

I covered this set of interactions in full on a Puzzlebox a week back, but in short, Immortus’ massive {5}{U}{U} Power-Up at best costs {3} on himself, but can cost a cool {0} if possessed by a Creature with a printed mana cost of {5}{U}{U} or higher. Unlike a rule like Emerge, which references mana value, Power-Up is all about mana cost (see CR702.193), which means it's a rare ability that scales with the printed cost of the card it appears on. Go figure, Sphinx of the Second Sun has a cost higher than the price on this Power-Up. While yes, it costs 3 mana to transfer texts by casting Exchange (the same as we’d pay with an Immortus that’d entered in the same turn), given it’s a noncreature Spell we can use Immortus’ mana to pay for it. Moreover, if we were to, say, blink Exchange itself and ‘print’ a new textbox each time it entered, by way of a Displacer Kitten flicker, we would be able to make use of a free Timetwister with every noncreature cast. That in turn leads to Immortus producing 7 more Blue mana per tap, or even more, if we have our final Sphinx onboard: Consecrated Sphinx. I don’t need to tell you Consphinx is good—it’s a Game Changer for a reason—but here it makes our Immortus Power-Up dig through half of our Library at a time. We draw 7, as does each opponent...leading to us drawing an additional 42 cards, for a total of 49. Seeing half our Library at a time means we can start to ‘four horsemen’ Lotus Petal and similar, until we assemble the means to cast Blue Sun’s Zenith 3 times, drawing out each opponent in one go (courtesy of the mana generated by Immortus). It sounds janky, and it’s enabled by way of an Unfinity card that should have perhaps had an acorn stamp, but that’s precisely why it’s cool. There’s absolutely no way this interaction was planned in design, and it’s part of why Immortus is the best Sphinx’s Approach Commander.

Out of Space, Not Time

With the terms of engagement laid out, it now becomes important to talk about the omissions, the cards which didn’t make the cut, but not for lacking of trying. See, in any deck based around a basic nonland, the space not taken up by your buildaround + Lands is far more slim than in other lists, so obvious inclusions like Faerie Mastermind aren’t synergistic enough to be worthwhile, in spite of the fact that they’re objectively good cards. We’re on the bare minimum Sphinx’s Approaches, at 29 out of likely up to 39, and 32 Lands (counting MDFCs). Those are greedy ratios helped only by the fact that Approach itself is a hand smoother, and it leaves us at only 39 cards to form the remainder of the list. The next culling of cards came as a recognition that we’re functionally a Storm deck, therefore couldn’t fit tools to play on opposing turns; Eluge, the Shoreless Sea was a shocking casualty, as it seems fantastic here, but while it normally provides 8+ mana per turn cycle, it only supplies 2-4 per turn. Immortus fills that role, and feels better for what we intend to do, but to be clear Eluge in the Command Zone could make an interesting Approach Commander in its own right. Along similar lines to Eluge, basically all of our interaction fell by the wayside, lacking the Game Changer slots for Cyclonic Rift or the best countermagic, and needing to keep the train rolling in terms of untap effects over tools like An Offer You Can’t Refuse & Misdirection, two cards I desperately wanted to slot in. We’re not a fragile list---there’s a ton of ways to give Immortus or our Sphinges Hexproof—but our gameplan is to compare game pace with opponents, and aim to come out on top, rather than interrupt their gameplans. Faith in our linear path of play is paramount, because the way Approach & Immortus works requires you commit full bore to density on things like untap effects. The only ones we’ve cut are those which don’t provide Hexproof, or are too pricy.

Balancing between untaps and Approaches is a nightmare to manage.

Also not appearing in this list are wheels & self-mill, for the most part. These are normally fantastic inclusions for getting your basic nonlands in the graveyard, but here we have such a slim pool of targets that milling one over too early can be disastrous. There are ways of getting them back in the Library, such as with Conjurer’s Bauble or Immortus’ Power-Up, but these aren’t always accessible when building up our board. The one wheel to make it in is actually Arc of Fortune, by way of the brand-new Variable Chaser—this is a second way to repeatably trigger Displacer Kitten, and the mana produced by Immortus immediately outpaces the cost of recasting the Prepared spell to get our Chaser blinked. It isn’t a win condition, as the wheel is optional, but it’s still a fantastic tool if we don’t have a Sphinx in hand, or are pushing into the lategame. Speaking of win conditions, while not technically a victory in of itself, there was no chance we’d go without Narset, Parter of Veils as one of our Game Changers with a Timetwister-esque effect in the Command Zone (mandatory, no less). It’s highly possible that you get to pass around the table with each opponent having but one card in hand, even if your Storm turn doesn’t go fully to plan. Finally, pour one out for the discounters we aren’t on, including the new Geist of Saint Thalia & Dirgur Focusmage; I tended to find that Immortus produced enough mana, if built around, to outpace the discounts when getting down to brass tacks. The Focusmage gets closest, as playing its prepared Braingeyser over Blue Sun’s Zenith seems like a far more efficient path to victory, but there’s not actually enough 5+ mana Instants & Sorceries we’re interested in to turn it on, during the endgame. Buyback & Splice don’t increase the mana value of their respective cards, so Capsize & Everdream are out, so we’re otherwise down to jamming Misdirection or similar into our own Spells...not exactly compelling. If the restriction on Extra Turns were changed, we could just play Nexus of Fate repeatedly on an empty Library, and never actually take the turns (instead Braingeyser-ing people to death), but that worries me in terms of the letter of the law Bracket-wise. If you’re okay with that, swap BSZ for Dirgur, and find room for Nexus. It’s probably better. Hell, just taking infinite turns and Powering Up each turn passing around the ability does get you infinitely large Sphinges & Immortus, just a thought.

Example Decklist: Immortus Chimes In

One card here that’s worthy of calling out is Energy Tap, because while Exchange of Words is the easiest way to enable Power-Up, we can ultimately force it through at full cost by tapping something like our Sphinx of the Chimes with a few Blue mana spare. These Sphinges' mana values are big, and represent a ton of mana we can funnel into hardcasting one from hand (if stuck there) or cards like Thrumming Stone to get the ball rolling. We can even use Energy Tap as a means to recast Immortus, if he dies and we’ve got a Sphinx up. Great card. I’d also like to highlight Bilbo, Thief in the Night as a cool card here, largely for the fact that he’s just a 2 mana rock which occasionally allows us to ‘Flashback’ a Spell at a discount. You’ll see this often in lists going forward, but if you have a 4 mana Ux Commander you want down early, Bilbo is often better for you than something like a Fellwar Stone. He’s a Blue mana dork that only assists in accelerating your Commander at worst, and at best helps a ton with navigating turns where you need one more Spell that’d otherwise be out of reach.

There are also minor tragedies, with the exclusion of Hour of Eternity & Leyline of Transformation being the most depressing. Hour is a way to funnel Immortus mana into Creatures, and can be a stellar way to access a Displacer Kitten you’d milled over during the course of a game. It’s absent, however, because...Immortus’ Power-Up and enough draw power finds those cards again anyway, with no risk of losing them forever to exile. Leyline would be in here, if we took the Dirgur path, because finding any of our key Creatures via Sphinx’s Approach sounds delicious. There is the mild downside of maybe playing too few Approach to cycle back through them in the midgame, removing 5 from our pool of 29 each time, but it still has some cool applications.

Decklists are kept updated, and may change with set releases.

Playing this at Bracket 3 means you have access to Consecrated Sphinx & Narset, two cards which suit our gameplan so well it’d be impossible to go without. That means we’re kinda barred from Bracket 2, where Sphinx’s Approach is more likely used in lists playing the 50+ copies to execute a Chimes win, or play strictly for value. Immortus can do that, sure, but having additional colors is likely worth more when you lack Game Changers. The better question is whether this list can play in Bracket 4, and that’s similarly tough to justify. Quick Study is not a cEDH playable card, even at fringe, and B4 approaches that format in terms of game pace. Sphinx’s Approach is a perfect candidate for Bracket 3, and does plenty of interesting things in the context of that power level. It’s the Goldilocks zone for decks like this.

Putting the 'X' in Sphinges

Harry Conway's promo for Approach is magnificent, and tracking down enough copies is going to be quite the quest.

Immortus has beaten out Monk Gyatso for me as the fastest turnaround between a deck’s concept and its acquisition in paper. Of course, I don’t yet own 29 copies of Sphinx’s Approach, but I’ll be attending the prerelease(s) to snap up any errant copies that show themselves. I’m beyond hyped to get this shown off at LGSes and conventions I attend, least of all for the fact that it’s a Sphinx’s Approach deck. At the outset of Reality Fracture, I expect to see a few of these crop up in my circles, led by such options as The Ur-Sphinx or some discounter, but as is the case with basic nonland strategies, these tend to get culled for being too janky or too linear; each of the options for these cards which require a third of your deck space have a preferred Commander they tend to stick to, after the novelty wears off. For Dragon’s Approach, it’s Florian, and for Persistent Petitioners it’s very obviously Bruvac. I’m confident enough in my testing to make the bold assertion that Immortus is in the company of those illustrious peers, the best option for Sphinx’s Approach decks.

These are still the early days of the card though, and indeed the archetype as a whole. Heck, for the first few hours following the card’s reveal, I had planned out a Kydele/Eligeth version of this article—that ended up performing far worse in testing, and with fewer interesting interactions, hence it being a footnote in our coverage of Immortus. This wasn’t going to be a paper deck for me, however, until around 3:30am the night of the preview, when I was searching for ways to make Blue mana, having found that to be the limiting reagent in Approach. I’d been looking to build something with Immortus at the helm, and even talked about him briefly in the Power-Up Puzzlebox; I had that mechanic on the mind, and then checked the mana values of some prospective Sphinges to tutor out...and then intended to sacrifice Immortus and plant him under Agatha’s Soul Cauldron. Then Quicksilver Elemental. It took some heinous Scryfall syntax to finally reveal Exchange of Words to me as an option (having pretended the only relevant card from Unfinity was Saw in Half), but at that revelation I knew this list was destined for greatness. I’m shrewd about what I bring to paper, with the last one having been Gau, Feral Youth this past April. At time of writing, Immortus has <150 decks. I expect that to change, and fast.

Until next time, don’t forget to go back in time to defeat your morally unscrupulous earlier self.
