Tabletop Battles Reviews: New Warhammer 40,000 Starter Sets

July 11th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Hot on the heels of the Armageddon launch box, Games Workshop announced a number of starter/entry-level products for the new edition. These are the Introductory Set, the Getting Started With boxes, and the full-blown Starter Set. Games Workshop sent us copies of each of these for review - let's take a look at each of them in turn, starting with the Introductory Set.

Warhammer 40,000 Introductory Set

The box contents are:

  • 12 models, six Space Marines and six Orks, all of which are sculpts unique to this set
  • 6 paints and a paintbrush
  • 6 dice
  • 1 playing mat
  • 3 sheets of card terrain
  • 1 range ruler
  • 1 handbook

The set retails at £47.

The twist off models with detailed bases make these a great first start.

The handbook here is entirely bespoke to this set - there's no copy of the core rules or anything. There's a short introductory section, then guidance on building and painting the miniatures included, a brief lore section, and then a set of intro gaming rules. These use simplified unit profiles similar to Age of Sigmar, with fixed wound values rather than using Strength and Toughness comparators.

The book makes it very clear what kind of set this is - it gives clear guidance, with lots of pictures, on absolute basics like "how do you even start painting a miniature." The first training mission in the gaming section sets up the Ork Nob and the Space Marine Lieutenant to fight each other one on one, and shows you the required hit and wound rolls by literally presenting the dice results and telling you which ones are successes and which ones are failures. This is a product you give a small child or someone who has never done any gaming or craft-based hobby before.

See: above mentioned small child.

That is an excellent product, in our view, which Games Workshop has historically not been great at; It's only within the last couple of editions that starters have been anything less than "a giant pile of models and hard plastic terrain and a copy of the core rules," which assumes a level of ability or knowledge that someone completely new to the hobby probably doesn't have. This set is much, much smaller in scope and is stronger as a result. The models are twist-off style and it is actually effective to twist them off - I made a point of doing it and not using clippers at all, and it was fine, good even, and while you would probably want a hobby knife to clean up a little you don't strictly need one.

Rob: I assembled this with my nine year-old son and he had a pretty easy time cutting and assembling the models. they're good kits, though you want to tackle them one at a time and follow the instructions (difficult for a kid that age).

The card terrain is simple to put together out of the box and is pleasingly aesthetic. It's a welcome return to older starter products which included this kind of simple, quick-to-play terrain. The entire product feels like something you can buy for your kid on Saturday morning and have them playing a game by lunchtime.

If you're an experienced hobbyist there's not a lot here for you except the unique sculpts - which are really nice, and include heavy weapons for the Orks that don't come in Armageddon, so may be worth picking up for that alone. If you're not interested in those, though, you don't need to buy this - but that's fine, it has an intended audience and it's not the veteran hobbyist.

Rob: This was a really easy set to work with and I liked that it came with paints and a brush. The model assembly was probably the best part.

Warhammer 40,000 Starter Set

Jumping straight to the top end of things, this is your meaty, top-end box that has everything you need in it for you and friend to start playing proper Warhammer 40K at Combat Patrol sizes.

It contains:

  • A Combat Patrol force each for Marines and Orks, cut-down versions of what each received in Armageddon. These comprise:

    • Space Marines:

      • Captain
      • Librarian
      • 5x Vanguard Veterans
      • 5x Intercessors
      • Land Speeder
    • Orks:

      • Warboss
      • Weirdboy
      • 20 Boyz
      • 10 Grots
      • Wartrakk
  • A rules handout for each army, providing Army Rules, Datasheets, Stratagems and Enhancements.
  • Terrain! It's the unpainted version of the new official terrain kits that have been previewed on Warhammer Community, totaling half a Strike Force table's worth (and the full amount you need for Combat Patrol. The terrain set is comprised of the following:
    • Four L-shaped Ruins.
    • Four small corner pieces of ruin.
    • Two barricades
    • One electric fence.
    • Two different generators.
    • A set of cable relays that are modular - for Combat Patrol you split them in half, for Strike force they assemble into a single piece.
  • Two double-side boards.
  • The Core Rulebook, with a stylish Ultramarine on the cover instead of a Blood Angel
  • An introductory book with a lore overview of the two factions, assembly instructions, detailed rules walkthroughs and simplified overviews of some thing like Core Stratagems, plus missions to play with the box contents.
  • Rulers and Dice

This rocks in at £185, which isn't cheap, but that's also a tonne of stuff, and it's a very good starter product, in that other than a pair of clippers (which you absolutely need for building the terrain), it contains everything you need to play an actual, genuine game of Warhammer 40k. This is a massive improvement on previous iterations, where you've always been short of something that you need to properly sink your teeth into the game. Even the top-tier Command/Ultimate tiers in 9th/10th didn't really hit this - the forces weren't punching at the same level, and you only got a mat and terrain quantity suitable for half the size of a Combat Patrol game. This box is £50 more expensive than the 10th Edition Ultimate set, but it's a much more complete product, which makes the added cost easier to swallow.

It's also very well thought through - the intro booklet is great, providing a softer introduction to how 40K plays out than just reading through the rulebook does, and there's also a really nice touch with the boards. I was initially worried that things wouldn't quite work without terrain areas, but they've thought of that - on one side, the boards are just a desert pattern, so if you're bringing your own areas use that, but on the other the terrain areas for playing the missions in the book are pre-printed onto them.

That brings us nicely to the terrain, with this set being our first opportunity to get our hands on the new official pieces. It's been moulded in slightly darker-grey plastic than normal, which should improve the aesthetics a bit if you just want to push fit and play with it, but obviously the goal should be to paint it (more on that in a second). Build and push-fit wise, the ruins are great, easy to prep, easy to mould-line, and they helpfully have identifying letters discreetly moulded onto each so you know how to put them together for the official layouts. The other terrain pieces are more of a mixed bag build-wise: the smaller pieces and relays go together fine, but the generators are a bit shakier, and the mould-lines on the cable relays pre-build are vicious.

Then there's painting and...look, it's Games Workshop terrain, it looks lovely, but it is a lot. There are lots of different textures and surfaces to attend to if you want it to look sensible, and even just getting everything prepped, sprayed and washed took me a while.

Pictures taken shortly before a descent into madness.

Once you've done that, you can get a long way with an underpainting drybrush of Stormhost Silver or white followed by Contrast and some detailing, and you can see my attempt at this below:

Credit: Wings

Credit: Wings

Will I keep going and do seven more ruin pieces to fill out a table? I'm certainly going to try, but I strongly suspect I will lose the race to the pre-paints being available. My main defense for not having more of this done is that it has been brutally hot in the UK for a decent chunk of the period we've had these. It's also fair to say that the non-ruin pieces will paint up a lot more quickly - the pain on the ruins comes from that fact that there's a stone substrate which needs something other than just metal and drybrush (at least it does if you are a perfectionist), whereas all the various industrial pieces should be able to be covered more rapidly.

Fools, now I have the high ground and +1 to my BS. Nyahahahah. Credit: Wings

That's my hobby plan for the next few months, and to circle back to where we began - this is easily the best Starter Set GW have ever done for Warhammer 40K, and on balance the higher price tag to get the real experience from day one is worth it. Only real gripe is that it should have a set of clippers in it, because you won't get some of the terrain pieces off the sprue without them.

Getting Started With Orks

This box contains the same stripped-down version of the Ork half of Armageddon (Warboss, Weirdboy, 20 Boyz, 10 gretchin, 1 Wartrakk - no Dakkarig, Painboy, or Big Boss) as the Starter Set. In addition to the models, you also get 10 full-sized pots of Ork Goff-flavoured paints (Ork skin, black, various browns, red, metallics) plus one half-sized Agrax Earthshade pot, a synthetic brush, and a texture spreading tool.

Evil Suns Wartrakk by Craig "MasterSlowPoke" Sniffen

We’ve seen all the models before when they came out with Armageddon, so there’s little need for me to review them here, only to say that they’re as easy to build and look as great as their Armageddon siblings.

The box retails at £105, which places it well in line with the other Combat Patrols, but with the added bonus of a whole bunch of paint and some tools to sweeten the deal even more. There is one rather glaring exclusion though - the box doesn’t contain any clippers or any other way to remove the pieces from the frame, so while it will help you in getting started with Orks, the set isn’t a complete solution for getting started with Warhammer in general.

A page from the Get Started With Orks booklet

The booklet included in the box is nice too, printed in high quality, full-colour and glossy, and with a sturdier cover than the usual box-instructions. The booklet contains lore snippets, beautiful double-page spreads of artwork, building instructions, and a comprehensive painting guide for using the paints in the box. All of which will be very useful for the beginner - even if you’re an old hand, you still get a bunch of paints thrown in gratis to your 11th edition Ork combat patrol; it’s a bit of a no-brainer really.

Getting Started with Space Marines

Similarly to the Ork box, this contains the models for the new Space Marines Combat Patrol: a Captain, a Librarian, a 5 model Intercessor Squad, a 5 model Vanguard Veteran squad, and a Land Speeder. It also has a selection of Ultramarines-themed paints, a brush, and a texture tool.

Crimson Fists Captain - credit: Keewa

There's a booklet in here equivalent to the Ork version which has build instructions, lore snippets, and profiles for your units to play Combat Patrol with them. At £105, it's a good product for what it says on the box i.e. getting started with Space Marines - in many ways much more straightforward and accessible than buying Armageddon or the Starter Set, since everything in here is focused on getting a Marine army going.

Wrap Up

Overall, these new starter sets are some of the best GW has provided in a long time; the introductory kit is firmly the best they've ever done in making 40k accessible to younger and completely inexperienced players, the starter set is the best bang for your buck kit to get you and a friend into an actual game of 40k, and the Getting Started boxes are both wholly complete Combat Patrols that even contain paint to get you going.
