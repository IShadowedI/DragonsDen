Tabletop Battles Reviews: Queen of the Rose Throne, by Gary Kloster

June 6th, 2026 via Goonhammer


Queen of the Rose Throne by Gary Kloster is the follow up novel to 2023’s The Last Volari and the second featuring titular character Nyssa Volari.

Not having read the first novel, it was an interesting experiment as to whether I would be able to pick this book up and still follow. Turns out I could. There were a few bits of the story that it was clear I was missing, but all the key bits were made plain enough and I didn’t feel I was losing out on the story for not having read the first book. At times the book did feel a little exposition heavy, but that did help me catch up, and I don’t think it necessarily would have detracted too much for those who were familiar with what had happened so far.

Nyssa Volari is a (comparatively) young Soulblight vampire of the Kastelai dynasty. Being Kastelai, Nyssa lived most of her second life under the curse of the Crimson Keep. (Basically, Prince Vhordrai pissed Nagash off, and the God of Death cursed him to be eternally chained to the Crimson Keep – a giant, teleporting fortress that bounces around the realms, attracted by sources of death magic. If Vhordrai, or any of his Kastelai progeny, happen to be left outside the castle walls when it vanishes, they suffer horrible and agonizing deaths.)

Prince Vhordrai. Credit: Matthew 'chimp' Ward

Nyssa was one such vampire. Once upon a time, Nyssa and a group of other vampires, including her father and mother, Corsovo and Vasara, were out fighting and sucking blood, when they felt the pang in their bodies telling them that the Crimson Keep was about to move on. They turned and rushed back to its safety, only to find they were too late and the bus had gone without them.

Some of the vampires died instantly; others were subjected to long and agonizing deaths in the days to follow. However, Nyssa, along with a handful of others (including her parents) were spared. For some reason, the curse had passed them by.

Eventually coming to terms with the fact that they were not about to die horrible deaths, they started trying to work out what it means to be a Kastelai without the Crimson Keep and Prince Vordrai. The Kastelai dynasty took great pride in martial honour and constant battle, but they have never been conquerors as they'd never been able to stay in a single place long enough to set down roots. However, now that they were stranded in a single place Nyssa and the others began to realise that as they fought, they sort of had to stick around and rule the places they defeated.

They eventually carved out for themselves a Kingdom among the Broken Plains of Aqshy, subduing the local mortal populations and establishing themselves as rulers on the Rose Throne. I won’t go into the story of the first book… because I haven’t read it… but by the end of it (spoilers, I guess), Nyssa’s parents are dead, and she is the queen.

Well, they’re vampires, so her parents were dead already. But now they're dead dead. Although Corsovo, her father, is not as dead dead as other vampires who get dead dead, because his body remains. Which means he will raise to life, again, at some point. So, in the meantime, Nyssa is sort of keeping the throne warm for him.

Nyssa’s mum, Vasara, also dies dies, but in a different way to Nyssa’s father. Her body is gone, but her soul remains, and now lives in Nyssa’s head, advising her and providing useful plot exposition when needed.

There’s also a cast of other vampires whose full stories are probably detailed in the first book, but only alluded to in this one - and definitely makes me want to go back and fill in my knowledge.

When we pick up the story at the beginning of this book we see Nyssa solidifying her command over the throne against the local nomad tribes, but also stomping out any internal challenges to the throne from within. For example, we’re introduced to the wolf-blooded Leta, who seeks to claim leadership over a part of Nyssa’s realm through one-on-one combat. She is defeated, but does prover herself a worthy fighter in the process and is thus granted permission to become a character in the book by becoming Nyssa’s squire.

It is just at this point, as Nyssa begins to make plans for the expansion of her kingdom, that the Crimson Keep returns after a century of absence. Nyssa is then put in the position of having to choose between her kingdom and her vows to the Crimson Keep. Does she go back to the Keep, as her honour would demand, and essentially become a mere servant there again, or does she remain in the Broken Plains as Queen of the Rose Throne? This dilemma provides most of Nyssa’s motivation and drive throughout the book.

The story also focuses on Culan, a shamanyev of one of the plain’s nomad tribes. He is studying death magic at the tutelage of his abusive father, the shaman Davan, but he’s not very good at it. Something his father likes to remind him of with regular beatings. That is until he comes into possession of a mysterious sentient skull, named Mau, who exerts a strange kind of hold over him, giving him new magic abilities and power like he has never known before. But, of course, at a cost. Mau wraps her corrupting tendrils around Culan’s mind and begins to use him to do her will in a classic ‘easy power comes at a cost’ trope.

Mau’s previous owners, a massive Beasts of Chaos hoard, come looking for the skull and it leads them to Culan’s hometown. At the same time Davan, his abusive father, comes to realise how powerful the trinket is too. It is also this skull that might have brought the Crimson Keep back. Vhordrai believes it might be a useful asset in trying to break Nagash's curse, so sends some of his finest troops to recover it.

Michigan GT display showing a Beasts of Chaos army. Credit: Michael O "Mugginns"

The story is written in first person, from both the perspectives of Nyssa and Culan, which does immediately set it aside somewhat from most other Warhammer books. At times, if I'm honest, it feels to me a little more like an author narrating than a vampire warrior-queen, I’m not sure the voice always ring true, but on the whole I think it works.

I enjoyed Culan, the shamanyev's, perspective most. Often we find ourselves viewing the Warhammer world through the eyes of someone who is truly remarkable – some great warrior, or noble wizard, or resourceful scoundrel. But Culan is thoroughly mediocre. He is not one of the great and the good. He's not even particularly likable. There is no hidden virtue or strength to him; no unrealized potential. So the fear he has to deal with is pretty much the same as every millennial – realising he's just not that special after all.

Mau offers him a quick fix path out of mundanity to power and glory and significance, and it begins to cost him what little he does have. This makes him a relatable character in many ways, a small player in a big game.

Of course, this is well contrasted with Nyssa, who is a bad ass. She has shown herself to be great as a warrior, as a leader, as a strategist. She has created a kingdom for herself and has met challenges with strength. She’s young for a vampire, both in terms of the current length of her second life but also in terms of how old she was when she was sired - normally vampires have a bit more of their first life before being turned. As a result, other vampires can look down on her, thinking she must be a little wet behind the ears, but time and time again she proves her worth.

Having access to Nyssa’s thoughts through the first-person narrative of the book actually also gives us two brains for the price of one, as her mother Vasara’s soul lives within her head. The dialogue between the two can be very interesting at times. In many ways, despite being queen, Nyssa is still a little inexperienced and naïve and looks to her mother’s inner voice for guidance. Especially when the Crimson Keep returns and Nyssa becomes all to aware that she is very unaware of its inner workings.

Despite the Kastelai’s fame for fighting and honour, they are still Soulblight, which means they must also be proficient at manipulation and court politics. Nyssa has proven herself with regards to fighting time and time again, but she really struggles with the mind games and politics of aristocratic vampire life. She has a clear distain for the political wranglings of most other vampires. The wheeling and dealing and power plays that happen behind the closed doors of the Crimson Keep are of very littler interest to her, and she often has to rely on her mother’s advice to navigate some of those dimensions. However, she proves herself shrewd when she needs to be.

That said, it is around the issue of honour that Nyssa faces her most interesting dilemma. Having sworn her allegiance to the Crimson Keep, she knows its reappearance means she must return to it and serve. But She has also sworn her allegiance to her kingdom and her people, so surely this means she must also remain and rule it well. Besides, there is a vast army of beastmen on the doorstep of her kingdom, and as queen she has a responsibility to remain to protect the place.

Nyssa’s inner wranglings with this are the emotional drive of the book, and we get to read it from inside her head. How might she juggle her conflicting obligations? How can her honour remain intact when she is forced to put one oath above another? As a reader, you know there will be some sort of clever twist that will allow her to navigate the dilemma and I found myself reading the book wondering how she was going to resolve it.

But it never came. Personally, I found the end unsatisfying. I don’t feel it gave Nyssa the opportunity to prove herself in any of the ways that matters to a Kastelai; neither in fighting prowess, political acumen nor honour. I do think the antagonist of the story got what he deserved, and watching his arrogance lead to his own downfall was satisfying, but I wasn’t sure that Nyssa got what she deserved. She didn’t really grow much as a character and her journey didn’t really have much of a pay off in the end.

Culan, on the other hand, did. There was a clear arc of growth and acceptance, a very clear lesson learned. The best way of dealing with one’s mediocrity isn’t to go hanging out with strange sentient skulls, but to find strength in the community of friends one keeps. (Something I will remember if I ever come across a sentient death-magic skull in the future.)

Talking of the friends one keeps, there is an interesting supporting cast in this book. Some of the characters were clearly more significant in the previous book, but had somewhat faded into the shadows in this one (Salvera, Jirrini) but others, like Shadas, remain important. Shadas is Nyssa’s personal necromancer, someone who had evidently messed up in the last book but has since redeemed himself through unswerving loyalty, showing an interesting level of grace and forgiveness from Nyssa. This loyalty is tested once more in this book, giving Shadas an interesting, redemptive arc throughout the story, and making him one of the more interesting characters to follow.

There are also new characters to follow who provide a bit of flavour. Leta, from Salvera’s bloodline, who I mentioned earlier, is ambitious and deadly. She also has her own political goals in the background, as all Soulblights do.

And then there is Boro, from the Crimson Keep. Powerful, arrogant, uncompromising and one of Vhordrai’s inner circle. He is also ambitious, but his ambition feels much more inevitable. His eyes are lidless, always looking upon Nyssa as the ever-judging embodiment of what it means to be Kastelai, living with their impressive and potent lineage. Oh yeah, and he has a big ass zombie dragon too.

Prince Vhordrai. Credit: chimp

As far as worldbuilding goes, you’re only really going to get a small glimpse of the Mortal Realms from reading this book. You’ll get a little look at the lore around the Kastelai vampires of the Soulblight Gravelords, and into how their society works and the general kind of Soulblight 'vibe'. You’ll get a little taste of some of the nomad societies in Aqshy, but in the grand scheme of things as far as the meta narrative of the Age of Sigmar goes, as Boro points out in the book, this story is set in some dusty insignificant corner of the realms. It has no real bearing on anything in terms of how the story of Warhammer plays out, and could have taken place at any point in the AOS timeline.

That doesn’t mean it doesn’t matter or isn’t worth a read, some of my favourite novels in the Age of Sigmar universe are completely insignificant in the grand scheme of things (Arkanaut's Oath, Blood of the Everchosen, Bad Loon Rising,) but just don’t go reading this expecting to gain an understanding of the wider Warhammer world (unless you have a particular interest in the Crimson Keep – but, even then, you’re not going to learn a whole lot.)

The main enemy - in terms of the army the heroes have to fight against, at least - are the Beasts of Chaos. But the conflict that matters the most are the internal wranglings among the vampires themselves, and, indeed, within the character's own minds. So, whilst it’s nice to know the Beasts of Chaos are still around in the setting, having been unceremoniously deleted from the game itself last year, there’s not going to be much here for anyone really hoping to delve into their 'faction' lore. Still, they are well described, the fights are interesting enough and they provide enough of a threat to give weight to the story.

Overall, this is a fun read. I don’t think it's going to blow your mind, but it’s something a little different and explores some interesting themes from some interesting perspectives. Some aspects of the book's resolution were unsatisfying, but Nyssa Volari's story is certainly interesting enough to keep on telling.
