Surviving an Edition Change in Warhammer 40K

May 18th, 2026 via Goonhammer
It’s been 3 years since Warhammer 40k launched its 10th edition, and now 11th edition is right around the corner. Things are changing, the sky is falling, and I’ve got one thing to ask you: First time? I’m here to hold your hand and guide you through the dark, letting you know what to expect, what to do, and how to thrive in this wholly precedented but still occasionally worrisome triennial process. 

The Video Version

If you'd rather watch this video and listen to my dulcet-adjacent tones for 20 minutes instead of reading this article, you may do so below:

Before the Edition

What to Expect

A high school game of 40k. Credit: SRM, circa 2005

I’ve been regularly playing 40k since 2005, starting with 4th edition. Obviously, 4th edition was the best edition, which certainly has nothing to do with the fact that I was 14 years old and could have pizza-fueled weekend long games with my friends in an environment where all of my needs were met, unlike now where the game is obviously terrible and my feelings couldn’t possibly have anything to do with my receding hairline, stiff joints, or overdue taxes. Over the course of 21 years in this hobby, I’ve seen the game change from being a plodding, kind of messy but still enjoyable wargame, to an overcomplicated hell edition full of invisible deathstars and free transports, to two separate reboots, both of which made the game more technical with more clearly written rules, but a relentless update schedule. Every edition has done some things right and some things wrong, and what those things were will change depending on who you ask, but they’ve all still felt like Warhammer. No matter the year or size of your Space Marine’s base, it’s still been a game where two or more players move, shoot, and charge with all their little dudes. The specifics of how those are handled might change, but whether your engagement range is one inch or three or you can charge after shooting a Rapid Fire weapon or not, it’s still Warhammer at the end of the day. 

What to Buy

Listen, I’m not going to tell you to straight up not buy anything here - if we were smart with money, we wouldn’t be playing Warhammer, and I wouldn’t have a backlog currently threatening to outlive me. However, if you’re planning on going all-in on the new edition with starter sets, splash boxes, and so on, it’s probably best to budget some money now and hold off on buying stuff for a while, at least for Warhammer 40k. We have no idea what will be good in the new edition yet, and trying to predict that is a fool’s errand.

If you’d rather avoid that particular advice, let me at least guide your spending a little differently. If you do want to buy some cool models because they’re currently really good in the game, understand that they might not be so strong once the new edition drops. I’ve seen units oscillate between actively detrimental to your army to being nearly game-breakingly good over the course of an edition change, only to wind up back in the dumpster a few months later. Good rules are temporary, cool models are forever. Unless they go to Legends anyway. Pour one out for my Contemptor Dreadnoughts. 

Ultramarines Ballistus Dreadnought. Credit SRM

The exception here is with starter sets for the outgoing edition, since the models from those either go out of production, or are harder to find at that price point again. I bought this Ballistus for 20 bucks from my buddy because they’re so easy to find right now, but once 10th edition starter sets stop being sold, they won’t be so prevalent on the secondhand market. If there’s something from the existing starter sets you want, grab it while you still can.



Anything which is sharing a sprue with a different unit, like this sprue from the Leviathan box with Sternguard, a Phobos Lieutenant, and an Apothecary Biologis, is likely to go out of production. Hell, some independent game stores might even discount old starters since they’re going to get replaced soon anyway, so get them before the secondhand market starts charging gold bouillon for out of production plastic. 

Hobby Projects

One of the recurring things you’ll hear is “new edition, new army”, four words that can spell instant financial and temporal ruination to any hobbyist. If I followed that practice, I would have 7 armies by this point, something I certainly could never do. I certainly wouldn’t collect more than that, and definitely wouldn’t have multiple Ultramarines armies specifically. It’s a fun challenge and something a lot of hobby stores and Games Workshop encourage for pretty obvious financial reasons, but don’t feel pressured to buy in unless there’s something out there that really makes your little heart go pitter patter. 

The time between editions is also an opportunity to work on the weird models that maybe you haven’t gotten to, those backburner projects you started but kept putting down because something else made more sense at the time. Maybe a dataslate nerfed that cool tank you were working on or the shifting meta made another unit or army more exciting than what was on your desk - now’s a great time to pick up those half-finished projects and get them done. Maybe by the time 11th comes around, they’ll be great, and if not, it’s one less thing in your backlog.

Avoiding the Rage

Warhammer Community typically drip feeds articles explaining any given new edition, and while these are interesting and fun little nuggets to speculate over, they don’t paint a complete picture. Leaks and rumors are the same way. A rule or unit in isolation might sound ridiculous or game-breaking, but once you see it in context, it usually makes some kind of sense - it might not always seem like it, but nobody’s designing rules at random over there.

A quick scroll through the very normal brained comments on a Warhammer Facebook post will see some of the angriest and most deranged takes in the world because they got to see half of a rule without its full context, and that must mean that everything is ruined and God is dead now. Hell, I remember someone on Warseer or Dakka Dakka back when 5th edition dropped who was absolutely convinced that units getting to run D6” instead of shooting would ruin the game forever, and now advance rolls have been in the game for more editions than they haven’t. You've probably been recommended some ragebait YouTube video where a bunch of red arrows are pointing at some angry grifter’s face or an AI slop image of a Great Value brand Space Marine facepalming, but just… don’t click on those. You’re better than that. You are what you eat in an informational sense, and you don’t need some wannabe provocateur raising your ire just to give them views and spike your cortisol. Wait for the complete picture to form, and maybe, I dunno, play the game when it finally comes out, then form your own opinion. That opinion can be informed by content creators and journalists if you’d like - after all, we’re writing about this stuff too - but you have a fully formed brain and can form your own opinions after earnestly engaging with something.  

Other Games and Other Ways to Play

Empire State Troops with Swords and Shields. Credit: SRM

The time before a new edition is also a great time to get into some other games, either playing something totally different or just painting up some cool minis while you bide your time. There’s fortunately more games out there than ever before, so if something’s catching your eye and you’re feeling a little burnt out on 40k, give something else a try. Maybe you lean into skirmish games like Trench Crusade or old favorites like Battletech. Maybe this is when you finally learn how to play The Old World and paint up the other hundred State Troops in your closet, not naming names or anything. 

This is also when you could try to wring some more enjoyment out of the edition before it heads out the door. For me, 10th edition was spent entirely in the trenches playing Matched Play and competitive games. I thought tournaments and competitive play were dirty words until near the end of 9th, then found myself having a blast playing competitive games all through 10th. It turns out just playing a bunch of Warhammer with clearly written objectives and balanced missions can make for a pretty good time. Only at the end did I branch out and try some more casual games, playing White Dwarf missions with sillier army lists, and I’m glad I did. It wouldn’t have been my preferred way to play most of 10th, but it was a fun change of pace and a different experience from the symmetrical layouts and mission cards I’d been using for the past three years. If you haven’t seen how the other side plays, give it a shot; it’s not often you get to try something new using stuff you already have.

When the Edition Drops

Playing Your First Games

Oldhammer Meets 8th Edition. Credit: SRM and Dylan Gould

One thing I always run into at tournaments is the person who has never played a game of 40k before, and this is their first time actually playing. Since they’re in a tournament environment, and people are there to win, these folks typically get smoked and don’t always have a great time. Conversely, it doesn’t feel great to stomp an inexperienced player, especially when that takes forever because they don’t know the rules and need to keep looking them up or having them explained. My point here is that if you want to learn the game, do that in a more relaxed environment where players aren’t crunched for time or trying to play at their competitive best. Get some relaxed games in with your friends or try and put together a learning day at your local game store or club. 

One of my favorite memories of 8th edition was getting together with a bunch of my buddies to get the new rules on launch day, then hanging out and playing small games of 40k with each other while the folks who weren’t playing could reference rules and find out what changed. It was the closest to that feeling of being a teenager and discovering this game I’d felt as an adult, and it was all because we decided to learn this game together in a chill, friendly environment.

My group rented out the event space at a brewery for a 10th edition community learning day. Credit: SRM

When you do get those games in, maybe opt for a smaller points size and simpler scenarios. Focus on the core mechanics of the game and how they’ve changed, and get those rhythms down before going for more complex missions and army variants. Read the rules thoroughly and don’t assume anything has stayed the same; sometimes they just change the wording of a rule, and sometimes they overhaul it completely. 

Edition Bleed

One unfortunate side effect of playing so many versions of Warhammer over so many years is something I call edition bleed. There’s always a million little edits and revisions that change the game in ways big and small, and sometimes you’ll remember the wrong version of a rule. In many ways, it’s easier to learn a whole new game than a new edition of one you’re familiar with, since you aren’t trying to edit what’s already in your memory. What’s worse is when the name of a rule changes or they bring back some old term only to have it mean something completely different, like how in 10th edition Twin Linked lets you re-roll failed wound rolls, but back in 3rd edition through 7th edition it meant you got to re-roll a hit roll. The only cure is reading comprehension and getting those reps in, so simply be smarter and have more free time. That shouldn’t be hard, right? Right?

Events

I’ll be real, it can be hard to feel motivated to go to a major 40k event when I know a new edition is right around the corner. I went to a tournament like two weeks ago, and all the skills I’ve built up with my list and detachment might not mean anything in a month or so when 11th drops. Instead of ruminating on the impermanence of life and the rules that govern it, I decided to look at that tournament more as one last hurrah or a going away party for an edition that I’ve genuinely loved. Going forward, an event can be a great way to play a load of games in rapid succession, drilling down the rules you’ve just learned. As I said earlier, you’ll want to have familiarized yourself with the rules and played some games before going to a tournament or whatever, but playing an 8 game GT will definitely cement that edition in your mind once you’ve gotten to grips with it.

Updating Your Collection

If “New year, new army” is the frivolous way to hobby in a new edition; and I mean, this is all frivolous, it’s toy soldiers, then updating your collection is the comparatively responsible thing to do. Part of that is going to involve painting new releases or whatever you need to get your army to meet the needs of the new edition, which is something the community will really only understand after the edition’s been out for a minute. Some editions treat melee infantry or vehicles better, while Codex updates or new detachments might make a certain type of character absolutely clutch. Once you have the lay of the land, you can figure out what to work on.

Speaking of Codex updates, understand that you’re almost certainly going to get one. Long gone are the days of waiting a decade for a new Dark Eldar or Sisters of Battle codex, or getting a crummy half-codex in the pages of White Dwarf. If the last two editions are anything to go by, when that Codex drops, you’ll get a bushel of new rules, probably some new units, and general rules updates that flesh out your army and bring it up to speed with the other stuff in the edition. Unless you’re one of the starter box armies, you might have to wait a minute, but just be patient. It’s not like you have a backlog to work through or anything.

The one thing basically everybody is going to need to contend with is updating their terrain. If you’re playing Matched Play 40k, you’re going to want to get up to speed with whatever the new terrain setups are. That could be buying whatever GW puts out, getting some third party terrain and footprints from a company like Tablewar or Bandua, or building your own stuff. Futureproofing terrain is really hard since the needs of the game have changed so much over the years, but if you are going the DIY route, build your terrain to be modular. Even if you just have some regular plastic or printed ruins you can move around on whatever footprints are the most up to date, that’ll go a long way. Now that’s probably less of an issue if you’re more of a narrative or casual player, but you should probably still think about how your terrain will work in the new edition and if it’ll still be fun to play on. Maybe that’ll be building footprints for your cool scratchbuilt terrain to sit on, or getting inspired by the rules and narratives of the edition and making something new to fight over. Even if your terrain is just for narrative games, it should still be playable.

Stepping Away and Coming Back

White Panthers Tactical Marines Robert “TheChirurgeon” Jones

A lot of people also see a new edition as a time to step away from the game. Maybe they really liked the last edition, maybe they don’t want to keep up with the new one, or maybe life has just been happening and they can’t focus on 40k right now. That’s all fine, even if it can be sad to see someone from your gaming group drop off for a while. Provided they don’t sell off all their stuff, they can always jump back in later. The inverse is people coming back to the game because they heard a new edition was dropping, and this was a perfect opportunity to dust off their old stuff and play with it again, or 40k was something they always wanted to get into, but didn’t know how to start. I’ve heard stories like these almost every edition, and fortunately, 40k is probably always gonna be there for people to come back to whenever they want.

If you’re getting reacquainted with 40k after a long absence, I’d recommend treating it like a whole new game and putting your old experiences aside. The worst kind of onlooker at the game store is the person talking at you about how the game used to be, and you don’t want to live in the past like that person. We’re living in the future, baby, Marines have good posture now! It can be discouraging if your old stuff doesn’t work, whether it went to Legends, changed sizes, or is otherwise less than optimal, but if you’re just learning the game and coming back, people should be understanding, and ideally welcome you back into the fold. Asking people in your community what’s changed or what’s new since the last time you played is a great place to start, and if you don’t have a community you’re keyed into, we’ve got a great Discord community over on the Tabletop Battles Patreon. More about that later. 

The Worst Case Scenario

So now for the elephant that may or may not be in the room - I don’t know what your floorplan looks like - which is what to do if you straight up don’t like the edition. Maybe your collection doesn’t work in it, a bunch of your favorite units got invalidated, or the missions aren’t to your liking. Well there’s a few options here. Putting it bluntly, you can play a few games and just get used to it - sometimes those first impressions don’t hook you, but eventually you come around. Alternatively, this is a living game, and the next time a balance update or mission pack drops, it might win you over. You could always stick with an older edition, but that’s kind of a losing prospect long-term. It’s a hard sell to find other people who want to get into an edition of the game with no ongoing support and out of print rules, and most organized play is going to be fully out of the picture. Then again, there was a 4th edition 40k tournament at Adepticon and people still play dead games like Mordheim and Adeptus Titanicus, so anything’s possible. Lastly, like we said before, you can just step away for a while. Maybe it’ll change to be more to your liking, maybe it won’t, maybe you’ll achieve enlightenment and free yourself of the concept of desire and obliterate the self, who knows. 



The worst, worst case scenario is your game actually exploding, which happened when Age of Sigmar had its infamously rocky launch back in 2015. It was sudden, poorly explained, and changed Warhammer Fantasy into something completely different and fundamentally incomplete. People were understandably upset, and while plenty of people went along with the change, loads of other folks talked up a big game and went over to other games like Kings of War or The Ninth Age. No shade to those other games, but once Age of Sigmar got more developed rules, points values, and excised rules like getting rerolls for having a bigger moustache than your opponent, people came back to it, and eventually The Old World came out to cover those square based rank and flank needs. Unless you’re super pressed for storage space, you can put your stuff away for a bit and come back once things change. Unless you burn your army like that one guy though. That video has been living rent free in my head for almost a third of my life, my god. 

Final Thoughts

So as someone who has been here before: All of this has happened before and all of this will happen again. In time, everyone will have their own favorite editions and some different ideas of what the best and worst times to play 40k have been, which means every so often I see someone pine for seventh edition and have to wonder who hurt them. Warhammer is still Warhammer, no matter what number comes after it, and like the weather in New England, if you don’t like it, just wait 5 minutes.
