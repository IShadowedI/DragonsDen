Tabletop Battles Reviews: Hive, by Dan Abnett

June 12th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Games Workshop

Before diving in between the covers (all four of them), it's worth taking a moment to recognize Hive not just as a book- the latest offering from longtime legend (and fourteen-time Black Library Readers' Hall of Fame inductee) Dan Abnett, but also what it represents. A double novel, one that not only has no ties to an established arena like Armageddon (currently in currency due to the game's 11th Edition launch) but also stays well away from most of the standard-issue story ingredients (looking at you, Astartes) and instead involves... life in an Imperial hive city.

How many Black Library writers could have pitched that, do you suppose, and gotten a green light? How many more could have seen it greenlit, perhaps, but with the caveat that they trim it to one book?

I don't know about yours, but my list is small indeed. Few writers have managed to push the boundaries out of what 40K can be like Abnett, but I have to think that with Hive even the editorial staff might have felt like this concept was a leap of faith. "In Hive," noted Abnett in his Afterword, "the idea for this novel came out of a conversation I had with my editor Kate." Oh, to have been a fly on that particular wall!

While he might not have employed it in that chat with Kate, the High Concept Pitch is a common marketing tool that helps explain a concept by framing it as the synthesis of two other concepts. Typically expressed similarly to "X meets Y," it's obviously reductive, so once you go looking for it it's not hard to find it just about anywhere. The Adeptus Mechanicus, for example, can be seen as "cyberpunk transhumanism meets medieval monasticism." Stormcast Eternals are "Space Marines meet Warhammer fantasy." Robbie MacNiven's excellent Vagabond Squadron (review)? "Aeronautica meets the Vietnam War." Ciaphas Cain is Flashman meets Blackadder, and so on.

It's a reflection of the imaginative fertility of the setting that so many so-concepted books have a welcome home in the Warhammer universe. The war stories are obviously at the heart of it, but at the same time those who love espionage will delight in James Swallow's Nemesis or Robert Rath's Assassinorum: Kingmaker. Warhammer Crime and Horror received full imprints, and of course there's the never-ending suggestion of someday getting a Warhammer Romance line (and if so, Mike Brooks will be ready!).

Dan Abnett's latest takes a run at something I didn't expect to ever see executed well in the canon: historical fiction. And sure, obviously you can't do 'historical' fiction in Warhammer because it's a fictional universe, but as I became drawn into the book the parallels were very quickly apparent. Hive, for all its unreality, is every bit as sweeping a saga as you'll find from some of my favorite historical fiction writers such as Ken Follett, Leon Uris, and James Clavell.

And it's not just good. It's a masterpiece.

A cop and his dog. Credit: Boyes

A Hive Is its People

My most-beloved historical fiction books, stories such as Trinity, Pillars of the Earth, and Colleen McCullough's The First Man in Rome, all have certain elements in common. They often run an ensemble cast, often with a number of disparate story arcs and threads that slowly, over the course of the novel, begin to both accelerate and gravitate towards points of convergence.

They also frequently feature major historical events, sometimes serving as a backdrop at first before growing in focus to subsume the story's protagonists. These can often be a kind of trainwreck-in-slow-motion since the reader already knows what's coming, but the delight of the story is in seeing how the characters the author has created are affected by it.

As always, I will endeavor to keep this review spoiler-free, but note that the review's final section (Bits n' Pieces) will have some story spoilers. A significant part of Hive involves mystery, including a police procedural subplot that has significant ramifications for the story overall.

In Hive, we are introduced to the world of Sacramentus, an Imperial world recovering from a brutal civil war whose embers yet smolder on. The story is set in Hive Sacramentus (everyone just calls it the Hive), the capital city of the planet and whose corrupt and incompetent Domne (planetary governor) has recently been deposed by the Adeptus Arbites. Meanwhile, the hive is a simmering cauldron of intrigue and discontent, one that threatens to boil over at any moment.

Meet the New Boss

This thread involves the installation of a new Domne, Aphra Montroff Gentileshi. Gentileshi isn't from the Hive, but rather has been appointed the position by the Administratum. She's an outsider, a fact that is both a source of tremendous tension as well as the reader's window into the upper echelons of Hive society.

The Domne-to-be and her retinue must learn the ways of the Hive and stamp their authority on the world- all while navigating the occasionally lethal palace intrigues of the nobility.

Once and Future Don

As above, so below? Szen Kaidemuller was a ganger of the Alphabetters before heading off to war as part of the LandLas, the planet's Astra Militarum regiment. When a vacancy opens up at the top of the gang's hierarchy, it sets off a power struggle that could take Szen all the way to the top- but only if he plays his cards very, very carefully.

Disorganized Labor

One of Warhammer 40K's most enduring themes is how the Imperium can often be its own worst enemy, and in the Hive it is little different. While the nobility thrive in the light, the commons toil below. As in a forest, when enough kindling has built up over time, all it can take is the right spark to set everything ablaze.

And enigmatic military officer Amaday Orquid is holding a lighter.

On the Beat

It seemed like an ordinary disturbance call to the remote agrarian outskirts of the Hive- until Investigator Moler found the bodies. A gangland killing? Ritual murder? The strange metal curio of a strange color placed next to each body suggests that whatever he thinks it could be, it's probably worse.

Smart enough to know when he's in over his head, he partners with Scuti, a member of the Arbites and together they try to unravel a mystery that raises more questions than answers at every step.

A Splitting Headache

Uthan Fenn lives the simple life of a Hive factory worker. Long shifts, few breaks, a single room to come home to while marking the time 'til your next shift. But Imperial workers don't get sick days, so when he starts to feel under the weather it means a dock in pay and potential reassignment if he doesn't honor his commitment to the Emperor.

But this is well beyond a routine fever or stomachache. There's something strange about this, something unnatural. And other people... they're getting sick too.

Squaring the Circle

In any society the tendrils of corruption find soil, and in the Hive they are close to fully fruiting. On the verge of the final ritual that will open the way for their patron to manifest in the world, one that will usher in the dawn of a new age of enlightenment for the Hive, the Circle finds to their horror that they're not the only ones moving in the shadows.

Love's Labour Lost

Pressure makes diamonds, and in the unlikeliest place Cherish Palgrave, a menial worker from the undercity tasked with lopping heads off outdated statues of the Domne, will find an unlikely friendship, an unexpected romance- and a lifetime's worth of terror in one night.

Spies Like Us

The civil war between the Hive and Ostrika may be officially over, but for an Ostrikan infiltration cell in the Hive that's no cause to stop ops. After all, sometimes all it takes to snatch victory from the jaws of defeat is one lucky bullet, or one well-placed bomb.

All of these threads grow, develop, and begin to connect with other threads across the roughly 550 pages of the novel. Some will be cut short, others persisting until the last, but ultimately one thread will rise above the rest to chart the future course of the Hive.

Orlock Ganger, Credit - Loxi

Crafting the Craft

Spare a moment to first consider the technical challenges that faced Abnett here. Eight different narrative arcs, each needing to develop in a way that their overlap seemed a natural progression of the story. Abnett employed longer chapters but with multiple POV swaps per chapter, keeping a lot of balls in the air to juggle.

Then add to that the need for character development as well as setting exposition, and you begin to get a sense of how monumental a task this was to pull off. Hive is Abnett at his most ambitious.

Of course, just as with recent films such as Tenet, Ad Astra, and The Tree of Life, there is such a thing as a technically exceptional creative work that nevertheless doesn't quite hit the mark with audiences. I had some concerns with this when some early readers complained of excessive worldbuilding, and while I could see where they were coming from as I dove into the book myself I had the opposite reaction. It took about a hundred pages to start finding more conventional 40K tropes, but this wasn't due to Abnett simply ladling in loredumping exposition.

Instead, he was building something that was living and breathing. The different regions of the Hive, its cultures and histories, right down to the way people speak...these weren't just background decorations, but infused the very characters of the story. This gave the story the kind of gritty realism that I've most frequently enjoyed in the Warhammer Crime stories, say, or Matthew Farrer's Shira Calpurnia trilogy.

It's a measure of Abnett's skill here that it's difficult to say exactly where the pace of the book starts to accelerate, only that it does. Halfway through the first book you've got labor unrest, an assassination plot, underworld crime power plays, a possible cult murder spree, hordes of mysteriously-dying rats in the tunnels and more starting to bring the pot to a boil. It was remarkable to realize, as I did somewhere around page 290, that in any other book it would be moving swiftly towards conclusion at that point, but here there was an entire second volume to go.

By the time you're midway through the second book, things are boiling over and Hive is a roller-coaster all the way through to the end.

Final Thoughts

In a career that has been as stacked as Abnett's has for the Black Library, so filled with foundational and influential stories and characters, it feels funny to call Hive his magnum opus. But in so many ways, Hive seems a book that Abnett's been working towards his whole career. Ambitious almost to the point of chutzpah, this double-novel pulls off the extraordinary: a fully-fleshed story of an Imperial hive city that first appears to put a healthy distance between much of the canon it shares publication with, before artfully landing right smack in the middle of it.

Hive won't be for everyone, but what book is? If the kinetic frenzy and larger-than-life heroics of Astartes "bolter-porn" is more your jam, you might find this too deep an investment for the payoff. But if you're after a deliberate and thoughtful tale that really gets in under the hood of what life is life in the Imperium of Man, you're in for a real treat.

I've always been something of a neophile in my entertainment, with openings more exciting than closings. The first hundred turns in Civilization are by far the most exciting, and even in books I enjoy I'll eventually get to a point where I'm looking forward to wrapping it up so I can crack into another one. That never happened with Hive. The rarest of rare stories, this one I never wanted to end.

Bits n' Pieces (SPOILERS)

In this section I've got some specific thoughts on the book that you might want to skip until you've had the opportunity to read it. You can't say you weren't warned!

  • Abnett nailed the argot here. It's no secret just how much vocabulary he's contributed to the world of 40K that feels like it's always been there, and he uses that to great effect here. While he helpfully includes a glossary in the books Afterword, you hardly need it as context is plentiful. It added just enough to give the story a feeling of place, never felt overdone, and even had a cameo in a major plot point about the Hive's origins.
  • One thing I found particularly impressive is how well Abnett blended genres here. In the narrative arc summaries above I called out ones like palace intrigue and police procedurals, but the book had no small moments of horror as well. An earlier scene when Annjanu Naysay is exploring the long-derelict tunnels and finding the dead rats had the same kind of claustrophobic dread that made Alien work so well, and later scenes with the Tyranids just as effectively channeled its sequel, Aliens.
  • The high-water mark here, though, was the scene were an expedition goes out into the wastelands beyond the Hive. I got genuine chills at the line, "it's eating me," which brought the terrible story of Olga Moskalyova to mind.
  • The last time I saw Tyranids used this effectively for unsettling horror was in my personal Black Library Book of the Year for 2024, Victoria Hayward's Deathworlder. I wish they were used like this more often!
  • Finally, I loved the plot twist that took the Slaaneshi cult off the board early. Having read so many books where the main villain is the secret Chaos cult, seeing them employed as a red herring here was masterful misdirection.
