Tabletop Battles Reviews: Necromunda Skirmish - Overview

August 1st, 2026 via Goonhammer
Roughly a month ago Games Workshop revealed that a brand new edition of Necromunda would be unleashed. Well scummers, the good news is that that time is now. The really good news is that it looks pretty damn good, and that most of the changes are focused on making play tighter and cleaning up some of the parts that were complex without contributing to the quality of the game. There is a lot for us to absorb and we will be providing multiple articles to cover as much as we can. That starts with this article, which will provide a general overview of the game and what’s changed. Many thanks to Games Workshop for providing us with an advanced review copy.

Credit: Games Workshop

If you’re new to Necromunda, welcome! We here at TTB are convinced that Necromunda is one of the best games that Games Workshop ever launched, and it’s a major part of our events such as the Baltimore Open. Necromunda is a skirmish-level game that takes place in the depths of a hive city, which are metal mountains containing billions of people. The core setting is the capital of the planet Necromunda, Hive Primus. At the top of the hive is The Spire that pierces the clouds and contains the ruling nobility of the planet. At the bottom is the Underhive where gangs of humans, abhumans, and mutants ceaselessly fight for meager resources. Each player forms a gang of five to ten fighters and fights through a campaign against other players to capture resources, advance their fighters, and survive. The advancement, campaign setting, and fighter-level rules give the game a RPG-style vibe that can result in moments of absolute chaos. It’s incredibly fun.

With respect to this review, we will be providing an overview of the contents of the Necromunda Skirmish Core Set. That consists of the new Core Rulebook, the Core Set Starter Guide, the Zone Mortalis: Ruined Factoria Pipes, game aids, and an Escher and Goliath gang roster that are designed to be balanced against each other. Warhammer Community revealed that other content will be coming soon, such as Tactics Cards, Campaign and Scenario Cards, and gang books for the Underhive and the Outlands. 

Credit: Hive Market Minis

We are overall bullish about the changes. The core gameplay loop is basically the same. Some important details about moving, shooting, close combat and incurring (and recovering from) injuries have been changed. We think most of these changes are positive, although some are simply different and some we can’t yet predict the full consequences of. We are wholly positive about some changes to the campaign layer of Necromunda. Scenarios have been flattened out, with a robust method for quick Scenario generation at the table. Some changes to the Post-Battle and Post-Cycle sequences, which are now better differentiated, should serve to flatten out gangs’ income streams, and make post-Battle/Cycle actions more interesting. Finally, the way fighters gain XP and level up, receiving Advances, has been significantly reworked and those changes are all very promising.

There’s a lot to go through here, so we will be dividing our reviews into several areas. This article will be a high-level look at everything, and then the other articles will cover the details of the major elements.

  • General Overview - That’s this article!
  • Core Gameplay - Major changes to the game and how fighters try to kill each other.
  • Gang Composition - The fighters from each gang and how different they are at this killing thing.
  • Skills and Progression - How fighters advance so they can get better at killing.
  • The Trading Post - The major changes to the weapons and wargear and their killing effectiveness.
  • Campaign and Scenarios - The framework by which fighters justify killing one another.
  • The Box - The models, tokens, dice, terrain, and other fiddly bits which enable simulating killing.
  • Roundtable - In which the TTB Necromunda Weirdos kill each other with words.
Necromunda. The only game where people will hate a model so much they will offer real money to see it dead. And yes, that's a horrifying cyber-monstrosity attempting to ram the Land Train to claim the bounty. Credit: Kevin Genson

Core Rules

Games Workshop has taken a very comprehensive and detailed approach to the rules. Many of these changes are designed to make things more cohesive, and should be easily understood by veteran and new Necromunda players after a few games. There are a lot of changes that will be covered in the deep dive articles, but this section will look at some of the major ones and how they indicate the philosophy that seems to be behind N26. 

The book starts with First Principles, such as how a Model can either be a Fighter or Vehicle, along with the definitions of players and who is controlling. Major elements such as Line of Sight, Cover, Measurement, the types of dice, how tests and checks work, Status, Conditions, Model types, and Model characteristics are all covered first. For the rare player who actually reads the book from front to back it will provide an easily followed progression of the rules. For the rest of us it will be an excellent reference after we jump to the Trading Post and freak out about whatever the hell Lethality is. 

Models (Fighters and Vehicles) exist at all times in a Status, of which there are four. Active is the default Status and imposes no restrictions. Engaged models are those locked in close combat, and Engaged models are limited to Fight or Retreat actions. Fighters and Vehicles behave differently; a Fighter in base-to-base contact with another Fighter is always Engaged, but a Fighter that activates within 1” of a Vehicle (or vice-versa) can choose to be Active instead. The third Status is Suppressed (the new version of pinning) which limits Fighters to a single action but Models automatically recover at the end of their Activation. The final Status is Seriously Injured (for Fighters) and Seriously Damaged (for Vehicles); when a model rolls a Seriously Injured result on an Injury dice they are put into this Status which severely limits which options they can perform. If a model regains a Wound then they immediately return to Active status, which was a surprise as we all assumed that a model would be Suppressed first. These states are all exclusive, which helps to remove confusion, and they are simpler and neater than the previous attempt to define them. 

Joe's (JoJo_Baggins) Squats at the GHO 2026 Necromunda Weekender. Credit: Kevin Genson

Actions have been consolidated and are now either Free, Single, or Double. A Fighter can’t perform an action of the same name twice, even if they’re different action types, unless a skill permits it. This means that rather than perform Move (Simple) twice, a Fighter will have to use Dash (Double) which is slightly less effective since the distance moved is Movement + Initiative. Heavy weapons have taken a significant nerf, as they can only be fired using the Braced Shot (Double) action. Vehicles also seem to be limited here as there is no option to fire and move with a Heavy weapon, but they can use the Fire All (Double) action to fire everything but still can’t move. There are several other examples of this, and every decision seems to be designed with a lot of tactical play in mind. Even Seriously Injured players have to make decisions; are they better off trying to Crawl (Double) away from enemy Fighters or just give a Desperate Escape (Double) so that they go Out of Action but only roll on the Lasting Injury table on a D6 roll of 1 or 2? 

Combat

The major themes of movement and combat are very similar to the previous edition. One significant alteration is the addition of the Agility Test, which is nominally a 4+ on a D6 to perform actions that were previously Initiative tests such as jumping across gaps or avoiding falling down a ledge. Falling itself has become considerably more lethal, with a falling distance between 3” and 6” (Sector Mechanicus terrain is 5” tall) automatically causing a hit that Wounds with no saves allowed. Also models can still fall if they are Suppressed or Seriously Injured/Damaged within 0.5” of a ledge, so this is still Necromunda. 

Combat has also changed. Apparently Hive Primus found a massive store of ammunition because shooting attacks do not carry the risk of going Out of Ammo by default; weapons need certain traits (Rapid Fire (X) and Ammo(X)) and unless the weapon has the Scarce (X) trait reloading via the Reload (Simple) action  is automatically successful. Cover now provides a bonus to saves against ranged attacks instead of modifying the Hit roll (which is offset by the fact that saves are generally worse). Close Combat has also changed significantly; each combatant fights in order determined by their modified Initiative characteristic. The Active model gains their full complement of attacks while other models only get one attack. When wielding multiple weapons models allocate all of their attacks to their Primary weapon, while a Secondary weapon gets one attack. Ranged weapons with the Light trait can be used in close combat, but only get one attack per weapon. Overall these changes significantly reduce the complexity of combat and streamline the process. We’re a fan.

One of the most significant changes in the new edition of Necromunda is how damage is applied. Unless a weapon has the Damage (X) trait (and there are very few) then it only removes a single Wound on a successful hit. Instead weapons have a Lethality trait which determines the number of Injury dice rolled once the target is reduced to 0 Wounds. The Injury dice are identical to the previous edition, but instead of a Flesh Wound fighters can get the Injured condition which prevents them from using any skills. There’s no degradation mechanic where subsequent Seriously Injured results changed anything, so we suspect that models will spend a lot of time in the Seriously Injured/Damaged state.

The quintessence of Necromunda is finding a way to launch a Cargo-8 into the head of a Titan. Credit: Kevin Genson

The Arbitrator’s Toolkit

Scattered throughout the rulebook are optional rules called the Arbitrator’s Toolkit. These rules are little boxes scattered throughout the core rules which offer an optional extra or alternative rule that some Arbitrators or groups may prefer. Resolving Stray Shots, for example, is now one of these, rather than baked into the core rules by default. Another is that by default pre-measuring is allowed at all times, while pre-measuring can be prohibited as an option. We are big fans of this approach, since it allows players to either play the game in its new, cleaned up state, or add on their preferred approaches. It’s also an endorsement of the community-led, customisable ethos of Necromunda as a game. The TTB staff are already at odds about which rules are truly optional.

No skill can prepare you for some of the darkest, most sinister horrors of the Underhive. Credit: Kevin Genson

Skills and Progression

A major shift from the previous edition is that models have ranks based on the amount of XP they accrue. Every time a model advances to the next rank they roll on the Advancement table. Fans of Mordheim or the 90’s version of Necromunda may recognize this approach; players roll 2D6 and pick anything on a table that is equal to or less than the result. Adding each Advancement increases a model’s cost by various levels; 5 credits for the lowest (Leadership, Intelligence, or a random Primary skill) and 30 credits if you roll a 12 and can select any skill. Although there is a rule that covers what happens if they cannot gain an Advancement, a roll of 2 allows a model to generate a random primary skill so it’s unlikely that this rule would ever apply. The Advancement table is divided in half with the skills and non-combat characteristics at a 7-8 or below, while the combat abilities like WS, Wounds, or improving a Save is higher. The chances of being able to improve your Wounds, Attacks, or Save are 1 in 12, so expect progression to be very different from the last edition.

Skills in the rulebook are divided into the six familiar core categories, and as with previous editions models will have access to Primary and Secondary skills. It’s not exactly clear how a random skill is generated; the book provides a table for each skill that you can roll a D6 on, but it doesn’t specify if a “random Primary skill” means randomly selecting a Skill Set and then rolling a D6, or if the player gets to choose the Skill Set and then roll. Common sense says it's the latter. The skills themselves are all familiar, and for the most part the Skill Sets are pretty solid. Brawn seems very good with Bulging Biceps now allowing Fighters to move a little while shooting Heavy weapons, Nerves of Steel being great by ignoring Suppressed with a Cool check, and Unstoppable allowing a model to automatically recover 1 Wound with a Willpower check (don’t forget that Injured models lose access to skills). The Savant tree is also interesting, with Connected now giving a bonus to the Visit Trading Post Action and Munitioneer enabling the Distribute Ammo (Single) action which gives all friendly Fighters within 6” the chance to make a free Reload action with an Intelligence check. Between the changes to how Advancement works and the prevalence of Specialists (every ganger gets a free skill) players should expect N26 to be significantly more skill-focused than before.

Credit: Manofwaaagh

Trading Post

The Trading Post has been thoroughly reworked. Accessing the Trading Post is done by sending Leaders and Champions via the Visit Trading Postaction. This is a post-cycle action, meaning you only get to do it a limited number of times (effectively six in a standard campaign). In place of rarity or illegality, items on the Trading Post have a Trading Post (TP) value. Leaders generate 2 TP when they visit the Trading Post, while Champions only generate 1 TP. The only other way to generate TP is through the Connected skill, which any model can take and allows them to perform a free Visit Trading Post Action which does not prevent them from performing another action (including doing Visit Trading Post a second time). TP cannot be banked, you must spend the TP (along with credits) to acquire a weapon, and the values of TP range from 0 to most weapons to 4 for exotic weapons like a warpstorm bolter, grav gun, or multi-melta. This prevents players from stocking up on large quantities of rare items. There are a variety of items at various levels of TP and price; you can always spend 4 TP and 10 credits on a single warp round. Turns out the price of damnation is pretty cheap.

The quantity of changes to prices and item abilities is extensive, and we encourage you to review our Trading Post deep dive. The multi-melta is the most expensive weapon in the game at 150 credits and 4 TP, heavy weapons are generally 100 credits or more (although the rad-cannon at 55 credits and 4 TP is pretty funny), most special weapons are around 60 to 80 credits, and melee stuff is fairly cheap (the power fist is most expensive at 105 credits and 3 TP). Armour is considerably more expensive and provides drastically reduced benefits; most protection is expected to come from the Save characteristic. For example, mesh armour is 40 credits and 0 TP and only improves the Save characteristic by 1 in melee. Light and heavy carapace armor are as expensive as heavy weapons and come with penalties to the Initiative characteristic and (in the case of heavy carapace) a penalty to Agility tests. For personal equipment there are a lot of familiar items including bio-boosters, drop rigs, grapnel launchers, grav chutes, and stimm-slug stashes. The Trazior Pattern Sentry Gun is also available and pretty affordable at 2 TP and 75 to 110 credits depending on the gun.

Players should expect nearly every weapon trait to be different. Major changes include Blaze (X+) which inflicts an additional hit depending on the Wound roll but the Blaze condition is gone, Scarce (X+) which requires a X+ roll on the Reload action to be successful but allows multiple attempts, and Unstable which activates on a Hit roll of natural 1 and causes the mode carrying the weapon to suffer at auomtatic Hit using the weapon's profile. Rad-phage might not be terrible this time, as when it wounds a target they gain the Rad Poisoned condition which reduces their Toughness characteristic by 1. Both the rad cannon and rad gun are S3 now, so that's nice.

Credit: Dylon

Campaign and Scenarios

Necromunda Campaigns follow the same 3 cycle, Downtime (which is also a cycle), 3 cycle format which we all know and love. The format provided in the book is the same territory-focused system used in previous core books, where players fight over a selection of randomly generated territories in the Occupation phase before taking them from each other in the Takeover phase. Players can play as many battles as they want in a cycle, but with things like the Trading Post and medical recovery being post-cycle actions attrition can have a major impact. Each battle has a territory at stake, and the rewards are standardized for each battle. All rewards are randomized, and the winner of the battle receives twice the reward as the loser or if there's a draw. New to this edition are Side Jobs (secondary objectives) which are worth the same as losing, so if a gang loses but completes its Side Job then they could get as many credits as a gang that only wins the battle. 

Territories provide a Boon, which is a bonus that's provided so long as the Territory is controlled. This can be income (which is earned once per cycle) or a benefit which can be received instead of collecting income. For example, a gang that controls a Promethium Cache can either earn 15 credits or add three hazard suits to the Stash for free (worth 30 credits). Every Territory also has a Battlefield Effect which apply to the battlefield when that particular Territory is at stake. For example, fighting in a Bullet Den lets each gang perform a Reload action for free once per round, the Bounty Den generates an additional Side Job, and the Tech Bazaar (which is unique in that it generates 1 TP and always allows players to purchase from the Trading Post) prohibits weapons with the Template or Blast traits to be used in the first round.

Battles are generated using a fixed approach. Players take a board and alternate placing terrain features (or Zone Mortalis tiles), and then alternate placing obstacles or small terrain features. If players agree there is also the option for a Tunnel Warfare scenario where walls cannot be climbed over. Scenarios are generated by rolling a D6 for deployment, objectives, side jobs, and Crew selection for a total of 1,296 combinations. It will be interesting to see if there are particularly good (or bad) options on each table, but it's a really fun start. The book explicitly states that additional scenarios will be available in the future and that tweaking the tables is an option under the Arbitrator's Toolkit. The box does helpfully contain cards for each of the options on the various tables.

Some friendly-looking Cawdor Credit: @co.rius

The Gangs

Regretfully we did not receive a copy of the gang books, so outside of the information provided in the Core Set Starter Guide and Warhammer Community we don't have much. Each gang will receive a Gang List, Datasheets, Equipment List, and references for weapons and unique options such as Wyrd powers. The books also contain options for what are called "Supplementary Fighters" which include Alliances, Hired Guns, Brutes, Pets, and Hangers-on. Games Workshop will also provide a Necromunda's Most Wanted download which will include free rules for many Dramatis Personae and other weirdos that we know and love.

Gang creation will be familiar to veteran players. You're still limited to one Leader, but now you can have as many Champions, Brutes, or Hanger-ons Subtypes as you have models without those options (which includes your Leader). Weapons have become more flexible (still a maximum of three, and bulky weapons count as two), with models able to buy and swap out weapons during cycles and very few fighter-specific equipment limitations. As for the actual fighters it's difficult to say; Escher gain +1 to all Agility Tests, Goliaths gain +1 Strength when performing a Charge action, and both have access to a pair of unique skill trees.

Yes, you can still do incredibly stupid stuff like this. It might take some time to put together all the rules, but it's still possible. Credit: LordPancakes

Wrapping Up

This is a huge transformation for Necromunda. Overall, we here at TTB are very excited about this edition. The changes are absolutely huge and seem like they're really designed to make the game fun and accessible without losing the soul of Necromunda. To the right of the first page covering the First Principles is a description of the game, and after mentioning the lore it has this to say:

it is not designed to be a competitive game, but instead emabraces the chaos and glorous randomness of close quarter battles between desperate gangers, where every cycle could be their last...

Based on what we've seen, it feels like Games Workshop has stayed true to their intention. The rules are considerably tighter than in previous editions, but with the Arbitrator's Toolkit players are free to run any kind of campaign they want. The core set is just that; a core to which many things can be added. There's nothing stopping you from running anything you want, and the game is close enough to previous editions that it shouldn't be hard to transfer over legacy scenarios and rules. Most importantly, it looks incredibly fun.
