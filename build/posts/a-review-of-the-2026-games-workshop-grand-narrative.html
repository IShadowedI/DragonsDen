A Review of the 2026 Games Workshop Grand Narrative

November 24th, 2026 via Goonhammer

This past weekend more than 350 players - most from the United States - converged on the Hyatt Regency in downtown Atlanta for the fifth annual Games Workshop Grand Narrative. The Grand Narrative is, for all intents and purposes, the narrative event for the game, and is the only such event which can boast an actual canon story for its characters and players.

In this review, we'll be covering not just this year's Grand Narrative but the event itself, with everything you need to know about its format and how it works. If you've ever wondered what it's like to be at the event and what goes down, this is your inside scoop.

The Format

Players at the Grand Narrative are split into three faction groups before the event begins based on the faction they're playing - Imperium, Chaos, and Interlopers, the catchall group for the game's Xenos factions. These represent the larger narrative forces arrayed around whatever planet/system is being besieged over the following three days.

From there players play six total games as part of their regularly scheduled program, two per day, broken up between briefings, with a morning, midday, and evening briefing to bookend the games. It's during these briefings that the campaign's warlords make their appearances to the assembled player crowd, talking about their backstories and making their agendas known. These set the scene for players in the coming game, letting them know how things are going and giving them hints about what to do next.

At the end there's a final presentation to show off the aftermath of the narrative, letting players know how their actions influenced the story, along with awards given out for everything from best costume to who was the campaign's biggest dickhead (the antagonist), narratively speaking. The whole event has a loose, laid-back atmosphere, with games being given time to breathe and players given extra time to hang out and chat between games.

Players coordinate during this on a Discord run by us here at Tabletop Battles, where they can talk amongst themselves both in- and out-of-character, show off their armies and cosplay, and receive announcements, room assignments, and campaign information during the event.

Skari as Arathiel, the Ninth Sail

The Warlords

The stars of the Grand Narrative are the warlords, Games Workshop staff who take on the roles of the campaign's key main characters. When you play the Grand Narrative, it's not so much your story as it is theirs - the story isn't so much about your Warlord and their army but rather those Warlords. And your games and actions are more about how you influence those Warlords and their story. Certain players may have an outsized influence on those stories through their actions, but generally the idea is that it's their story, and you're here to help tell it.

The 2026 Grand Narrative featured five Warlords (usually there are six):

  • Kaelen Voss, the Free Captain - The Space Marine at the heart of the whole thing. A former Executioner who left to join Huron's rebellion, then got sick of Huron when he realized the Tyrant of Badab was just another Tyrant. Voss fled and conquered the planet of Ferrum's Wake, liberating it and advocating freedom at all costs. Voss is kind of obviously doomed and delusional - no one is free in the 41st millennium - in a way that's vaguely endearing if you pretend not to know how the galaxy works. He's a Chaos Space Marine who doesn't worship Chaos, which also puts him in a weird spot - he ostensibly appeals more to renegade or rebellious Imperial players and factions than Chaos followers, but there are plenty of players out there who like Chaos but don't actually want to admit to worshipping the evil gods of Space Hell.
  • The Warrant - A mysterious daemonic (?) entity tracking down Voss on behalf of Huron Blackheart. The Warrant is the "loyal to Chaos" side of the Chaos faction, carries an axe, yells about the pantheon a lot, and is mostly here with the straightforward goal of claiming skulls and killing Voss. He's kind of a weird loser beneath Huron's notice and hoping to claim fame and glory by killing Voss. It's unclear if this will actually work.
  • Commander Orestes Morn - A gruff, no-nonsense Imperial Commander who believes in discipline above all else. You know the kind - cold, hard, imperious, and dead set on punishing treachery with the fires of his void ships, letting the Emperor sort out the souls of the faithful. The kind of typical hardass you see in these things who won't listen to reason and shows off how inflexible the religious zealotry of the Imperium can be.
  • Mara Venn, Reluctant Saint - A commoner among the free people of Ferrum's Wake, Venn is a guerilla fighter trying desperately to save her people from Voss' inflicted cruelty and the Imperial bombardments. She focuses on saving her frightened people while raiding armories, rigging bombs, and moving through the shadows. A few close calls and weird coincidences have people calling her a saint.
  • Arathiel, the Ninth Sail - A bold and arrogant Anrathe captain, leading a force of plunderers, marauders, and raiders across the face of Ferrum's Wake for some unknown goal. They seem focused primarily on plunder and having a good time clashing with Imperial and Chaos forces alike. Arathiel is the kind of comedic, scenery-chewing scoundrel that does best at these kinds of things, able to be bombastic and over the top without being so self-serious that the performance collapses under its own attempted weight.

The lack of a sixth warlord option for the Xenos here was unfortunate, as not giving them a second option felt a bit lame, particularly given the associated rules for each warlord.

The Brave Soldiers of Imperial Battlegroup 5

The Battlegroups

Before the event, players are organized into groups of of roughly ten players each called Battlegroups. These are the players you'll be campaigning with over the course of the campaign, loosely aligned to the same goals and making some decisions as a group. Each round, your battlegroup is paired into another rival battlegroup, and with rare exception, always against a battlegroup of a rival faction team, i.e. you don't tend to see Imperium vs. Imperium infighting unless a special game declares it or a player really wants to pretend to be a turncoat. Each battlegroup also had one or two Kill Team players, who represented the special forces/operations part of that team.

How much you interact with your battlegroup is up to you. In my experience, this format works best when you have friends in your group, as you'll be in the same rooms playing with those people over the course of your six games and it makes coordinating group activities easier. If you're not in a battlegroup with people you know, it's a bit more of a mixed bag - I've met some really great people in prior years this way, and I've also had years where I just didn't talk to any of them. That's partly because while a lot of the Tabletop Battles crew show up to the Grand Narrative, most of them play as Imperium factions, meaning we generally aren't allowed to be on the same team.

This is a rough quirk of the team/battlegroup system - You are more or less forced into a faction choice based on your campaign alignment. While this largely makes sense - you do want most of your chaos forces to be chaos forces - it also has a few odd consequences, like making it so you are very likely to end up only playing against space marines for the entire event if you're not on team Imperium. It also means the campaign results end up skewed a bit by how good a Codex or set of Codexes are - it's no coincidence that Xenos have historically done well when the Eldar books were strongest, and this year's Imperial result has a lot to do with Space Marines being overtuned. Winning or losing your games doesn't necessarily affect the story, but being able to do your narrative goal is a hell of a lot easier if you beat the brakes off your opponent.

For this year's campaign, each battlegroup was called to pledge their loyalty to a warlord before the campaign began (with Xenos groups having the option to serve a nebulous "voidborne horrors" instead of Arathiel, if they so chose). My battlegroup took a vote and narrowly chose to support the Warrant, at least initially. Not one battlegroup aligned to Voss in this initial offering, but late in the campaign players were allowed to secretly change their allegiance, at which point around 30ish players did, including about half of my battlegroup. I was literally running Red Corsairs, so defecting to a guy who betrayed Huron was never going to happen.

Once you pledge yourself to your warlord of choice, each player in your group is given an Ascendancy card, which has benefits (buffs) and consequences (debuffs) for your army depending on how well your faction is doing in the campaign. These were wildly unbalanced, ranging from game-breaking to fairly mid, and the Chaos ones in particular had some heinous drawbacks - Voss' let your units advance/fall back and perform an action at the lowest levels and still shoot/charge at the highest levels, but you'd take leadership debuffs at every level. Trying to make Dark Pacts on a 9+ across my army sounds like a nightmare and being able to advance and do an action is meaningless when you spend the whole game battle-shocked.

The Briefings

Before the event, and between every round of games, players are called down to a shared space for a Briefing. These briefings see each Lord of War come out and talk about how things are going, advancing the plot, dispensing new information about how things are going, and then giving players an idea of what they need to do in the next round. These are bookended by a quick reading to set the stage and a note at the end about which level of ascendancy your faction is currently in.

These play out with a lot of bluster, and resemble dinner theater more than anything else. They're a lot of fun, and work best when the actors themselves aren't trying to be too serious. They're often limited by what they can do within the confines of the space and the costumes, and this can be a challenge when they want characters to meaningfully interact in a scene. One particularly rough spot this year had the Warrant come face to face with Voss after the first round, and neither of them fight, which seems very strange given the Warrant's stated goal of killing Voss. I'd later learn that this wasn't intended to be a face-to-face conversation, but it didn't end up coming across that way at all and so it just looked like the two characters were posturing at each other instead of fighting.

This year's standouts were Mara Venn (Lydia Grant) and Arathiel (Skari). Lydia always puts on a strong performance at these things and did a particularly fine job this year as the reluctant saint, who would turn out to not be who she claimed. Or who others claimed she was, anyways. Meanwhile Skari's Arathiel was loud, arrogant, and just the right amount of funny for the role, making him a clear fan favorite. Nick Bayton did admirable work as Kaelen Voss, wearing a massive suit of power armor that clearly limited his mobility but looked amazing, though the role of Voss felt less fleshed out than it needed to be. Voss was always clearly going to turn out to be a tyrant in his own right, but it felt like he should have started closer to that endpoint - as a very clear false hope of freedom under a new populist tyrant rather than someone who seems earnest and becomes corrupted later.

Battlegroups square off

The Games

You play six games as a participant in the Grand Narrative, two per day. Each round gives you about four hours to play, and for that round your Battlegroup will be assigned to a room that represents a specific warzone for the campaign. These are typically decorated to resemble that warzone, with extra foliage or scents, and the tables themselves will be themed to match the vibes of that warzone's battlefields.

This year's battlefields in particular were a massive step up from prior years, in that they featured honest-to-god 11th edition terrain footprints, meaning that each table had actual line-of-sight blocking terrain pieces using abstracted lines of sight. The terrain itself was fine as always, a mix of detailed custom terrain, modern prepainted stuff, and the painted ruins and terrain from recent Narrative/competitive events.

The 2026 Narrative tables, now featuring terrain footprints!

You'll notice these aren't standard layouts - they're set up on the table before you get there, and may not be symmetrical. They also don't always work for every deployment, so you kind of have to figure out which deployment map to use when you arrive so that you'll have an adequate number of objectives outside each player's Deployment Zone. Though I heard several players just show up and say "do you want to just do Dawn of War?" without thinking about this. I think that's a natural inclination, especially for people who don't play often, so I'd suggest to GW that they look at taking this into account when setting up layouts in the future.

The missions themselves were... just normal primary missions for 11th edition 40k, based on your Disposition. Before the campaign we were told to pick two Dispositions which matched our Warlord's temperament, then we could only use those during the campaign, even if we swapped armies. This could have been better worded, as I think many players interpreted it to mean they could take those dispositions regardless of what their Detachments allowed.

Pairings are handled via a Tarot Card system, something they introduced a few years ago. At registration you're given a single Imperial Tarot Card and told to keep it for the entire campaign. When you go to your assigned battlefield rooms, you'll head to the table with the matching card, where your corresponding opponent from the opposing battlegroup with the same card will also go. This works fine, but the cards themselves are difficult to read and there's no mind being paid to player skill, faction, or any other factors when using these cards. It's all random - which again, is fine but I really think the event could benefit from some kind of record matching.

A number of the Vox Localum, flanked by The Warrant on the right

Each room is attended by a member or two of the Vox Localum, GW reps acting in roles as servants to the Warlords. Each Warlord had three associated Vox Localum. These reps are responsible for coordinating players when they enter the battlefield rooms, collecting results, and answering questions. They had more to do this year, and were given more chances to interact with the story and send players on quests. One new quirk this year were the custom secondary missions.

That's the other big quirk this year - upon pledging your loyalty to a warlord, you received a special Secondary mission to go in the deck provided to each player at the outset of the event. Every member of the Vox Localum had their own special secondary mission, and you could secure one of these to add/swap into your deck if you interacted with them the right way. This was my favorite addition this year, as it created a great collectible element for players to find while not adding too much cognitive load - you don't have to think about these all the time, you can just put them in your deck, then when you draw them you do what they say, score them, and you're done.

The downside is you can put these in your deck and never see them, especially if you only get one or two. And I think they were a little too hard to get ahold of during the campaign, though I did see players showing off a full set, which was cool.

During the aforementioned briefings you're given a faction goal in addition to the game's primary objective. This will often be vague - something like "collect skulls," "raise the beacons," or "erect shrines to the dark gods." It's largely on you as a player to figure out what this means, and when you report results to the Vox Localum, they'll ask if you won your game and if you accomplished this objective. You honestly kind of make these up - I decided that erecting Chaos Shrines meant doing Cleanse on central objectives, so I went with that. In one round we decided that while I claimed plenty of skulls, I'd thrown them all onto a pyre in the middle of the battlefield, inadvertently creating a beacon like my opponent wanted to create all along (he spent time charging the middle of the table to no avail). If I'm honest, I'd like these to be better defined. I like that you can take some freedom with them, but they often end up feeling either like you're just making up whatever reason you want for them to be a success or you're left wondering what the hell you're supposed to do.

The Story

The story itself was fairly solid this year. Generally speaking, nothing is decided before the event - there are some rough outlines of how things can go and some clear possible endpoints, but the staff meet up between each round to talk about what happened, review results, and figure out what happens next. It's very much organic in that respect, though there are obvious paths planned for some - Mara Venn explored alliances with both the Imperium Commander Morn and Kaelen Voss, and end the end wound up in the Imperial fold. This was the result of player choices and decisions, and she could have gone either way before her final conclusion. More on that in a moment.

Voss talked a big game about freedom and then ended up turning to the Dark Gods for help. Not a big shocker, and he killed the Warrant, who turned out to be a former serf of his desperate for glory and recognition from Huron but was never more than an eager aspirant. Neither was particularly impressive and Voss' new devotion to the dark gods was short-lived, as he was almost immediately shot in the face by Arathiel, who was there to retrieve a soul stone from a fallen former lover/leader from Voss' armor. The entire set of raiding operations had been a ruse, a ploy to draw attention so he could get close to Voss and reclaim his prize. This sounds cooler than it looked in practice - they really needed to have Arathiel stab Voss, not pretend to shoot him - but the concept was solid.

Morn emerged triumphant, and was reconsidering his faith after witnessing and bringing in Mara Venn and her faithful survivors, who were now en route to Terra on an Imperial ship. Of course, this is when we got the big reveal: Venn was a Genestealer Cultist all along, and her faithful a bunch of infected third-generation devotees.

This was probably the coolest aspect of the event, because Imperial players, led by a Custodes player in battlegroup 6 - fought very hard to convince Morn to listen to Venn and take her in. In doing so, they became responsible for the ship full of genestealer cultists headed for Terra. I got an extra chuckle out of this when I spoke to a fellow player named Jason at the baggage carousel in Houston after the event, and he mentioned that when he read her lore initially it read "exactly like the entry for the Reductress Saboteur." Said Custodes player was very unhappy to discover he'd unwittingly betrayed the Imperium, but I think it was a great moment to showcase how players can affect the narrative. Venn could have ended up siding with Voss or neither, or even not getting off the world. There were a number of very interesting ways that could have gone, and the reveal at the end was very well told. A number of players had a feeling she wasn't who she professed to be at the start, but few if any figured out she was a Genestealer in advance.

This was by far the strongest aspect of the event's story. The rest was more generic but told well enough. This year's Vox Localum had a lot more to do and that made a huge difference. They had clear agendas, changed loyalties, and were more interactive with players, making for a much more fun time.

The Setting

Ferrum's Wake was... a planet. We got one paragraph on it being a Forge World and there was one notable location - Emancipation Spaceport - but otherwise we were told much less about it this year. There was no map and even less visual flair and fewer descriptions than when players were fighting in the webway last year. That's a huge miss, as it kept the location from feeling real or substantial, even as it was about to be dragged into the warp. I think they still struggle in this regard, making the location and setting feel real and tying it concretely to the battlefields they're putting out.

Final Thoughts

This year's Grand Narrative was the strongest yet. Not in every aspect - the lack of custom primary missions was a big miss and some of the faction mechanics were a bit too wild - but many of the added elements were a welcome improvement and the games themselves played better on battlefields with actual terrain footprints. The key sticking point of the Grand Narrative continues to be a disconnect between the time and care put into the narrative and costumes versus the game you play on the table and how you interact with it all but many things improved this year and I'm hopeful they'll continue to learn the right lessons and make next year's event even better.

If you've never attended the Grand Narrative, it's worth giving it a shot - there's no one else able to run an event like this at the scale Games Workshop does right now and while some smaller events may be tighter or have more focus, there's something about having 300-plus costumed fans in a room, all invested in the outcome of a narrative campaign. It's one of the most fun events of the year, and the only one on which I regularly bring along my wife and son, because they're liable to have just as much fun as I do.
