Lore Explainer: The Rings of Power in Tolkien's Work

September 14th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Three Rings for the Elven-kings under the sky,

Seven for the Dwarf-lords in their halls of stone,

Nine for Mortal Men doomed to die,

One for the Dark Lord on his dark throne

In the Land of Mordor where the Shadows lie.

One Ring to rule them all, One Ring to find them,

One Ring to bring them all and in the darkness bind them

In the Land of Mordor where the Shadows lie.

- Epigraph to The Lord of the Rings
NB: This explainer post deals with solid canon from The Lord of the Rings, The Hobbit, and The Silmarillion, and other supplementary material written by JRR Tolkien. The TV series is separate semi-canon; I haven’t seen it, and I don’t feel it’s relevant to this particular discussion.

The One Ring - Credit Peter J Yost. CC BY-SA 4.0

As the name of the books suggests, the Rings of Power are at the very core of the Lord of the Rings’ narrative, but what are they? Where did they come from? What do they do? To answer these questions, we need to cast our minds back to the dying days of the First Age of Middle-Earth (we’ll discuss exactly what happened in each age in a different article; keep an eye out for it). Morgoth, aka Melkor, aka Middle-Earth’s Lucifer, finally did enough evil deeds to the men and elves of Arda that the rest of the Valar (best understood as kind of archangels - again, we’ll discuss the cosmology of Arda in another article) had enough and came to give him a damn good shoeing. They succeeded in beating up their errant cousin and pushing him through the Door of Night into “the void,” but in the process they made a real mess of the land of Middle-Earth, sinking the westernmost part (called Beleriand) beneath the sea. Oops. The Valar didn’t quite succeed in destroying all the evil, though. One of Melkor’s lieutenants by the name of Sauron (a Maiar) managed to escape and go to ground for a while, along with a whole lot of Orcs, some dragons, a bunch of Balrogs, etc. That’s right, the Big Bad in The Lord of the Rings got his start as a lackey of Middle-Earth Satan. 

In the interim, numerous realms sprang up across Middle-Earth: Elven, Dwarven, and Human. A polity of elves known as Gwaith-i-Mirdain inhabited an area west of the Misty Mountains known as Eregion, where they were ruled by the extremely handy artisan-lord Celebrimbor. Geographically, Eregion borders the Dwarven kingdom of Khazad-Dum/Moria, and the elves of Eregion cooperated closely with Durin’s folk to their mutual benefit - Celebrimbor crafted the magical doors of Moria (the same ones that the Fellowship find their way through by solving the “Speak Friend, and Enter” riddle). By the time of The Lord of the Rings, all that remains of Eregion is the ruins seen by the Fellowship as they travel south from Rivendell. 

After spending a good five hundred years laying low, Sauron came to the conclusion that the Valar had stopped looking for him, and so the heat was probably off. No longer fearing being beaten up and shoved into the void with his master, Sauron embarked on his grand scheme to dominate the world. Being a very powerful Maiar, Sauron had little difficulty corrupting the already-sort-of-corrupt men of the east and south (awkward implication alert) and bent them to his will. With these men under his control, he began the construction of his own realm in the east - the land of Mordor.

Ready to enact the next stage of his plan, Sauron put on a pretty face and went to introduce himself to the Elves, disguised, acting naturally, and going by the name Annatar (Gift Lord). The Elven ruler of Lindon, a mighty king named Gil-Galad, was not at all convinced by the Annatar disguise and told Sauron to hit the bricks and get lost. Undeterred, Sauron/Annatar visited the elves of Eregion and offered to teach them some special ancient elven crafting secrets for their next project. This offer was apparently enough for Celebrimbor to invite Sauron- I mean Annatar into Eregion. Since Annatar/Sauron was a master manipulator, he proposed the creation of magic rings that would help each of the species, bringing peace and prosperity to the various species of Middle-Earth. In secret, he mixed a bit of hocus-pocus into the creation process, meaning that the rings would be able to act on the desires of the wearer and twist them to Sauron’s benefit. Sauron’s ultimate trump card would be the creation of a master ring, which he alone would control. Wearing this master ring would allow him to activate the poison pill buried in each ring’s creation and dominate the mind of the wearer, turning them into his puppets.

The elves of Eregion made an unknown number of so–called Lesser Rings, both as trials and as practise, but when we’re talking about The Rings, we’re talking about the major rings.

As detailed in the poem above, if you add ‘em all up, twenty rings of power were produced through the collaboration of Celebrimbor and Sauron/Annatar, each intended to be presented to the rulers of kingdoms and sorted by species; so let’s have a look at them that way, shall we?

Galadriel. Credit: Rich Nutter

The Elves

Celebrimbor made the three elven rings by himself, with knowledge gained from Annatar, but without the Dark Lord ever having actually touched the rings or been personally involved in their creation. Because Sauron never actually handled the rings, his power over them is lesser compared to the rest of the rings; conversely, having learned about them, he was gripped with a very strong desire to possess the three. Since the rings act on the desires of their wearer, and what the elves desired most was to stop their decline, each of the Elven rings has the power to alter the flow of time and create a kind of stasis bubble that arrests or slows change, allowing the elves to live in peaceful elven enclaves, protected from the alteration of the outside world.

The uncorrupted nature of the three, combined with the insight of the elves in the first place, allowed them to react quickly after the completion of the One Ring; they heard Sauron’s voice in their heads and yanked the rings off tout-suite. What do you do with magic rings that perform a really desirable function when worn, but are talking to you inside your head? The elves chose to hide the rings in safe places until Sauron was apparently defeated at the Siege of Barad-Dur. With the owner of the One Ring turned into a kind of ghost, the Elven lords felt safe enough to put the rings back on and keep their enclaves lovely once more.

The three elven rings were:

Nenya - The Ring of Adamant or the Ring of Water - worn by the Elven Lady Galadriel and used to maintain the elven enclave of Lothlorien. When Galadriel wears the ring, it’s normally invisible; only Frodo (and presumably Gandalf, as another ringbearer) are able to see it. The power of Nenya centred around protection, preservation, and concealment from evil. 

Narya - The Ruby Ring, or the Ring of Fire - originally worn by Cirdan the Shipwright, the Lord of the Grey Havens, later given to Gandalf (who isn’t an elf, but that doesn’t seem to matter).

Vilya - The Sapphire Ring, or the Ring of Air - originally worn by the King of Lindon, Gil-Galad, and after his death at the hands of Sauron, worn by Elrond and used to maintain Rivendell.

Why would Cirdan hand over such a powerful artefact? When Gandalf was dispatched along with the rest of the squad of Wizards on their mission to defeat Sauron, he arrived at the Grey Havens. Cirdan, having the power of foresight, saw something in Gandalf and was moved to offer him the powerful ring, saying:

"Take this ring, master, [...] for your labours will be heavy; but it will support you in the weariness that you have taken upon yourself. For this is the Ring of Fire, and with it you may rekindle hearts in a world that grows chill." - J.R.R. Tolkien, The Lord of the Rings, Appendix B, "The Third Age"

From this we can assume that the power of Narya grants the wearer an increased ability to inspire hope and courage in others, which makes Gandalf an ideal bearer after all. Presumably ol’ Cirdan saw that too.

Medbury Dwarves - Completed Group - Credit Thundercloud

Dwarves

The Dwarf Lords received seven rings in total, six from Annatar, and one from Celebrimbor himself. Of these, we only really know details of the latter, known as the Ring of Thror. When Sauron sprung his trap, he found that the Dwarves were annoyingly resistant to his mental domination - possibly because, unlike men and elves, dwarves were not created by Eru Ilúvatar, but by the Valar Aulë. While he was unable to dominate the dwarves and therefore impose his will on them, the rings still worked for them.

The rings worn by the Dwarven Kings boosted their innate technical skill, ambition, and desire for wealth, making them avaricious and extremely wealthy, allegedly giving them the power to multiply whatever ores, jewels, and metals they mined. While that may sound like a positive outcome for the Children of Durin, you really have to be careful what you wish for, don’t you? See, amassing enormous hoards of treasures, gold, and jewels has a habit of calling down and attracting the only creatures that are as obsessed with gold and jewels as Dwarves: Dragons. It’s almost as if there’s an invisible treasure-o-meter that goes up the more you gather in one place, until ding ding, dragon time. One by one, the holds of the dwarven clans were attacked, ransacked, and eaten by the giant fire-breathing lizards, their holds serving as big dens for the kleptomaniac beasties. As a result, most of the Dwarven rings were destroyed when the dragons chowed down on their bearers.

The Ring of Thror was a little different, though, being given by Celebrimbor as a gift to King Durin III of Khazad-Dum and passed down to his heirs before gracing the hand of Thror, the King of Erebor, the Lonely Mountain. We’re all familiar with the arrival and subsequent conquest of the Lonely Mountain by the dragon Smaug - rather than being eaten like the other Dwarven Kings, Thror actually managed to escape and lived in exile, passing the ring to his son Thrain, the father of Thorin Oakenshield. Thrain and his kin moved to the Blue Mountains. However, Thrain grew increasingly obsessed with the lost wealth of Erebor (probably a result of the ring he was wearing), launching an expedition to reclaim the mountain; as the band of dwarves camped under the eaves of Mirkwood, Thrain disappeared. Gandalf later found that Thrain had been captured by agents of Sauron and taken to his hidden citadel at Dol Guldur, there he was tortured, the ring and finger cut off, and he was left to die - Gandalf found a much diminished and mentally incapable Thrain in the dungeons of Dol Guldur, from whom he received the map and key that acted as the catalysts for Thorin’s quest in The Hobbit.

Earnur, Last King of Gondor (otherwise known as Aragorn, King Elessar from Wish) - credit Warhammer Community

Men

The race of men received the greatest number of rings from Sauron, nine in total. We don’t really have any details about these particular rings; Tolkien kept it vague and didn’t elaborate all that much on the rings or their wearers - we know that three of their number were Numenoreans, while another (the only one with an actual given name, Khamûl) was an Easterling. The rings were given to kings, sorcerers, and mighty warriors, and through their power, the men all achieved wealth and glory. Lacking the insight of the elves or the alien mental fortitude of the Dwarves, these men were easy prey for the mental domination of Sauron, and quickly fell under his spell, losing themselves piece by piece, before becoming inhuman, invisible wraiths and Sauron’s greatest lieutenants - the Nazgûl, or Ringwraiths. 

Credit: Games Workshop

The One Ring

Sauron’s masterwork, the One Ring is a fairly unassuming ring - described as being a perfect circle of unadorned gold, appealing in its perfection of form, but not ornamented or set with any stones. Unlike the other rings of power, Celebrimbor and the elves had no input in its creation; Sauron fashioned the ring alone in the fires of the volcano Orodruin (aka Mount Doom) in the corrupted land of Mordor, and as Galadriel says in the prologue to Fellowship of the Ring: “Into this ring he poured his cruelty, his malice, and his will to dominate all life.” 

When Sauron slips the finished One Ring on his finger, it acts as a sort of antenna for his psychic domination, with the remaining Rings of Power acting as conduits, connecting Sauron to the other bearers. As mentioned before, the perspicacity and insight of the Elves enabled them to intuit what was happening and pull the rings off in time to avoid being mentally overwhelmed. The Dwarves, with their unusual minds and general resistance to psychic mumbo-jumbo, were largely unaffected by the domination power of The One Ring, and ultimately only the bearers of the Race of Men were fully captured and corrupted by the powers of the ring. 

When worn by mortals, the One Ring confers invisibility by moving the wearer into a wraithlike parallel world that ordinary creatures can’t see; conversely, wearing the ring renders the bearer extremely visible to other “unseen” creatures, like the Ringwraiths and Sauron himself. Owning the ring extends the bearer’s lifespan dramatically (Gollum was almost 600 years old when he died; Bilbo is quite spritely and appears relatively young at his 111th birthday party - an age at which Hobbits could be described as “pretty elderly”, and ultimately lived to 131 - and even then he didn’t “die”, but sailed west with Gandalf and Frodo) while causing a severe physical and mental dependence in the manner of an addict. Gollum, who spent 500+ years with the ring, is so dependent upon it that he can scarcely live without it; all his thoughts are utterly consumed with it. Bilbo owned the ring for a comparatively shorter time, and is able to give it up (with some difficulty) to his nephew Frodo. Even so, when Frodo brings the ring to Rivendell, Bilbo has a hard time keeping his desire in check (yes, that part in the film where Bilbo’s face goes all scary and he goes “Graaar!” is in the book). 

The One Ring manipulates its bearers, exerting a kind of psychic pressure on them, attempting to bend their will towards helping it return to Sauron. Ultimately, the ring “wants” to return to Sauron and be reunited with him. Despite being an inanimate object, in my opinion, the One Ring appears to have some kind of limited sentience (debates have raged on this topic since at least the 1960s), making decisions and taking actions (abandoning Isildur at a critical moment, abandoning Gollum at a critical moment) that display some kind of strategic thinking, but as I said, it’s an inanimate object, the only physical thing it can “do” seems to making itself fall off someone’s finger at an opportune moment. 

While its physical actions are extremely limited, its abilities to manipulate and poison the mind of the bearer are very much in line with Sauron’s own abilities as a master manipulator. If a normal human had worn the ring, they would probably have succumbed to the temptations in short order and marched immediately against Mordor, inadvertently delivering the ring to Sauron one way or another. The corrupting power of the ring is so singular that, had a powerful elf like Galadriel or Elrond worn the ring, they would still have been captured by its manipulations and gripped by the desire to become a dark lord themselves, as Galadriel herself says:

“And now at last it comes. You will give me the Ring freely! In place of the Dark Lord you will set up a Queen. And I shall not be dark, but beautiful and terrible as the Morning and the Night! Fair as the Sea and the Sun and the Snow upon the Mountain! Dreadful as the Storm and the Lightning! Stronger than the foundations of the earth. All shall love me and despair!”

 - The Fellowship of the Ring, Book II, Chapter 7 “The Mirror of Galadriel”

So if the corrupting power of the ring is so impossibly strong, why were Bilbo, Frodo (and Sam) able to bear and wear the ring without being corrupted? The answer is a little convoluted. Despite being a subspecies of Men, Hobbits aren’t possessed by any strong desire towards power, wealth, or greatness - their general contentedness (petty concerns and squabbles aside) means that the Ring doesn’t have as much to “latch onto”, giving the Hobbits a kind of natural resistance to its manipulative effect. That’s not to say that Hobbits aren’t tempted by the ring - When Frodo is out for the count at Cirith Ungol, timely intervention by Sam stops the ring from falling into the hands of the Orcs and he wears it on a chain around his neck, the ring acts on him and shows him visions as a mighty hero who destroys Sauron and becomes the world’s greatest gardener.

Even so, against Hobbit resilience, the corrupting effect of the ring is only delayed, rather than averted - Gollum was a Hobbit once, before centuries of exposure to the One Ring irreparably damaged his mind and body. Despite this damage, Gollum never turned into a wraith, but was able to endure the presence of the ring for five centuries.

Mount Doom and Barad-dûr, as illustrated by J.R.R. Tolkien

In the later parts of The Lord of the Rings, the Ring - perhaps being “aware” that it was coming closer and closer to Mordor and Sauron - begins to inflict a heavier physical and mental toll on Frodo, rendering him closer to Gollum and further from the untainted Sam (who bore the ring for a short time, but never actually wore it). The ring’s corrupting effect increases the closer the bearer comes to Mount Doom, becoming overwhelming. Indeed, at the critical moment, Frodo is unable to destroy the ring. In his letters, Tolkien explains that at the place of its creation the power of the ring would be so immense that no one would have been able to destroy the ring:

“I do not think that Frodo's was a moral failure. At the last moment the pressure of the Ring would reach its maximum - impossible, I should have said, for any one to resist, certainly after long possession, months of increasing torment, and when starved and exhausted. Frodo had done what he could and spent himself completely (as an instrument of Providence) and had produced a situation in which the object of his quest could be achieved. His humility (with which he began) and his sufferings were justly rewarded by the highest honour; and his exercise of patience and mercy towards Gollum gained him Mercy: his failure was redressed.”

- JRR Tolkien, Letter 246.

Ultimately, the ring is destroyed not by Frodo’s action, but by Gollum’s inability to let the ring go as the result of centuries of corruption and dependence. 

Once the One Ring is destroyed, the other Rings of Power lose their magic and become useless as well, and all of their effects begin to dwindle at once. Bilbo’s physical age rapidly begins to catch up to his real age, leaving him old and frail upon Frodo’s return to Rivendell. The elven rings cease to function, and their preservation of the elven refuges begins to falter, causing rapid depopulation as their inhabitants make their way to the Grey Havens and the ships to take them to the Undying Lands. A century after the destruction of the ring, Arwen goes to Lothlorien to die and finds it in ruins, silent and empty. The ringbearers (Galadriel, Bilbo, Frodo, Gandalf, and Elrond), no longer needed, depart the Grey Havens for the Undying Lands on the White Ship. Sam follows them later, before Cirdan (once a ringbearer), his task as the guy who operates the metaphysical ferry port at an end, builds the Last Ship and follows them.

Note: The nature of the ring itself changed quite dramatically between the first publication of The Hobbit and the conception of The Lord of the Rings. In early editions of The Hobbit, the One Ring is what it appears to be: a magic ring that conveys invisibility, serving as a plot contrivance to help Bilbo survive later dangers. Bilbo acquires the ring as a prize for besting Gollum in the game of riddles; Gollum gives up the ring willingly, shows the way out of the Goblin cave, and Bilbo continues on his merry way. During the writing of The Lord of the Rings, a decade or so later, Tolkien realised that, in the context of The Lord of the Rings, through its corruption, Gollum would never be able to give up the ring willingly, and so revised the original text. In the new (much more familiar text), Bilbo finds the ring and uses it to escape Gollum, who is trying to find and kill him. The Lord of the Rings reveals that the ring sensed the reemergence of Sauron in Dol Guldur and “abandoned” Gollum in order to hitch a lift with a new “victim”, one conveniently headed in the same general direction as Dol Guldur.
