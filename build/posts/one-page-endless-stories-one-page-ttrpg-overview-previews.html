One Page, Endless Stories: One Page TTRPG Overview & Previews

July 8th, 2026 via Goonhammer

I’m just gonna come out and say it. Tomes are getting too big. Every other wizard wants to brag about the supposed depth and complexity that their weighty volume brings to the adventuring party but, let’s be honest, shall we? They only manage to utilize those overstuffed bastards because they’ve employed some cheat at making them work. Whether that be an overworked and underappreciated homunculus or animated servant, a bound spirit, or ten thousand rainbow bookmarks. If they lacked these peripherals, these tomes would be more useful for applying concussions.

No, for me, the humble scroll is underappreciated. One page, two sides, and as many spells as one can fit on it. What’s not to like? Sure, we lack the supposed ubiquity of the tome, but is that really a loss? Do you need the granular details, or can you just work past them with a little imagination and somatic handwaving?

Speaking of which….

Also why are they ALWAYS on the HIGHEST SHELF POSSIBLE!?

Hello and welcome to the very first entry in a series of articles we are calling TTRPG ADVANCED. A sequel series of sorts to the previous TTRPG ESSENTIALS articles, each ADVANCED article will be focused upon presenting you a window into the weird and wild extremes open to you in this hobby, in the hopes that you only get weirder and wilder with your own experimentation to match. In short, TTRPG ADVANCED is written not to welcome you in, but to encourage further development of your skills, interest, and investment within the TTRPG hobby and Community to the benefit of both you, and any table you sit at.

As the overwrought paragraph of meta-narrative alluded to, today’s article will be focusing upon the underappreciated and truly incredible world of TTRPGS whose entire system of rules lives upon one sheet of A5 or A4 paper. We’ll be covering a vertical slice of this microverse and highlighting several systems that express a breadth of the strengths and capabilities of this wonderful format. As always, this article is not intended to be in any way exhaustive, but to invite you, the reader, into further exploration.

Let’s get to it.

One-Page TTRPGS

As initially described above, One-Page TTRPGS are typically designed to contain everything important within one sheet of A5 or A4 paper. Now sometimes (usually), this means using both sides of the sheet, but there are a few placemat style systems that are so succinct in their design as to require only one. Some of these systems might also have supplementary material that lives on another handout, whether that be maps, character sheets, etc, but the idea is that all the mechanics themselves live on that first sheet of paper.

Fantastically, and as you’ll see later in this article, this physical limitation is in no way a limitation on design, feel, theme, or gameplay.

One of the best known examples of this medium is Grant Howitt’s Honey Heist, which in itself has been lauded for its accessibility and vector as an entry point for many into the TTRPG hobby. For those of us that want something similar but play with a table full of freaks with deeply curated tastes, Tom Bloom’s Goblin with a Fat Ass serves a similar purpose.

Breakdown of Gameplay Styles

Now, whilst our first examples above might seem to curate a very specific sort of mechanically light, improvisationally heavy gameplay style, this isn’t the case for One Page TTRPGs. Some are dedicated to providing a more conventional TTRPG gameplay experience, with combat systems and recognizable resolution mechanics, though many of these find unique ways of remixing or flipping the usual script. Others are more interested in providing a journey inwards for one player; wherein they encourage them to engage with the system through a deeply personal exploration of a fantastical hypothetical. Similar to multi-page TTRPGS, some even focus even further into providing specifically curated experiences and gameplay; encouraging players to invent wholly new mythology, or traverse both mechanics and roleplay through conversational interaction.

The best thing is, those possibilities described above are just an extremely thin vertical slice out of the absurd spectrum of possibilities presented by this TTRPG medium. You’ll also notice that, as we explore several examples of One-Page TTRPGs below, there is absolutely no limitation in genre for this medium. Horror, fantasy, investigation, action, exploration, all of it lives just as floridly within One-Page TTRPGs as much as it does within their multi-page siblings.

Not even a flat and stationary object can limit your options for great games!

Traditional TTRPG Gameplay

These are the One-Page TTRPGs whose gameplay might feel most immediately familiar to those that have played campaigns or even just one-shots of some of the more popular multipage systems (D&D, V:TM, Lancer, etc). What’s different here is two-fold (or more, depending on the page layout).

Firstly, resolution mechanics will be as cleanly and succinctly described as possible, due to the limitations of the format. If something is left to interpretation here, it’s because the system expects and trusts your table to read between the cleanly defined lines and borders that the system has spent its scant space describing. An perfect example of this is how in Grand Armor, the game has detailed systems and tables for charting both the loadout, construction, and piloting of a player’s mech in combat, but advises GM’s and Players to collaboratively describe the world within which this combat scenario takes place without offering much mechanical underpinning beyond the same d12 resolution mechanic utilized for the rest of the experience. This isn’t an oversight or mistake, this is simply because the system and the designer both trust you and your table to understand and be able to engage with basic roleplaying.

Secondly, there will almost always be some sort of unique wrinkle to gameplay that carries forward the momentum of the system. In some cases this might be providing a physical manifestation of an abstract concept or pressure, such as Vertigo Rising’s use of a Jenga Tower to highlight the mounting pressure of failure whilst providing a physical totem for players to track just how fair or foul their mission might be going.

Grand Armor by Lone Archivist

You are pilots of Grand Armors – 50 ft. tall, 120-ton humanoid tanks that operate as mechanized suits. What role you play amongst the factions vying for power over and control of your solar system is up to you.  -Lone Archivist; Grand Armor itch.io Page

Described as a microlite tabletop roleplaying game, Grand Armor provides an intensely curated and expertly focused mecha roleplaying experience which balances the mechanical specificity of traditional gameplay with the creative adaptation and interpretation of a theatre of the mind experience. This game also includes supplementary material through Jovian Opera, which provides both additional mechanics, world-building, and a pre-written scenario.

Check it out here.

INSTANT BULLETS by DNGNCLUB

You are a hired gun, one of the most dangerous people alive, and you have one more mission before you retire. Too bad you’re gonna die…  -DNGN CLUB  Games; Instant Bullets Pg1

Channelling the action movies of the 90’s and early aughts, INSTANT BULLETS is designed to be a fast-paced and intensely lethal combat ttrpg where it’s breakneck momentum starts with character creation and doesn’t stop until those characters do. Utilizing 1d6 where other systems might demand a handful, this system is designed to propel players through scenes, violence, and decisions with equal speed.  In fact, because of the high chance of character death, the system even features a mechanic where instead of encouraging players to roll up a new character, they instead take on the role of a new unique boss enemy to oppose the other players. This ensures that the session keeps rolling even after escalating loss after loss, until your table reaches the final, bloody end.

Check it out here.

Vertigo Rising by Unknown Dungeon

You are Freelancers; part vigilante-hacker, part corpo-spy, part cyber-ninja. You’ve been hired by [REDACTED]  to infiltrate the monolithic skyscraper headquarters of a rival Zaibatsu and undertake a series of increasingly dangerous missions.  -Unknown Dungeon; Vertigo Rising itch.io page

A cyberpunk inspired heist one-shot TTRPG, Vertigo Rising is designed with the intent of allowing your table to experience the mounting pressure of a heist whilst encouraging them to consistently push their luck, despite the risks. In this system, the GM is encouraged to consistently Up the Stakes by rolling for complications to add to the mission whenever a player rolls odd instead of even, whilst asking the player to pull a piece from the standing Jenga Tower that represents the depreciating stability of their ongoing job. When the tower falls apart, so does the job, but until then (and even after) the players are encouraged to roll with the punches and consistently push their luck, without the GM providing the momentum death of flat failure. The job only ends when all characters are dead/trapped/left behind, or the survivors decide to walk away, and not a moment sooner.

Check it out here.

Solo-Journaling

Alright, but what if your table isn’t available and you want to continue exploring the TTRPG hobby on your own? You could go ahead and invest in one of the many longform Solo-TTRPGS such as the critically lauded Thousand Year Old Vampire by Tim Hutchings and you’re likely to have a great time. If you want a smaller or perhaps more curated experience to start off with instead though, One-Page TTRPGs have you covered.

The following titles are both examples of Solo TTRPGS that encourage inward exploration by way of personal storytelling and utilize both recognizable dice rolling and perhaps unfamiliar card-draw mechanics to help you define the boundaries and experiences of this story you’re telling yourself. These games are both a chance to grow and develop your personal storytelling skills, as well as possibly learn a thing or two about yourself in the process.

They Should Have Sent a Poet by Telling Games

Leaving Earth and its disasters behind, you search the cosmos alone. You encounter the impossible and unimaginable. It challenges the beliefs and training that brought you here. Humanity feels so far away. Science and language aren’t sufficient. You write: documenting your journey, or to secure your memories. Remember your mission, and who you are. Find what you were looking for. Or don’t.  -Telling Games; They Should Have Sent a Poet itch.io page

A game about the inner alchemy of a lone wanderer through the innumerable stars, They Should Have Sent a Poet is a game about inhabiting an impossible situation as someone that isn’t you, but whose personal, physical, and metaphysical journey might reflect you all the same. This isn’t a game just about a destination or a goal, but what it might make or demand of the one seeking them. The story you’ll experience is carried across three separate eras, where work, discovery, and personal growth propel your personal narrative forward.

Check it out here.

A Play list for the End of the World by Ludipe

The world is ending and everyone knows it. And I don’t mean it as in “a post-apocalyptic event is coming”. No. Everything will cease to exist in about 42 minutes. There’s only a 1% chance that won’t be the case, so don’t get your hopes up.

And you find yourself at an empty radio station with nobody in sight. Broadcasting a program while the world ends seems as reasonable as any other option. Is anybody listening? No way to tell, phones aren’t working. So you might be talking to yourself or to a big crowd looking for some company as the end draws near.

Will you play your favorite songs and ramble about them? Send a message of hope? Be goofy? Get intense about the meaning of life? What does anybody do in these situations? Well, it’s up to you.  -Ludipe; A Playlist for the End of the World itch.io page

Most of us listen to music. Most of us have favorite songs, and some of us maybe even go so far as to make playlists of those favorite songs. Maybe those playlists have themes that go beyond simple genre, and group together songs that hold a shared personal meaning. A Playlist for the End of the World asks you which of these songs, and what stories alongside them you might choose to share, if you knew the world was ending in 42 minutes. Just like the tracks you choose, the tone of the messages you share between them is entirely up to you, but the system utilizes a list of prompts, each of whom is determined by drawing from a shuffled poker deck to help give you a push in a particular direction. What story, what song, what words matter enough to you to share, knowing that they might be the last ones ever?

Decide for yourself by checking it out here.

Mythmaking

As with their longform siblings, some One-Page TTRPGs focus upon breaking the mold in new and interesting ways. One such type is that of Mythmaking TTRPGs, also known as world-builders. Instead of being focused upon the individual struggles of small groups of characters or factions, these games as you and your table to build and shape the fate of a brand new world. This typically involves drawing a map, collaborating and falling into conflict with those other grand architects at your table, and creating something wholly unique by the end of the experience.

Cosmoira by Cezar Capacle

A game designed for 1-6 players with no assigned GM role, Cosmoira guides your table through the shaping of a brand new world from inception (or First Spark, as referred to by the text itself), terraforming of the continents, the creation of manifestations of wonder, and the birth of its population, all the way until the coming of the First (or modern) Age. This game invites your table to work together to weave a shared world through collaborative and conversational storytelling.

Check it out here.

A Panoply of Pages

The best thing about the systems we’ve highlighted today, is that they’re simply drops in the bucket when it comes to the sheer depth of options available to you across the One-Page TTRPG format. There are so many more games like these that can be found by searching online, or perhaps specifically on itch.io using the One-page, micro-rpg, and one-page-rpg tags.

Somebody called me "Steampunk" yesterday so I think I might be retiring this outfit soon.

Speaking of...

All of the games highlighted within this article were entrants in the One-Page RPG Jam 2025 hosted by Unknown Dungeon (2026 opens for submissions July 12th 2026) . Game Jams like this one are the beating lifeblood of the indie TTRPG design scene, and are also an excellent place to find your next favorite system.
