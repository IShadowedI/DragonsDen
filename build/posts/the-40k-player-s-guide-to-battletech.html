The 40k Player's Guide to BattleTech

November 6th, 2026 via Goonhammer

When I saw BattleTech being played for the first time, my initial thought was, “wow that’s a ton of charts. This game must be even more complicated than 40k.”

What a fool I was.

I’ve been a competitive 40k player since mid-9th edition, with my fair share of GT top 4s and best in factions, and that competitive angle is how I approach new games. It’s been a touch over two months since I played my first learning game of BattleTech, and while this article is not about why you should play BattleTech (you should), I sincerely hope that’s the effect.

What this article is, is my honest perspective of the game from an outsider: my initial presumptions and how they bore out in reality, and a few tips for other like-minded gamers looking to expand their wargaming horizons.

Before I dive in, I should specify that I have only played Classic BattleTech, so this article will only be covering that. Alpha Strike is not something I’m currently familiar with, but I do have plans to try it out.

Ghost Bear Alpha Galaxy Ebon Jaguar (Cauldron-Born). Credit: porble

Presumption 1: The Complexity

It’s the first thing anyone sees when they come up to a BattleTech table. No, not the mechs, or the dice, or the map. The Charts. One for each mech. A cacophony of numbers and graphs and boxes with even more numbers and graphs and on and on and on.

Or so you think.

Yes, obviously these charts are real and initially daunting; but they are your friend, not your enemy. If you’re reading this, you are familiar with how much of a behemoth 40k is (or you’re my mom and you’re excited to support me. Hi mom). It’s hundreds of units with their own stats, bespoke rules, and combinations that are completely asymmetric with anything your opponent could be bringing. Learning the game is learning dozens of games in one big trench coat. Naturally, you would assume the same thing for BattleTech. After all, it’s the same venerable age of Warhammer, with almost as many units–far more if you count all the variants–why wouldn’t it be as hard to learn?

I have taught five of my friends to play BattleTech so far. None of them had ever played a wargame before. The teaching took about fifteen minutes before we hopped into a game. Two of them now own their own mechs.

The beauty of BattleTech is in its simplicity. Everything is settled with rolling two dice. Everything involving those two dice is either basic addition or subtraction. All of the charts on those sheets simply tell you what the numbers you rolled do, and it’s always the same on every mech.

For one unit in 40k, you’re looking at:

  • The base unit.
  • How many models in that unit are equipped with what weapons, and where are they in relation to the other models on the field.
  • Do they benefit from any auras or cover?
  • What any attached leaders do to add to or change them. 
  • What any potential strats could do to increase their lethality, speed, or durability.
  • What detachments could buff or change them.
  • What their army rule could do.

And then comparing that to your own unit, which has the same questions being asked.

Conversely, with BattleTech, you’re looking at:

  • The base unit.
  • What number do you need to meet or beat on your dice roll to hit with any weapon.
  • If you hit, where do you deal damage on their mech.
  • If you deal damage, does it get through the armor and crit anything?

That’s it.

Okay, it’s not entirely it–there’s minutiae like any other wargame–but the sheer amount of stuff you need to hold in your head at any given time is miniscule compared to 40k. There are no abilities you could forget. There are no reactive moves or stratagems you need to constantly play around. It’s just numbers. And it’s fun as hell.

An additional key piece to this complexity, and one that goes into my next point, is that the list of weapons is–comparatively–small, and the depth of the game comes from which weapons are equipped in what combination and how many are equipped. A medium laser is a medium laser. It’s never going to have more AP, or reroll hits sometimes, or randomly deal mortal wounds, or any number of other things weapons in 40k have stapled to them. Everything you ever need to know is on that one piece of paper in front of you (more likely a tablet/phone screen but you’re picking up what I’m putting down).

How To Paint Everything Battletech, Wolf's Dragoons, Alpha Regiment

Presumption 2: The Depth

Okay, I hear you thinking, but without all the reactivity and layered buffs, how does it have the same depth as 40k?

Like all things, the devil is in the details. Sure, medium lasers are all the same, but a mech with three medium lasers that moves 10 hexes is a much different beast than one that moves 5 hexes but boasts 6 lasers. It may be stating the obvious, but it is crazy how much of a difference one number makes between variants. When each of the 700+ mechs in the game has anywhere from two to thirty variants, you’ve found your depth.

This is also where I learned that everything in BattleTech is a trade-off. More speed generally means less armor and/or an XL engine, which is easier to kill. More guns means more ammo, which can cook off with an unlucky crit and cost you a mech. The Battle Value system working as a formula where every component costs something leads to a much tighter balance than the sometimes vibes-based points in 40k.

This depth is further compounded by building a lance. Standard games are anywhere from eight to ten-thousand Battle Value, which roughly translates to four to six mechs. Which mechs you add and what you want their roles to be is a delightful optimization puzzle I’m still exploring two months in. This also leads me into…

Presumption 3: The Cost

Coming from 40k where an army will run you anywhere from a PS5 to a used car, I initially assumed BattleTech would be the same and I would struggle to afford both.

Imagine my surprise when a box of six mechs was slightly cheaper than a single Space Marine Captain.

The real problem is that individual mechs are around $7 on ebay and you enter a ‘why not, it’s only $7’ phase, and then you blink and you own thirty-five mechs. And maybe there’s a few more on the way. And maybe a few of those are mechs I already have but want to paint in a different scheme. It’s fine. I can stop any time.

Clan Heavy Battle Star. Credit: Jack Hunter

What I've Learned

As a knights player, I immediately gravitated towards the mechs with the biggest guns. I had images of dominating the battlefield in the same way a Knight Tyrant might, turning mechs to molten heaps in a single volley.

A few games in, I learned probably my single greatest lesson: mechs are simultaneously impossibly durable and made of dust. It’s a sort of quantum durability that depends entirely on your own desperation. Did you put the entire arsenal of three mechs into one lightly armored scout? It’s walking out of there with some chipped paint. Did you put a throwaway laser into your opponent’s biggest, scariest mech? Headshot, pilot rolls snake eyes and gets knocked out.

You see, BattleTech isn’t actually a wargame. It’s a slapstick comedy. It’s a game where stupid shit happens all the time and it rules. Someone’s emotional support small laser rolled a TAC (through armor critical) and hit the one in twelve chance to blow up my torso ammo, instantly turning my mech into a mushroom cloud? That’s BattleTech, baby. Did my commander mess up his combat drop and scatter completely off the board? You bet your ass he did.

Jokes (that aren’t really jokes) aside, there’s a few core lessons I’ve learned that you can actually take away from this article. To a seasoned BattleTech vet these might be obvious, but I assure you these lessons were learned in blood and oil.

  • Face your mechs where you need them for the next turn. I lost so much movement–which in this game is your prime defense–to needing to turn my mech because I was careless with how I ended my move. Using terrain to my advantage was something I brought from 40k and was easy to plug into my mental model of the game, but paying attention to my back and sides was new.
  • It is way harder to shoot things than you would expect. The target number for a successful hit can very easily get to ten plus. While this is often mitigated by there being no real cost to simply trying (lasers have infinite ammo so as long as you don’t overheat, just keep on blasting) the inability to reroll can be brutal. That easy shot seven hexes away rapidly becomes not so easy because you ran, they ran, and there’s a tree in the way. The numbers change and they change in big ways. There’s no plus or minus one to hit here; you can go from auto-hitting an immobile opponent to hitting on 12s within a turn.
  • Kill the little guys. That big beefcake on the other side of the board isn’t going down easy (unless he does, see above) but those little bastard locusts will get behind you and shoot you with way too many lasers and then scamper off. If you get the chance to blast them, blast them with everything until that section of the map is glass. 
Vedette. Credit: porble

I am by no means an expert (yet), but it’s safe to say BattleTech has bewitched me, body and soul. I’ve gone from learning how to pilot just one mech in a teaching game to being insufferable about teaching it in my group chats. Hopefully this has piqued your interest, and you can start exploring the massive catalogue of BattleTech articles on here written by much smarter people than me.

Until next time.
