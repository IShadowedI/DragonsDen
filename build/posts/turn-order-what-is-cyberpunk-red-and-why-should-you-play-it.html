Turn Order: What Is Cyberpunk Red, and Why Should You Play It?

September 22nd, 2026 via Goonhammer

The Cyberpunk universe is suddenly everywhere at the moment, be it the video game, the Edgerunners anime, or the many spin-offs and crossovers those media have spawned.  But this is Tabletop Battles, so maybe you're aware that all of this stuff actually originates from an RPG of the same name.  That RPG had a big re-release just a few years ago, and is still going strong with regular updates and content, including some recent additions based upon the aforementioned spin-offs.  It’s also really good.  So, what’s it all about and why should you give this game a go?  Well just slot this chip, choom, and let the braindance begin.

History of the RPG

Cyberpunk 2020 cover art, from R Talsorian games

The original Cyberpunk RPG was released by R Talsorian games way back in 1988, mere years after the term itself had been coined to describe a new wave of science fiction exploring themes of high tech and alternate culture.  The game’s designer, Mike Pondsmith, was heavily influenced by the novel Hardwired by Walter John Williams, amongst other now-familiar staples of the genre such as the film Blade Runner.  Shortly afterward a second edition followed, titled Cyberpunk 2020, which for most fans became the definitive version of the game, and still retains an avid player base.

A third edition of the game was released in 2005, using a completely different game system, but was so negatively received that it is officially non-canonical.  Let's move on!

Cyberpunk Red was released in 2020, still under original creator Mike Pondsmith's auspices, and offered not only an evolution of the much loved 2020 rules, but also an expanded future timeline with a somewhat grittier vision, bringing the game into a new near-future of 2045.

The Cyberpunk Universe

Cyberpunk red Night City 2045 cover art, from R Talsorian games

Most people have their own idea of what cyberpunk means, but as far as the universe of the RPG is concerned, this is a world in which international political entities have lost much of their power to mega-corporations.  The gap between the richest and poorest in society has reached new extremes, with those on the corporate paycheck receiving all the benefits of modern living and privatised services, while those at the bottom of society live in run-down and lawless urban hells at the mercy of street gangs.

Alongside these economic and political developments, technology has massively accelerated in certain areas, most notably cybertechnology and biotechnology.  The human body is modifiable and changeable to an almost limitless degree.  Artificial eyes, limbs, and internal organs are commonplace, as much for fashion and an edge on the mean streets as for any medical need. 

Finally there is the Net.  In Cyberpunk Red the Net has fallen from its days of a global internet of the kind we all know and enjoy, as an onslaught of terrifying killer AI programs necessitated its fragmentation into LANs protected by advanced AI firewalls.  But for those with the right skills, hacking is still one of the best ways to get data, money, and control.  Just don’t get yourself killed by Black ICE.

Cyberpunk has its own geographical epicentre of these themes, the most extreme example of all things Cyberpunk: Night City.  While nominally part of the fragmented near-future USA, Night City is a de facto independent state, ruled by a convoluted city council composed of private and public interests, and free of many of the national laws and taxes.  A sprawling megalopolis, Night City is home to unnamed millions and a rare few who somehow emerge from the teeming masses.

So what about the player characters?  The PC’s are Edgerunners, guns-for-hire who make their living in the cracks and shadows of society, taking the work too dirty and dangerous for others, living for money and fame, for as long as they can.  Whether you’re a street kid with big dreams, an ex-corp with a grudge, or just someone with a unique skill who stumbled their way into the life, you know the only things that matter in this world are your rep, your edge, and The Rules:

  • Attitude is everything
  • Style over substance
  • Live on the edge

How Does It Play?

Interior art from the Cyberpunk Red rulebook, from R Talsorian games

Basic Mechanics

Cyberpunk Red is a relatively simple game, with the vast majority of actions performed with a simple D10 skill check.  Each character has a set of attributes, and a set of skills, and ranks in both are combined with a D10 roll in an attempt to beat a Difficulty Value, or DV, which might be a specific number defined in the rules, or a value set by the GM to reflect the relative difficulty of a task.  If the action is directly opposed by an NPC, e.g attempting to persuade them to do something, or trying to hit them in melee combat, then the GM makes a roll using the NPC’s skills, and the required DV for the PC to beat becomes the GM’s roll.  This is a very intuitive and effective catch-all system that is easy to pick up quickly.  The only other dice you’ll need are a few D6, for rolling damage in combat.

Characters and Roles

In Cyberpunk, the player’s class or archetype is called their Role, and there are ten to chose from.  These include Cyberpunk’s equivalent of the fighter, the Solo; its bard, the Rockerboy; and the likes of Techs, Netrunners, Fixers, and others whose particular skills make them an asset in the near-future underworld they inhabit.

There are multiple methods for generating characters' stats and skills, from simple plug-and-play options to complete custom builds, so GM’s and players can work this out as they please.  A core part of character creation is the PC’s Lifepath.  This is a summary of the character’s family background and journey to the point of becoming an Edgerunner.  It is a really important part of the player’s role in brining their character to life, and an invaluable tool for the GM to get hooks for personalising in-game stories for the player.

The final piece of each Role’s kit is their Role Ability.  This is a single powerful skill that encapsulates the character’s mastery of their field.  The Solo for example, has multiple different combat benefits that they can activate and switch between at any time.  The Rockerboy can convert NPC’s into fans and then exploit their fandom.  The Nomad can call on customised or exotic vehicles as and when needed.

As a result of their kits, characters in Red are very much good at the things they are supposed to be.  The Solo can easily dispatch a gang of goons or security on their own; the Fixer can source improbable and scarce items (or find someone who knows how to get them); the Netrunner can compromise a secure network and disable its security systems, and the Rockerboy can charm the pants off whatever they set their desires on.  This tends to lead to feel-good situations for players, who can live out their character fantasy and contribute in the way they expect.

Combat

Red’s combat mechanics are mostly quick and simple, with only certain specialisations having a lot of mechanical depth.  Initiative is rolled once at the beginning of combat and then persists through it, and PC’s with high Reflex stat or Solo abilities will normally go first.  Combat rounds are three seconds long, and players may make two actions, a move, and then some other kind of interaction, be it making an attack of some kind, or operating a device, etc.  There are plenty of tactical nuances to combat, such as earlier-acting players able to hold actions (“I’m going to aim down this corridor and shoot the first thing that appears”) and PC’s and NPC’s alike having access to combat abilities that can limit other actions, such as suppressive fire that forces combatants to take cover.

Most weapons can be fired once or twice in an attack, and do damage in the 2d6 to 4d6 range. If two or more 6’s are rolled, a critical injury is caused, which does additional damage and an additional random effect, including extreme results like blowing limbs off.   Armor worn by players and NPC’s reduces the amount of damage taken, and is quite strong, with the starting armor given to PC’s able to shrug off the worst of a lot of small arms fire.  And taking cover is very effective, with shoot-and-move tactics making mobility a significant asset.  As a result, Red leans toward spike damage as the most impactful part of combat.  When players or NPC’s cause critical damage, combats can swing suddenly and dramatically.

Melee combat has some of the greatest options, with the Martial Arts skill having a wealth of unique style-based abilities that can really dominate action economy, while Brawling has powerful grappling attacks that can immobilise and restrict other combatants.  Most melee attacks ignore significant parts of armor protection, making them feel relatively powerful.  Meanwhile, the Evasion skill can be very powerful in avoiding all manner of attacks, with players who are built right able to ‘bullet dodge’ multiple attacks against all but very lucky NPC’s.  This ability is so good that it is often house-ruled to tone it down a bit.

Advancement

The primary method of advancement in Cyberpunk Red is via Improvement points, which are awarded in chunks after a session or adventure is completed.  They reflect a combination of task success, roleplaying, and team-work between the PC’s.  Characters can chose to invest the Improvement Points they have gained from each session into either individual skills (the lower they are the easier they are to improve) or to raise their Role Ability.  The latter is where the real power improvements are generally found, with increases improving not just their mechanical aptitude but their status as an exponent of their craft.  Players who attain the higher Role ranks are on their way to being legends, with abilities that let them call on favours from public personages, play gigs to stadiums full of people, or make news stories around the country.  But Red has relatively slow advancement, and reaching such dizzy heights will take a long time.

Gear-wise, players can find many ways to get hold of better guns and armour, and to customise and upgrade them.  A Fixer can source you military-grade gear through the black market, while the Tech Role's Maker ability lets them modify and invent equipment to create powerful new gear, working with the GM to come up with new stat lines and abilities.   The other option is cyberware, which can provide large power spikes by pumping PC's raw stats or by granting new abilities.  Fancy inbuilt nightvision?  A monofilament whip built into your finger?  Bullet-proof plating under your skin?  Skill chips that allow you to become a moderate practitioner of any art?  The possibilities are enormous, but come at the potential cost of Humanity.

Humanity is Red’s mechanical expression of the mental health and outlook of the player.  It starts at the same level as the player’s Empathy stat, and is modified (usually downward) by various kinds of trauma that the PC experiences.  By far the most common and significant impact on Humanity comes from installing elective cyberware.  The bigger the installation, the bigger the loss, and when Humanity reaches very low levels, PC’s can suffer from the psychological and dissociative effects of having discarded their meat body for power.  Players can offset this problem by paying for therapy in their downtime, but it can be a tricky balancing act to manage.  Humanity can be a bit of a contentious subject among Cyberpunk players, with many feeling that it is a bit of black-and-white system in relation to the nuanced subjects of mental health and self-perception.  I think the Edgerunners anime did a fantastic job of showing how this creeping erosion of self can work in practice, but unquestionably, Humanity exists in Red not least as a mechanical check on simply chroming your way to easy power.

Supplements and Expansions

Cyberpunk Red Edgerunners mission kit art, from R Talsorian games

Red has had a steady stream of updates since its release, including monthly DLC via the R Talsorian website.  Most of this content has been collected and re-released with other new material in the Interface Red books, which are now up to Volume 5.  These are great places to start to expand your game world, as they are full of new gear, settings, and expanded mechanics.  That said, I think you might find some content more or less interesting or relevant for your own campaigns, so I would suggest checking out what’s in each book before you buy.

In addition to this, there have been several full supplements, including most recently the most comprehensive look at Cyberpunk’s flagship location, Night City 2045.  If you want to run your campaign in Nighty City, this and the Danger Gal Dossier, which includes detailed guides to the most significant gangs and power-brokers of the city, have you covered.  The best expansion for adding new gear and cyberware to your game is Black Chrome, which has everything from monofilament katanas to cyberpets, and food processors to attack helicopters.  For the anime and video game fans, there is the Edgerunners Mission Kit, which is a very slick expansion that provides rules for new technology and campaign play in the 2077 era.  Most notably this includes rules for quickhacking, which gives Netrunners the ability to hack opponents in combat.  Finally, Single Player Mode gives you all the tools to run Red solo, though many of its random tables and 'quick & dirty' rules (Netrunning in particular) will be of great interest and use to all GM’s.

Why Should You Play It?

Cover art from the Cyberpunk red worldbook, from R Talsorian games

A unique, incredibly immersive world, and a lightweight, evocative rules system are two huge reasons why Cyberpunk Red is an easy recommendation.  Cyberpunk also offers players the chance to play characters on the greyer end of the moral spectrum.  No one has to act the hero here.  You can indulge your impulses and excesses, and enjoy dealing with the consequences too.  I’ve had multiple PC’s in my games become addicted to drugs, as well as suffering the dehumanising psychological effects of excessive cyberware modification.  These aspects of the game make playing a campaign in Red so much fun, with your character development about much more than just pumping stats.

Fundamentally, Cyberpunk Red has a powerful focus on roleplaying and immersing yourself in its world, rather than its mechanics.  Players are encouraged to try cool stuff, act like badasses, and aim to be legends.  Social skills and interactions with NPC’s are every bit as important as combat skills (I’ve run plenty of cyberpunk adventures where not a shot has been fired) and in the huge open world of the near-future, players are limited only by their imagination (and maybe the odd dice roll) in how they solve the problems you throw at them.

For the GM, Cyberpunk is a game that works really well for one-shots and intro games.  Structuring an individual session as a single task, job, or heist that the players have been paid to complete very much fits the theme of the game world.   It also works well with player groups who change from session to session, as each group of Edgerunners can be assembled afresh by a Fixer for each new job.  And as player progression is not linear, groups of mixed experience can play together perfectly well.

The Cyberpunk universe is such a unique and fun world, and Cyberpunk Red does a great job of bringing it to life and making it easy to enjoy, so I heartily recommend it to all wannabe Night City legends.
