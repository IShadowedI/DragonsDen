[40K] Hammer of Math: Charge Basics in 11th Edition

July 6th, 2026 via Goonhammer
In Hammer of Math, we look at various statistical concepts in Warhammer 40k, looking at how to evaluate different options mathematically and understanding how those probabilities and statistics affect your games. In this article we're looking at coherency rules in 11th edition and some unique geometric quirks which can help you in ways you might not have expected.

With a new edition comes a new opportunity for us to brush up on older topics and revisit some of the basics of the game. For new players it'll be crucial information to know as you start on your journey to the heights of competitive play; for established players it will be a helpful refresher on how probability works and can impact your games and decision making.

On that note, the charge roll may be the single most important die roll in Warhammer 40k. Closing the gap and trapping an enemy. Making a key charge will often swing an entire game of 40k, and as a result players will go to great lengths to make those charge distances as short as possible.

Dice Math with 2D6

When it comes to charge rolls, you'll typically be rolling 2D6 to determine the maximum distance you can move. This creates interesting consequences: Unlike on a single die, where the results (1-6) are uniformly distributed, i.e. equally likely to happen, with 2D6 your results have a triangular probability distribution that looks more like a normal distribution or bell curve. This is because when you roll 2D6 and add the result, while there are 36 possible results, there are more ways to make certain combinations than others. For example, there are six ways to roll a seven (1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4, 4 and 3, 5 and 2, 6 and 1), making a seven the most common results, occurring 1/6 of the time, or 16.67%.

This also means that results near 7 - specifically 6 and 8 - will also occur more often than the more extreme results. If you've ever played Settlers of Catan, you'll be intimately familiar with this phenomenon, and why players rush to take those hexes on the board. When it comes to charge distances, we're looking for the odds of rolling a result or higher, so we can just sum the odds for each result that's equal to or above the result we rolled.

At least they get to start at 9"

The most common charge distance we'll have to worry about is 9", as that's the distance you'll need to roll to make a charge out of deep strike in most cases - even though you drop just outside of 8", you still need to roll high enough to touch a model base-to-base, so you still need to roll a 9+ to make the charge.

Re-rolls

When you re-roll a charge, you'll typically have to re-roll both dice. Having a re-roll will improve your odds of generating a result. When you do this, your odds of making the charge improve to:

P(Charge) = P(success) + P(success) * [1 - P(success)]

So your chances of making it are equal to the sum of your chances of initial success, plus your chance of making it on the re-roll, which is your probability of success multiplied by the probability of that initial failure, or 1 - your chance of succeeding. For a 9" charge, your initial chance of making it is 28%, so if you have a re-roll, then your odds of making that charge before any dice are rolled are about 48%. That's a little worse than 50/50, and should help underscore that even with a re-roll, making those kinds of charges is literally worse than a coinflip. What this means is that you shouldn't be risking the outcome of the game on these charges unless you absolutely have to - if not rolling a 9+ will lose you the game, you should really think about what will happen if you fail and planning for that accordingly.

The Psychological Factor

It won't feel like 50/50 most of the time - humans struggle to accept pure randomness. We typically fall into things like the Gambler's Fallacy, believing that random events must balance out. There's some truth to this - things tend to regress to the mean over time, but independent events will never influence each other - it doesn't matter how many times you just rolled snake eyes, your odds of rolling it again are the same each time. But when you're re-rolling 7s into 6s for your charges and your opponents are nailing three 9+ charges in a row, it's going to feel like bullshit.

Evaluating Charges and Mitigating Risk

Failed charges can cost you the game. The good news is that when you're setting up for your charges in 11th edition, you'll have a few ways you can reduce the risk of a failed charge. Some things to consider:

  • Rapid Ingress. Rapid Ingress is a powerful tool, letting you set up anywhere more than 8" away from an enemy unit, after which you can move in your own turn and close the gap for an easier charge. For units which move 6", this will mean going from a 9+ to a 3+, which is almost guaranteed. Note that with the new Hidden rules, if you set up more than 15" away from enemy units or 12" with Go to Ground placing, you can also avoid being shot in the enemy units as you won't have made a ranged attack yourself and get the benefits of being Hidden for a turn.
  • Multiple Charge Targets. The new rules let you roll your charge distance first, then choose eligible targets within that range and decide who to charge from among those. Setting up with multiple targets will help you keep your options open, so even if you don't reach your primary target, you may still be able to make it to some other valuable secondary target which is closer.

You may also come on a situation where you have multiple charges to execute but only a single re-roll to use via the Command Re-roll Stratagem. In those cases I'd recommend you save your CP for "high leverage" re-rolls. That is, re-roll the charges you need the most because they're going to provide the biggest payoff for your investment. Unless there's some other reason to sequence your charges differently, start with those most important charges, as getting them out of the way early will free you up to make better decisions later - if you just make that 9" charge out of deep strike, you can save the CP for another charge that's more likely to succeed.

Battle-shock Rolls

Battle-shock rolls just follow this same math, using a 2D6 distribution, just with 6+ or 7+ being your most common targets. Knowing these odds also helps, as you can generally count on a unit making more than half of their Battle-shock rolls if they need to hit a 7+, and the chances of a unit passing two 7+ rolls is about 25%.

Final Thoughts

This is definitely a topic we've covered before but one that's worth revisiting once an edition or so to refresh people's memories on the topic. Charges are a key part of the game and understanding the math behind them will help you make smarter decisions around when and how to bank your entire game on a charge.
