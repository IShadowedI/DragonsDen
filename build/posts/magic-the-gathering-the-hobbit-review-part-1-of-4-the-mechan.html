Magic: The Gathering The Hobbit Review, Part 1 of 4 - The Mechanics

August 4th, 2026 via Goonhammer
This week, from a hole in the ground, out steps a Hobbit. The Hobbit, in fact, as we follow the tale of Bilbo, Gandalf, and frankly too many dwarves to reclaim lost glory in a new Universes Beyond set. In our starting chapter, we'll begin the tale with mechanics both new and old, exploring synergies that may arise from these thrilling designs. While The Hobbit is a small set—as befits master Bilbo Baggins—it's rife with big ramifications for formats like Standard, Commander, & Modern.  We're here to offer our thoughts on how their introduction will affect these formats, and the set as a whole.

Magic: the Gathering The Hobbit will release to Magic: the Gathering Online and Arena on August 11th, and to the tabletop on August 14th.

New Mechanics

Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Storied/Enduring Story

Storied is a new spin on the Ascend/City's Blessing mechanic that debuted in Rivals of Ixalan, rewarding players for the rest of the game once they meet a specific criteria. For any card with Storied, it checks for controlling 3+ artifacts, legendaries, and/or Sagas (e.g. Historic permanents). Once you've done so, for the rest of the game you're treated as having an Enduring Story, which cannot be removed in any way, and gives many cards in the set additional effects.

Saffgor: Let's get it out of the way, Storied is about as free as a mechanic gets, in any constructed format. Simply having a Treasure token, the (usually) Legendary Creature with Storied itself, and any other card will secure an Enduring Story for the rest of the game, so it's best to evaluate these cards as always having it turned on. That said, this is no small feat in Limited, and likely won't be on until the mid-game, if you're not able to build around it. Crucially, a ton of these cards are absolutely terrible without an Enduring Story, so you do ultimately need to build around it a little bit, with things like Legendary Lands, if you've got a Storied card in the Command Zone.

BPhillipYork: This is like Ascension, and works functionally the same. It doesn't literally create an emblem, but it might as well (and honestly should; it would clear things up, especially since nothing interacts with emblems.) In any case, once you hit the threshold, i.e. you have a storied permanent and you have 3 Historic (though it doesn't use this term) permanents, then you have an enduring story.  This is probably the most trivial of these sorts of static abilities to reach.  Most decks will do it as soon as they have a storied permanent, so as a balancing act most of the abilities aren't too strong.  But Dain, Lord of the Iron Hills for example is pretty decent, especially since it's a tax that your opponents can never get rid of.  Anyway, because of how complex it is, and how easy it is to achieve, and because it essentially creates an emblem without actually creating one, I don't like new mechanics like this that just keep stacking up the sort of cognitive tax of playing the game.  Commander board states are complex enough as it is.

FromTheShire: I'm in agreement that this is largely a solid mechanic that feels like it was implemented a little clunkier than it needed to be. Even Wizards called out in their mechanics article that the shorthand for these categories already exist as Historic, so I'm not sure what the reasoning for not using it was. Because of how easy it is to turn on basically everywhere outside of Limited I'm happy that they went largely conservative. Thematically it fits perfectly with the set as well. Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Recruit

Recruit is a more heroic take on Connive, with the same sequence of drawing, discarding, and checking to see if the discarded card was nonland. If it was, you create a 1/1 Human Soldier token, rather than receive Connive's +1/+1 counter. In this way, the effect helps you go wide instead of tall, and should play out quite differently despite a similar baseline filtering effect.

Saffgor: I am a huge fan of Recruit, due in no small part to the designs it appears on within the set. I would contend that a spare body is worth more than a counter, with the capabilities to use it in everything from go-wide token strategies, to that of aristocrats. Recruit is 'priced' similarly to Connive on a lot of cards, in terms of design budget, and I think that will lead to a number of stand-out cards with the mechanic across multiple formats.

BPhillipYork: The ability to create tokens is inherently a strong one with many many synergies across deck types.  Adding on some nice card selection is useful, if not amazingly strong.  It's kind of nice it isn't leveragable to also benefit from dumping creatures to your yard to cheat out, though you can choose to do that and not get the 1/1 Human Soldier.  It's particularly strong for decks that have one time per turn triggers they need to hit, like Welcoming Vampire.

FromTheShire: I love getting effects you already want with a loot or rummage tacked on already, and now I also get an extremely useful 1/1 as a further rider? I really hope this one becomes at least deciduous. Even the creature typing is great. Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Hone Counters

A new type of counter appears in The Hobbit, which are placed exclusively on Equipment. They provide an extra +1/+0 to the equipped creature apiece, and are retained even if the wielder dies. In this way, it's an incredibly interesting means of allowing Equipment to scale over time, passed between a lineage of noble warriors.

Saffgor: Hone counters are a cool way for Boros, predominantly, to have scaling 'value' in a way in which the color pair is allowed. Things get bigger over time, allowing you to push more damage, but Hone isn't as valuable as many of the other means of scaling for formats like Commander. You may see it appear on cards in the Command Zone, or on one very specific Equipment, but I think it's applied too conservatively across cards in the set. This is exactly the kind of mechanic to appear again in Commander-specific product though, hopefully with fewer constraints.

BPhillipYork: This ability is fine, a little meh, but it just stacks up to make Equipment even better, which there's nothing wrong with.  It's not like Equipment is normally too strong.

FromTheShire: Equipment along with Aura based Voltron strategies tend to get phased out as the power of a pod increases, with more efficient threats and a shift towards spell based strategies. This feels like it can maybe shift that window back half a step or so and let some decks punch above where they currently sit, and I'm a fan. At the end of the day I love a good, clean, people dying to combat damage kind of game.

Returning Mechanics

Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Adventure

Adventure is back, appearing on creatures & artifacts in the set in order to offer greater flexibility in formats like Limited.

Saffgor: Adventure is functionally deciduous at this point, and is about as common as things like Vehicles, or Enchantment Creatures. It's a good mechanic, straight up, and only gets better the more that effects are printed which key off of things like casting two spells, Instants/Sorceries, or even cards from exile/anywhere but your hand. Not hard to see why it's a mainstay of modern set design.

BPhillipYork: Adventure is is a solid mechanic that is sort of a modal choice and really just buffs relatively simple cards.  It plays so well with cast from other than hand, and also decks that want to store up resources somewhere outside the game.  Funny to me that prepared didn't show up, but of course Hasbro is dumping out sets and mechanics so quickly that I'm sure there's no cross-communication between teams.

FromTheShire: Your cards essentially being two cards is great, who could have known? Adventure cards are always big hits and see tons of play, the flexibility and value is unbeatable. There will absolutely be at least a handful of playable we're going to see in constructed for years to come. Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Amass (Goblins)

Amass returns with a new type in tow, this time in the form of Goblins. That's in addition to Zombies, Orcs, & Slivers, which means you could in theory wield a shockingly cosmopolitan Army token.

Saffgor: We're getting to a point where Amass is able to be flexed as an effect using all the different types, and I'm interested in seeing how The Hobbit could lead to Amass being an archetype on its own.

BPhillipYork: Amass is a solid mechanic; you probably get the most value out of repeatedly sacrificing Armies to get death and ETB effects and the sacrifice value, at least in Commander, since a vanilla 8/8 or whatever just isn't a big deal.

FromTheShire: Amass is interesting in that it tends to always be useful, but in varying ways depending on the format. Depending on what other riders it has it may be a useful secondary ability when say your Orcish Bowmaster also happens to make something that beats down, or instead in Commander you may be trying to launder away instances as fast as you can create them for value. It's good to see it back, and again it fits naturally with the set. Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Gift

Gift returns from Bloomburrow, appearing on a single card. Regardless, it's a welcome return, and another arrow in Jolly Gerbils' quiver.

Saffgor: This is the kind of thing they've done recently that I'm all for, in bringing back a mechanic for a flavor win that might not otherwise be seen in years. More Gifts, please!

BPhillipYork: I really like the way gift works for multi-player games.  It's inherently sort of political, and allows for some meaningful favor trading at times, i.e. who will you gift?  I'd really like to see mechanics like this showing up in game all the time, and more mechanics like this coming into existence.

Next Time: The Set’s Multicolor & Colorless Cards
