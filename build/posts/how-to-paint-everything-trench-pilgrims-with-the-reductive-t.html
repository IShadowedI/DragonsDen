How to Paint Everything: Trench Pilgrims with the Reductive Technique

June 5th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Before I begin this article, I want to mention that this warband project wouldn't be what it is without the work of John Blanche. Not only for his collaboration with The Army Painter to produce the Masterclass paints I used, but more importantly, for the inspiration his work and teachings provide. The grimdark world owes it all to him. Thank you, John. May we continue to paint the grimdark forevermore.

Today, we’re back with another How to Paint Everything article. This time, we’re taking a look at the Trench Pilgrims from Trench Crusade. We’ll be taking you through the process of building and painting up the models, with a particular look at the reductive technique using enamels, how that works and just how easy it is for people to try out. 

When I first heard about Trench Crusade, I was intrigued, but it wasn’t until I saw the art and models that I knew I needed to play this game. I immediately fell in love with the Trench Pilgrims, and there’s something about their ferocious religious zealotry that rings all the right bells. Like many, I started with Games Workshop, painting in the layer-up method. It’s simple and intuitive, and there's a wealth of guides to help you paint. Heck, many of the models you see on box art are painted in this way, and the reason is that it looks clear and shows off all the wonderful details on each figure. For my warband, though, I wanted to do something more aligned with the setting, aiming to get down and dirty to reflect the realism of trench warfare. So, let’s get started!


Credit: Dandy

Building

Bases

First off: Bases! Super easy and super simple to do with just a corkboard and coffee stirrers. For the cork, all you need to do is break off a piece of cork to an appropriate size, rough up the edges, and leave as much space for your texture/basing paste as you desire. Next up, we want to break up your coffee stirrers, roughen them up, and line them up like a duckboard. Before we glue things down, though, there’s an important decision here, which you’ll probably want to continue with as you go on building and painting, and that is, to what extent you want to go down the grimdark rabbit hole? Lining up your duckboards all neat and tidy portrays a certain sense of order, compared to a few broken planks of wood your character can barely navigate on. Think about how you want the environment to look before committing to anything in particular. Give it a try on a few test bases and place your models on them to get an idea of what sort of weathering you’ll want to do further down the line. 

For your larger bases, you can leave spots to add extra elements to the base, like skulls or supply crates, or add wooden crosses using some of your leftover coffee stirrers. 


Credit: Dandy

Painting

After getting our models on our bases and giving them a zenithal prime, I added my base coats. For the most part, I used Army Painter’s John Blanche Masterclass set 1 & 2. I wanted to give these paints a try and stick with a scheme that primarily uses them, as a quasi-selective but inherently cohesive colour palette.


 

Metallics 

For the steel, I used Heavy Metal from Volume 2, which gives a really nice dark base coat to start with. In places where I felt highlights were needed, for example, the shoulders of the Anchorite Shrine, I applied Vallejo Metal Color Silver. For the Gold, I went with Vallejo Metal Color Gold, a really nice pale gold that complements the colder hue of the Heavy Metal. A few smaller gold pieces were done in Emperor's Gold found in Volume 1 of the John Blanche set, but I refrained from using it too much, as it was a little too warm for my tastes, and bordered on a bronze colour. Speaking of Bronze or Copper, for the bells, some were done with Vallejo Metal Color Copper to add a bit of variety in places, such as the back of the War Prophet or the bells on the Communicant's giant flail.


Credit: Dandy

Skin Tones

I broke down the skin tones into two main categories: Human and Communicant.

For the most part, there isn’t much exposed flesh on the human models, as I opted for the Armoured Stigmatic Nuns, and helmets mostly sort out our face-painting issues. Regardless of skin tone, my general approach was to use washes rather than flat base colours. One of the downsides of rattlecan priming your minis is that no matter how hard you try, you always get a bit of speckling after your prime from your grey and white primer. Still, we can turn that frown upside down by using that speckled effect to our advantage, creating a blotchy, spotty skin tone that meshes well with the bleak nature of the setting. After all, skin care is not a primary concern for our warband! For lighter skin tones, I used a Warm Skin Shade from Volume 1, and for darker skin tones, I used Agrax Earthshade. You can give as many passes as you would like. I felt like I initially went too hard on the darker skin tone of my Ecclesiastic Prisoner, as I will be adding an enamel wash later on, and I probably could have stuck with just the one pass, instead of adding the second. 

For our Communicant friends, I wanted them to really stand out, signifying their status as ones who have held communion with the flesh and blood of a Meta Christ. I wanted to go with a more purplish hue - to signify how inhuman it makes those who partake in it. To start, I did a thin glaze of Army Painter Speedpaint Lizardfolk Cyan, hitting just the shadow areas, and feathering it out. After that, I hit the model with Army Painter Speedpaint Murder Scene, which, when overlaid on the cyan, creates really interesting shadow tones. You can add more cyan glazes afterwards to create more definition.


Credit: Dandy

Clothing & Wood 

For clothing, I went with a strict 2-3-colour setup. Blanched Berry, Blighted Green and Sunburst Ochre from the Army Painter John Blanche sets. I want to keep it simple to maintain cohesion, since all our models are under the same warband. For the wood, I went with Banshee Brown (also from the Masterclass sets) or Sunburst Ochre again.

Bases

I added my texture paste to the bases at this point. I went with Vallejo Diorama European Mud. It’s a greenish mud colour that fits nicely with the decayed nature of Trench Crusade, and as a bonus, it comes with a bit of debris, adding some texture to the base. I painted up the duckboards with Sunburst Ochre, and hit the whole base with a Voodoo Shade and then Agrax Earthshade. I wanted to darken the duckboards and add a little bit of a green hue to the base, so I drybrushed the bases with Tainted Garden and then Vallejo Model Color Green Grey. 

Now with the base coats down, it's time to decide how to add some highlights, either by picking out some localised edge highlights or, as I did with my shrine, by giving it a drybrush pass to add some general highlighting. It’s important not to make your highlights too pronounced; the enamel washes will unify the model, but they won’t blend your highlights seamlessly. Speaking of washes...

Before that, I gave my models a quick varnish. It's important to do this to avoid damaging our acrylic paint layers when we come to remove our enamel paints later on. The character of the varnish will, to a small extent, determine how the enamels behave. A gloss varnish will make enamels act more like a wash, collecting in the recesses. The more matte the varnish, the more enamels will “stick” to and stain all the parts of the model. For this, I went with a satin varnish to get a good in-between.

The Enamels 

So, now it’s wash time! I used Villainy Ink, a set of enamel-based paints, washes and weathering products. Being enamel-based, we can’t thin it with water and need to use a solvent instead. I went with an Odourless Thinner, mostly because it is less aggressive on the paint, whilst still thinning your enamels nicely. 

Now, before we go to town, a few tips:

  1. Get some mixing balls! You really, really have to mix these paints well. I cannot underestimate how much you need to shake these paints. Imagine you were a Guardsman, and someone handed you a pile of rocks, saying that they're Cadia. The resulting carnage is the level of ferocity you need to shake these paints with. In fact, if you think you’ll be using these often, just go ahead and grab a vortex mixer. A minute on the mixer with some mixing balls and you will be fine. 

  2.  The way these enamels behave and the results they produce will vary depending on how thinned they are, the type of paint, how quickly they dry, and a bunch of other factors. Villainy Ink has a resource page on its website that features a short video introducing the paint and some techniques you can use. For instance, you can use Decay of Death as both a patina effect or as a moss effect, with the latter being more prominent if you hit the paint with a hair dryer after applying it. That’s not to say you can’t just slather on the paint and remove it, which is exactly what I did. 
Now it is time! I hit all the models with an all-over coat of Villainy Ink Goon’s Grime, which, after a bit of reading and watching, proved to be a good all-around go-to wash. After about 20 minutes, I came back with a Q-tip dipped in my odourless thinner to remove the excess, focusing on removing the parts where the highlights would be in a regular “layer up” method. The more you soak the Q-tip, the thinner and more “wash-like” your paints become, and the more they flow into the recesses. Do be aware, though (and this happened on a couple of my models), that doing so will reveal a lot of the dried pigments in the recesses, creating a heavily weathered effect. Now, this is all up to taste, of course, but I found that being too thin was a negative. I try to rely on the simple motion of physically removing the enamel. 


Credit: Dandy

Speaking of which, I varnished beforehand so I wouldn’t damage the base layer, which could happen through the friction generated by removing the enamels. Use the Q-tip's natural gravity on the model to remove the paint, rather than applying pressure. You can also use a rolling motion. One negative I found using regular Q-tips is that they can leave fibres on your model afterwards. As an alternative, I think I will try eyeshadow applicators, which are little pads on sticks. (keewa: In addition to eyeshadow applicators, you can use cotton swabs made by hobby companies like Tamiya, which have a much denser wind and lose far fewer hairs!)

For the larger models, I used a makeup egg sponge, dipped in a little thinner. Again, gently removing the parts of the thinner where I wanted highlights. 

The great thing about these enamels is that they have a long drying time, giving you a lot of time to work with the paint. After an initial pass, you can go back with a brush and a little thinner to reach some hard-to-reach places, or go back in with a flat, dry brush and spread the paint around a little, creating streaks or specific spots you want to paint. 

For more specific uses, I used Villainy Ink Sector Rust for my rust effects, picking out our joints, rivets, and folds in the armour. For some of the gold parts, like the Shrine’s chest, and the Observer's head, I went with Villainy Ink Ichor of the Damned, a nice black universal enamel. Lastly, for the wood, I added some Villainy Ink Decay of Death, and, let me tell you, with this paint specifically, less is more! It is deceptively pronounced once it has dried. It may not appear strong and may look a little translucent, but once it’s dry, it becomes opaque and noticeable. 

What we are left with is a model in which your highlights are simply the areas where the enamel has been removed the most, and your shadows are where the wash has collected.


Credit: Dandy

Weathering and Finishing Touches

Now even more stuff! So, after all the enamels were dry and I was happy with how things looked, I could have stopped here if I just wanted to get models on the table. But where's the fun in that? We can easily take things up a notch with some simple steps. You can add highlights here and there to give your models some pop and weather to add a sense of gritty realism. 

To complement the rust effect, I added some Army Painter Grim Rust, as well as some Vallejo pigment - any reddish or orange will do. The Grim Rust gives off a yellow hue, so by adding the paint and pigment, you get a nice range of rust colours. 

Lastly, blood! For the fresh blood, I used Army Painter Fresh Blood, and for blood pools, I took the same fresh blood and added some browns and Blanched Berry to give it a different look.


Credit: Dandy

Final Thoughts

This first foray into the reductive grimdark technique was, I have to say, a lot more forgiving than I thought it was going to be. I understand the initial hesitation people have about using enamels, knowing they may have to remove the wash and potentially the paint, but luckily for me, that happened only on one small bit of a Castigator that I, upon reflection, forgot to varnish. I added my base coat back onto the part, varnished that part of the model and added some Villainy Ink Goon’s Grime again and voila! It was like it never happened. 

Enamels, by their nature, have a longer drying time, which allowed me to work with them and correct mistakes long after an acrylic would normally have dried. I batch-painted the wash and removal stage in sets of 3-4 models, but one pesky Observer I have left the Ichor of the Damned on for too long, and his once radiant golden helmet now had a sticky black paint all over it. Had this been an Acrylic, well, that would have been the end of that, and I would have had to start afresh. However, I was still able to remove the paint even after almost an hour of time on the model. It was a little more stubborn, but following the gentle removal process I mentioned, it came out fine. 

As a final note, it’s more forgiving of mistakes. I’m not gonna lie, I am really incompetent when it comes to the layer-up method. My edge highlights look like thick, goofy lines; the angles all look messed up, and don’t get me started on glazing. But this? Add a universal wash and remove it? That I can do! That’s not to say there is no delicate brushwork! Far from it, even. This grimdark style offers a plethora of creative avenues to explore. Oil pencils, superglue, heck, anything. Others in the space have created truly masterful works that go beyond a simple wash and removal. Various enamels can be used to paint in a more traditional sense, adding skin tone variation, for example, without removing them afterwards. The unique property of some enamels having a slightly textured finish adds the extra three-dimensionality that makes this style so engaging and enticing. 

For anyone interested in the technique, I highly recommend you give it a go. I certainly will as I look to expand my army, which I'll cover in a future article. In the meantime, I encourage anyone who wants to try enamels or oils to give it a go! It opens up a new space to play with and engage with your minis in a very different and fun way.
