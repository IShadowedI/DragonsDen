Lore Explainer: A Song of Ice and Fire - Fan Theories

October 23rd, 2026 via Goonhammer

The story of A Song of Ice and Fire is notoriously sprawling and, painfully, still incomplete. “But keewa,” you might think “The TV show is finished; it is complete,” to which I would say, “Lies! The TV show is not canon! No! Get out of my house!”

Because the story is NOT FINISHED, the community of superfans online (I consider myself a superfan too) loves to come up with fan theories based on close readings of the text, the companion works and novellas, and general wild speculation. These theories range in importance from predicting how major plot details will shake out (if Winds ever gets finished) to completely inconsequential theories about complete ephemera.

As someone who has read an awful lot of these fan theories, allow me to share some of my favourites with you, whether because I think they’re likely to be accurate, they’re interesting, or just funny.

Note: This article will contain many spoilers for all books in A Song of Ice and Fire, I am going to assume that the reader is at least au fait with A Song of Ice and Fire and has read the main line of books as they currently stand and has an understanding of the story and its world; I’m not going to start out by explaining what Westeros is, put it that way.

Frey Pies

In A Dance With Dragons, Ramsay Bolton and “Arya Stark” (actually Jeyne Poole) are due to be married in Barrowton as the Northern army goes home after the War of Five Kings, and all the noble houses of the North are supposed to be represented in one way or another. The Boltons have a bit of a flair for the dramatic, and so they move the wedding to Winterfell, the castle they secretly sacked and burned after capturing Theon Greyjoy (and blaming the burning on him) all the way back in Book 2.

After the Red Wedding, three awful Freys are dispatched to White Harbour with the bones of Wyman Manderly’s son Wendel, both to return them to his home and to make sure Wyman remains loyal to the Iron Throne, presumably by reporting on him. The Iron Throne holds Wyman’s remaining son Wylis after his capture in the Riverlands. Wyman is a bit of a schemer himself, pretending to be a bluff and guileless fat guy, so he makes nice with the Freys until his living son is returned to him and everybody (including the Freys) set off to Winterfell for the wedding, even though the snow is falling pretty heavily. As they left White Harbour, Wyman gave the three Freys some nice horses as a guest gift, and at this point the Freys disappear from the story and don’t turn up again when the Manderlys arrive at Winterfell for the wedding with a lot of food for the celebration, including three huge wedding pies, described as

"three great wedding pies, as wide across as wagon wheels, their flaky crusts stuffed to bursting with carrots, onions, turnips, parsnips, mushrooms, and chunks of seasoned pork swimming in a savory brown gravy."

Wyman claims that he doesn’t know where the Freys got to, only that they got impatient with how slowly Wyman travels (since he can’t ride (too fat), he has to ride in a litter) and rode ahead on their horses.

Not a Frey Pie, but still, a very large pie.

The Frey Pies theory suggests (as you can probably guess from the name) that Wyman had the three Freys ambushed and murdered on the way to Winterfell and baked them into pies to serve to the Boltons and Freys in secret revenge for his son’s death at the Red Wedding.

There are lots of little textual hints that this theory is probably correct:

  • Wyman gives the three Freys fine horses as Guest Gifts when they depart White Harbour. The giving of Guest Gifts is seen as marking the end of the host’s obligation to their guests. Since the Red Wedding was a flagrant violation of Guest Right, waiting until after the giving of gifts to murder the Freys might be a symbol of Wyman wanting to do things “by the book” in contrast to the duplicitous actions of the Freys.
  • When Wyman secretly meets with Davos Seaworth, he tells him that he doesn’t believe the lies the Freys tell about the Red Wedding (Robb Stark started it by turning into a wolf and attacking the Freys, etc.), and expresses bitter anger towards them.

    “My son Wendel came to the Twins a guest. He ate Lord Walder's bread and salt, and hung his sword upon the wall to feast with friends. And they murdered him. Murdered, I say, and may the Freys choke upon their fables. I drink with Jared, jape with Symond, promise Rhaegar the hand of my own beloved granddaughter ... but never think that means I have forgotten. The north remembers, Lord Davos. The North remembers, and the mummer's farce is almost done. My son is home.” - Davos IV, ADWD.

  • Wyman serves the pies himself at the Wedding Feast, eagerly making sure that the prime culprits of the Red Wedding, the Freys and Boltons, eat pieces of the pies.
  • At the conclusion of the Winterfell wedding feast, a very drunk Wyman Manderly asks the singer to play a song about The Rat Cook. In an earlier book, Bran Stark reflects on the legend of the Rat Cook, a Night's Watchman who killed a visiting prince and served him to his father baked into a pie; the cook is then cursed by the gods by being turned into a monstrous rat. The story is explicit that the crime of the Rat Cook is not that of murder, but of breaching the custom of Guest Right by killing a guest beneath his roof (which Wyman accuses the Freys of doing).  
  • The pies are described as being filled with vegetables and pork - it’s said (by cannibals, presumably) that human meat tastes a lot like pork.

With all this evidence taken together, it’s pretty much certain that the three Freys were indeed baked into pies by Wyman Manderly.

Young Griff/Aegon Is Illyrio’s Son and a Blackfyre

Okay, this one is a little complicated, but please bear with me: there’s a lot to unpack. During A Dance with Dragons, Tyrion crosses paths with a party of adventurers in Essos when Illyrio places him with them; the leader of the group is a man called “Griff” travelling with his son “Young Griff”. If you’ve read ADWD, you know that Griff and Young Griff are actually Jon Connington and Aegon Targaryen, travelling incognito to meet Daenerys Targaryen. Aegon was allegedly spirited away by Varys from the Red Keep before his “murder” during the sack of King’s Landing, secreted across the Narrow Sea to Illyrio’s keeping so that one day he could reclaim the Iron Throne/Westeros.

It’s a pretty convenient story, so convenient that it’s hard to believe: let’s take a look at some evidence, shall we?

Daenerys has a bunch of trippy, prophetic visions in The House of the Undying in Qarth, while the Undying give her warnings, and a lot of the things shown in the House of the Undying are metaphorical and evocative predictions of things that do actually happen in the Story. Here’s the relevant passage:

“Glowing like sunset, a red sword was raised in the hand of a blue-eyed king who cast no shadow. A cloth dragon swayed on poles amidst a cheering crowd. From a smoking tower, a great stone beast took wing, breathing shadow fire. . . . mother of dragons, slayer of lies . . .”

Daenerys reflects on this with Jorah Mormont later in the same book:

““A mummer’s dragon, you said. What is a mummer’s dragon, pray?”

“A cloth dragon on poles,” Dany explained. “Mummers use them in their follies, to give the heroes something to fight.”

Ser Jorah frowned.”

 

Taken together, this vision implies that at some point in her story, Daenerys will encounter a fake dragon, a Mummer’s dragon, and that she will slay a “lie” related to it.

Three whole books later, Quaithe speaks to Daenerys through some mystical means, and reminds her (and us) about the Mummer’s Dragon once more:

"Hear me, Daenerys Targaryen. The glass candles are burning. Soon comes the pale mare, and after her the others. Kraken and dark flame, lion and griffin, the sun's son and the mummer's dragon. Trust none of them. Remember the Undying. Beware the perfumed seneschal." 

Who is this “Mummer’s dragon”? There’s really only one candidate - Young Griff, aka “Aegon Targaryen”. As the only other person in the story claiming to be a Targaryen, Young Griff is immediately suspicious; this suspicion is only reinforced by Young Griff’s association with Illyrio and Varys… Two very adept schemers. But wait! Wasn’t Varys a mummer before he was castrated and became a spymaster? Yes, yes he was! With this information in hand, we can quite confidently say that “Aegon” is the Mummer’s Dragon, and as such is almost certainly not a Targaryen. George RR Martin knows a lot of English history, and he knows very well that there have been several instances of royal pretenders claiming to be the lost Princes In The Tower, returned from exile overseas.

Well then who is he? That question is far less certain, but there is a relatively convincing theory that “Aegon” is actually a Blackfyre. The Blackfyres were a pseudo-cadet branch of House Targaryen founded by a legitimised bastard known as Daemon Blackfyre; they then fought several failed civil wars (known as Blackfyre Rebellions) against the Targaryens to try and take control of the throne. They typically share the classic Targaryen traits of blonde hair and purple eyes, too. The Blackfyres were finally wiped out some thirty years before the main story begins, when Barristan Selmy killed Maelys the Monstrous, the final male Blackfyre.  But wait, “Aegon” is only 16 years old; if the last Blackfyre died thirty years ago, how can he possibly be a Blackfyre? Well, Maelys was the last male Blackfyre; it’s entirely possible that the Blackfyre family continued through the female line. If so, it’s possible that “Aegon’s” mother was a Blackfyre, living incognito in Essos.

There’s a fun bit of imagery that hints towards this in A Feast For Crows: The Inn at the Crossroads once had a black dragon sign made of wrought iron, but following the first rebellion, the local lord was angered by the sight of it, cut the sign to pieces, and threw it in the river. Some years later, one of the dragon’s heads washed up on a tidal island known as the Quiet Isle, but in the intervening time it had become red with rust. A black dragon that goes to the sea and returns to Westeros coloured red (on the outside).

Illyrio Mopatis’ second wife was called Serra - he shares his story with Tyrion in ADWD. To give a short précis: He met her in a brothel in Lys and liked her so much he brought her home, at some undetermined point later, she got greyscale and died. Illyrio keeps her turned-to-stone hands and wears a locket with her portrait in it, which he shows to Tyrion. Serra is described as having blue eyes and blonde hair streaked with silver, typically Valyrian traits, though this might not mean much since Lyseni tend to share Valyrian traits more than the other peoples of the world. This extension of the Blackfyre theory suggests that Illyrio’s wife Serra was, in fact, Serra Blackfyre, and that Illyrio intends to get “Aegon” on the throne to fulfil some kind of promise to the wife he loved so much that he keeps her hands in his bedroom.

But wait, there’s more! Some fans take this theory even farther to suggest that Varys himself is also a Blackfyre, potentially even the sibling of Serra. This is a bit “out there” - some fans believe the reason Varys was sold as a slave, castrated, and his penis used in a magic ritual by a sorcerer is that he also possesses King’s Blood, through his Blackfyre ancestry, and King’s Blood is noted throughout the series by magic users as being a kind of catalyst that makes magic spells more powerful (Melisandre relies on this quite a few times in the novels).

Adherents of this particular extended theory claim that Varys isn’t actually bald, but shaves his head to disguise his silver-blonde Valyrian hair. The relevant evidence put forth for this is that, when introduced, Varys is described as being “as hairless as an egg”. In the Dunk and Egg novellas, Aegon Targaryen (Egg) shaves his head to disguise his silver-blonde Valyrian hair.

Obviously, the implications of “Aegon” being both a pretender and a Blackfyre would have huge implications for the wider story of A Song of Ice and Fire, so if this one is true, it’ll be pretty seismic.

Peter Claffey as Dunk in A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms: Copyright HBO

Hodor Is Dunk’s Great Grandson/Dunk Had Sex with Old Nan

In Bran’s final chapter in A Dance With Dragons, he continues his greenseer training with Bloodraven, looking into the past through the eyes of Winterfell’s Heart Tree. Later, when going to sleep, he has a dream composed of a bunch of visions from the same Heart Tree in quick succession, going backwards in time – one of these visions is as follows:

“Then there came a brown-haired girl slender as a spear who stood on the tips of her toes to kiss the lips of a young knight as tall as Hodor.”

There are a bunch of textual hints as to the implications of this single sentence.

  • The scene takes place in Winterfell, and we know that Old Nan has always lived in Winterfell. Dunk and Egg visited Winterfell at some point between The Mystery Knight and Egg’s coming of age. Old Nan would be a similar age to Dunk, if he were still alive.
  • Hodor is explicitly mentioned as a comparison, and Old Nan is Hodor’s great-grandmother. Hodor is unusually tall and strong, standing over 7 feet - Dunk is also around 7 feet tall.
  • The man is described as a knight - knighthood is unusual in the North, since they do not follow the Faith of the Seven, which is the religious grouping in Westeros that has knighthood. Dunk is, of course, a knight.
  • Another especially tall and strong character has been confirmed by GRRM to be a descendant of Dunk - Brienne. 

As to how this affects the story, well, I can’t think of any way that Hodor’s parentage would have much impact on the plot, but even so, it’s a nice little potential wink towards the Dunk and Egg stories in the main series.

Tywin Lannister Secret Sex Tunnel/Marei

During the events of A Clash of Kings, Tyrion visits a fancy, high-class brothel in King’s Landing called Chataya’s as a cover for secretly paying visits to his lover Shae. This deception involves entering the brothel, ostensibly as a customer, going to the fanciest room in the place, where he is met by Varys, who opens a secret passage and accompanies him to a stable. While they do so, Varys provides some information about the provenance of this secret passage.

“How is it a brothel happens to have a secret entrance?”

“The tunnel was dug for another King’s Hand, whose honor would not allow him to enter such a house openly. Chataya has closely guarded the knowledge of its existence.”

 

Who is a good candidate for this Hand of the King who maintains a severe and honourable public persona, but visits the city’s most expensive brothel on the sly? The earlier part of the chapter provides some sneaky little clues as to his identity.

“Over the door swung an ornate lamp, a globe of gilded metal and scarlet glass.”

“Within the room was a great canopied bed, a tall wardrobe decorated with erotic carvings, and a narrow window of leaded glass in a pattern of red and yellow diamonds.”

Taken together, these conspicuous descriptions of decorations in red and gold, including the window of the fancy sex room itself, suggest that the randy Hand is, in fact, Lord Tywin Lannister himself. He who chides Tyrion for using the services of sex workers and is claimed to have not even touched a woman since his beloved wife died is a massive hypocrite, revealed fully when he has sex with Shae herself shortly before being killed by Tyrion.

But that’s not all! One of the workers in the brothel is a girl called Marei, who is described as being pale, solemn, and having blonde hair and green eyes. Blonde hair and green eyes are classic Lannister traits, so building on the suggestion of Tywin’s sex tunnel, it’s possible to imagine that Marei is actually Tywin Lannister’s bastard daughter. Marei is older than Shae, who is 18 during the events of Game of Thrones, placing her birth year at 281 AC - meaning Marei was probably born either in 280AC or before. When was Tywin Lannister the Hand of the King? From 262 until 281AC.

The dates match up pretty cleanly, so it’s entirely possible that Marei really is Tywin’s bastard daughter; and therefore a half-sister to Cersei, Jaime, and Tyrion. It’s a good thing she didn’t sleep with Tyrion!

What impact does any of this have on the story? None whatsoever; it’s just more easter-egg evidence that Tywin Lannister was a massive hypocrite.

Sam Tarly Has the Horn of Winter

During A Clash of Kings, Ghost leads Jon Snow - potentially via some Old Gods magic - to a cache of Dragonglass blades and arrowheads, wrapped up in a Night’s Watch cloak and buried at the Fist of the First Men. Also present in this cache is a cracked old war horn, which he gives to Sam Tarly because, as a book-learnin’ kind of guy, he figures that Sam will like a useless artefact.

At around the same time, the wildlings, led by Mance Rayder, are searching ancient graves for a powerful magic artefact called the Horn of Joramun, or the Horn of Winter – a legendary horn with the power to destroy The Wall once sounded. Mance plans to use this horn as a bargaining chip in order to force the Night's Watch to let the Wildlings through The Wall so they can shelter behind it, since the Others can’t pass through the magic wards built into The Wall. It’s a pretty desperate gambit. Threatening the Night's Watch to either let them through the Wall or they’ll destroy it altogether and doom everybody is quite high-stakes, to put it mildly. Mance claims to have found the Horn of Winter and brings it with him when the Wildlings march on The Wall. After their surprise defeat by the forces of Stannis Baratheon, both Mance Rayder and the Horn are captured, and the Horn is ritually burned by Melisandre. However, in the time between the Wildling’s capture and Jon Snow’s murder, he is told several times by friendly Wildlings that the Horn was never found, and the one burned by Melisandre was a fake, intended to bluff the Night’s Watch.

“Melisandre burned the Horn of Joramun.”

“Did she?” Tormund slapped his thigh and hooted. “She burned that fine big horn, aye. A bloody sin, I call it. A thousand years old, that was. We found it in a giant’s grave, and no man o’ us had ever seen a horn so big. That must have been why Mance got the notion to tell you it were Joramun’s. He wanted you crows to think he had it in his power to blow your bloody Wall down about your knees. But we never found the true horn, not for all our digging. If we had, every kneeler in your Seven Kingdoms would have chunks o’ ice to cool his wine all summer.”

Jon XII - A Dance With Dragons

 

So, if we accept that the Horn of Winter is real, and that it has already appeared in the narrative (admittedly a big leap), there’s only one candidate as to which horn it can be - the cracked Warhorn Jon mysteriously found on the Fist of the First Men, which he subsequently passed on to Samwell Tarly. Maybe a Night's Watchman ranger found the horn in the wild, and realising its true nature, took it to the Fist and buried it surrounded with blades of dragonglass, to prevent the Others from getting hold of it. The horn is mentioned a bunch of times, too many times for it to simply be an unimportant bit of junk. When Sam left The Wall, he took the horn with him to Oldtown, so it’s currently about as far from The Wall as it’s possible to be while still being in Westeros, so it’s safely out of the way, right?

Well… Euron Greyjoy - who has recently displayed some troublingly apocalyptic desires of his own - is currently attacking the Reach and threatening Oldtown itself. Were he to acquire this potential Horn of Winter, the existential situation Westeros faces would quickly go from bad to worse.

Rory McCann as The Hound in HBO's Game of Thrones - Credit: HBO

The UnKiss

During the Battle of the Blackwater in A Clash of Kings, Sandor Clegane, traumatised by the massive conflagration of burning ships, deserts his position, gets extremely drunk, and plans to leave the city.  On his way out the door, he visits Sansa Stark’s bedchamber and, in a very uncomfortable passage, offers to take her with him. When she declines, he threatens her with a knife and demands she sing a song for him. After she finishes singing, he rips off his bloodstained white cloak and leaves.

What’s more peculiar about this scene comes later: Sansa reflects on this event several times as her story progresses, mostly when the topic hinges around intimacy or kissing, but something about her recollection is amiss - each time she thinks about the night of the Blackwater, Sansa remembers The Hound kissing her, although this didn’t happen.

“Sansa wondered what Megga would think about kissing the Hound, as she had. He’d come to her the night of the battle stinking of wine and blood. He kissed me and threatened to kill me, and made me sing him a song.”

- A Storm of Swords, Sansa II

“As the boy’s lips touched her own she found herself thinking of another kiss. She could still remember how it felt, when his cruel mouth pressed down on her own. He had come to Sansa in the darkness as green fire filled the sky. He took a song and a kiss, and left me nothing but a bloody cloak.”

- A Feast For Crows “Alayne” II

Later in the same chapter, Sansa is asked if she knows what goes on in a marriage bed:

“She thought of Tyrion, and of the Hound and how he’d kissed her, and gave a nod”.

So why does Sansa remember kissing The Hound when that clearly did not happen? Search me, I have no idea. GRRM has, however, said that Sansa’s faulty memory of this event will be important later in the story, so this oddity still warrants a mention.

Sansa and the Ashford Tourney Champions

Another oddity in Sansa’s story has been remarked upon by fans since A Feast For Crows came out… oh my god, 21 years ago. In AFFC, Petyr Baelish organises a betrothal between Sansa (in her guise as his bastard daughter Alayne) and Harrold Hardyng, the heir apparent of the sickly Robert Arryn. Eagle-eyed readers recalled a detail from The Hedge Knight, the first Dunk and Egg novella from 1998 - in The Hedge Knight, the final roster of champions for Lady Ashford’s birthday tournament is:

  • Lyonel Baratheon
  • Leo Tyrell
  • Tybolt Lannister
  • Humfrey Hardyng
  • Valarr Targaryen

Sansa was first betrothed to Joffrey Baratheon before he cast her aside for Margaery Tyrell. After being cast aside by Joffrey, Olenna Tyrell suggests marrying her to Willas Tyrell. This marriage plan reaches the ears of the Lannisters, who interrupt it by forcibly marrying Sansa to Tyrion Lannister. After the Purple Wedding, Tyrion’s capture, and Sansa’s flight with Littlefinger, he attempts to marry her to Harrold Hardyng. If this pattern holds true, it implies that Sansa will marry, or be promised to marry, someone from House Targaryen.

You could argue “but they’re all great houses; it makes sense that Sansa Stark might get betrothed to any one of them”, but the inclusion of House Hardyng casts a spanner in this line of thinking, because, in the grand scheme of things, the Hardyngs are a nothing-family; they’re not even nobility, just landed knights. If Harold weren’t, by a series of very unlikely familial ties, the heir to the Eyrie, he’d never be considered a match for someone as important as Sansa Stark. GRRM loves leaving little hints in his work, and the coincidence is too strange to be discounted out of hand.

That's my roundup and explanation of only a few of the very, very many fan theories bouncing around the A Song of Ice and Fire fan community. What do you think? Are any of them likely to be accurate? Let me know on the Discord!
