Tabletop Battles Reviews: 007 First Light

June 13th, 2026 via Goonhammer
This is a spoiler-light review. I think I mention which obviously-evil character you meet turns out to be the villain, who Lenny Kravitz plays, and the number of women Bond sleeps with. There are no specific story beat spoilers.

It only took 24 years, but we’ve finally got another good James Bond video game.

That’s a bit glib; I think some people had nice things to say about that extremely bizarre GoldenEye remake that used Daniel Craig’s likeness instead of Pierce Brosnan’s, for instance. But for me, James Bond video games basically wrapped up with 007 Nightfire, an original adventure that sort of mashes together the weird, kind of Orientalist futurism of Tomorrow Never Dies (yes, you attempt to capture a white man at his traditional Japanese estate but are betrayed by his beautiful Japanese bodyguard, Kiko) with the space-hopping full bore white-plastic sci-fi nonsense of Moonraker. It was very fun when I was…well, I decline to do that math. Let’s say when I was a teenager.

Nightfire was also a first person shooter; the success of GoldenEye (the first one, on the Nintendo 64) and its Brosnan-branded sequels (The World Is Not Enough in particular) sort of set the tone for what a James Bond video game looks like and plays like; systems -- both in the hardware sense and in the “systems design” gameplay sense -- weren’t quite powerful enough to do more than arcade-style experiences, and even when Bond games branched out into third-person shooters with vehicle sequences (2004’s Everything or Nothing, mostly successfully; 2010’s Blood Stone, quite a bit less so), the games concerned themselves first and foremost with the action and violence of the Bond films; that’s always been a significant part of the character, but anyone with familiarity with the films knows that at least an equal amount of them -- the majority of them in the pre-Brosnan era -- was concerned with the business of Being James Bond, which is to say, oozing smugly between various vistas and having casual sex with the women there.

Video games in the early 2000s didn’t have the interest or, frankly, the toolkit to do anything interesting with that part of the character; the idea of gaming around infiltration and social manipulation -- as distinct from a straight-up stealth action game, which was also a genre just finding its legs at that time with the Metal Gear Solid and Splinter Cell franchises -- was extremely underdeveloped. The Thief series did some of this, but there was still too much “the levels are full of enemies and if you are seen you are in a failure state” for it to really be the sort of game that models what Bond gets up to; he’ll sneak around from time to time, but he’s not a stealth character. There was a small Danish studio, IO Interactive, working on a series of games called Hitman that dabbled in this space; Hitman: Codename 47, their first attempt released in November 2000, was pretty clunky, but they had revised the formula significantly by the time Hitman 2: Silent Assassin rolled around a couple years later. By the time of their smashingly successful Hitman: A World of Assassination trilogy at the end of last decade, they had an excellent formula on their hands for how to model moving around a social space that your character isn’t supposed to be in and which becomes more or less hostile to the player based on their actions, as the main mode of gameplay. And then they got the Bond license!

Perhaps the most interesting thing about the IO Interactive take on Bond, then, is that if you handed it to me blind (a version of me that had no idea who had made the game but still played a lot of video games and understood this genre’s general history, somehow), Hitman is not the franchise I would immediately twig to as this game’s formative inspiration. When this game is firing on all cylinders, the game it most reminds me of is the Watch Dogs games (at least the first two; like many, I did not play Legion). Your character has a device -- there a jailbroken smartphone, here a Q Watch, both with the same levels of techno-magic -- that he uses to impose his will on the world around him in a number of formalized ways, like rote spells; in Watch Dogs it was subroutine programs that would hack cameras or set off the grenades on guards’ belts, while in First Light it’s your expect Q Gadgeteering. Bond’s devices are always disguised as mundane items-of-the-day; while old reliables like the explosive grenade that looks like a lighter return, he’s also got more modern updates, like a taser trip mine disguised as a pair of air pods and their carrying case. (Another game in this vein, though angling more towards the stealth action instead of the social infiltration, was the excellent Star Wars Outlaws; there, Kay Vess’s magic device wasn’t a device at all, but her little alien axolotl-cat Nix.)

To layer additional Bond flavor on top of the gadgets, he also gets a separate mana bar, the Energy meter, to spend on various social actions. This is basically swag. Bond gains swag by doing a variety of cool things, usually knocking out enemies in stylish ways but also by doing incidental sidequests or optional goals in a social area in a Bond-ish manner, and he can spend it on certain actions: Lure, for 3 Energy, operates the same as the knock in a Metal Gear game or a whistle in various other stealth games, bringing an enemy to your concealed position so you can easily dispatch them. Bluff, for 6 Energy, fast-talks a guard with a boutique, location-specific back-and-forth of dialogue that briefly disables that guard’s alert meter, so Bond can just walk right past. There are specific guards -- Watchers, denoted by a white dot above their heads on the HUD -- that this trick won’t work on, but those guys can still be distracted away from their posts with the rest of Bond’s social playbook: Distractions, poison darts (the poison here isn’t neurotoxin; it’s the kind from Hitman that makes the victim swiftly seek out a toilet), or good old fashioned social engineering. Getting the right keycard, press pass, or disguise can get you past a lot of those guys.

The entire game isn’t social infiltration, of course; you will eventually go loud, and there will be sections that start as sneaking missions (though you retain access to Lure and Bluff in them for the most part) that quickly escalate to firefights if you’re spotted. This is all governed by the License to Kill mechanic; Bond is not allowed to shoot first, and you will not be able to use your gun until someone tries to use a gun on you. Then you have a License to Kill, a red banner displaying such appears in the status area at the top of the screen, and it’s time to rumble. I like the way this is implemented a great deal; generally speaking, Bond isn’t clearing out entire levels of guards through methodical stealth the way Agent 47 in Hitman can if you put your mind to it. It feels proper that he uses his fists and his wits until the other guys’ guns come out, and then he responds, usually with their guns that he’s taken off of them. And gun exchange is a huge part of Bond’s firefight toolkit, especially in the later parts of the game as enemies armor up into tanks. Pistol rounds might not penetrate juggernaut SWAT armor much at all, but throwing said pistol at that guy’s head will stun him long enough for Bond to charge the guy, hit a finisher combo on him, and kick the fallen guard’s shotgun up into his hands to face the rest of the guy’s team.

As you might have guessed from a punch and kick combo being better against a tac’d up PMC goon than a 9mm handgun, brawling is very powerful in this game. Guns will kill you very quickly, but when Bond gets in close with enemies he basically removes firearms from the equation -- even one-on-four, Bond has the advantage in a fistfight. Lots of satisfying environmental stuff here; if there’s a coffee mug or a brick or a hammer lying around, Bond can pick it up and hurl it at a guy to create an opening to hit a finisher, and a simple block and dodge system of responding to strikes and grabs gives him opportunities to get foes in grapples of his own, many of which end with Bond slamming his foe’s head into a table, a wall, or a nearby circuit breaker. There are boss characters who elevate the difficulty in hand to hand combat, especially the big plot enemies around whom setpieces are built -- then it becomes a lot more rock-paper-scissors, figuring out how to use the terrain to your advantage and what attacks simply won’t work. All of it feels pretty good.

There are vehicle sections in the game; they also feel good, but they aren’t a focus, and hours of the standard gameplay loop of social infiltration to stealth action to open firefight and back down the escalation scale will pass between them. The Land Rover sections in Africa are purely there as extended playable exposition dumps. I think there’s one more vehicle section I haven’t gotten to yet; I’m writing this review with, I believe, about 70-80% completion of the game’s story. It’s longer than I thought it would be; Steam says I have 25 hours in it, at least some significant chunk of which is having the game alt-tabbed, but the properly-adjusted number is still 14+ hours. (A skilled player who knows what they’re doing, not outright speedrunning but also not dawdling and messing around in the sandbox areas, can probably get to the point I’ve reached in six or so hours. Dawdling and screwing around with different ways to complete objectives is a big part of the fun of a first playthrough, though.)

So the gameplay is a massive success, in my view; the amount of game on offer is likewise worth the price of admission. The last question, then, is to do with the quality of the story and the Bond.

This is a Bond game for people who like James Bond fine. It is not a game for people who loathe James Bond, and it is not a game for people who really love James Bond in the strange distorted ways that superfans can get. If you are open to the concept of Bond -- you watch the movies when they come out, they’re either fine or not depending on the quality of the film in question, and then you mostly forget about the guy until the next one -- this is perfect for you. This instance of the character is brand new and exists off in his own little world; there has been no James Bond before him, the identity isn’t a codename, none of that nonsense. The story is not an adaptation of any previous Bond film; broadly speaking this is a game about Bond vs. an AI tech villain, though without any ripped-from-the-headlines qualities to it -- our master foe isn’t identifiable as a fictional Elon Musk or Sam Altman, and the story takes more influence from Minority Report than it does current discussions about the technology. I find that stuff generally well done but unremarkable as far as Bond plots go; to the extent that it has politics about generative AI, they’re the correct ones about the reliability of the technology and so on, but it is mostly concerned about moving you from setpiece to setpiece and woman to woman.

This gets us to Bond himself, and whether you’ll be able to tolerate him. This Bond is a standard-issue Bond: He’s straight, white, cis male, and has casual sex with a number of women. And fair enough, as far as it goes. These are loadbearing pillars of the character’s relationships with MI6, with all the people he encounters (and how he’s permitted to treat them), and with British Empire writ large; changing that formula was not a pool IO Interactive was willing -- or almost certainly even permitted by the IP holder -- to dip a toe into. You’ve got a factory-standard Bond, down to the point that they cast a guy who from a lot of angles looks almost interchangeable with Cal Lightsaber from over in the Jedi Survivor franchise. He just has one of those faces.

First Light is the story of this Bond’s training and first mission as a 00 agent, and he goes from an unpolished twerp and comfortable pack animal during the tutorial in the first act of the game into the recognizably modern-Bond instinctive loner by the end, though at least so far in my playthrough he hasn’t dabbled in the psychopathy that the Craig version of the character demonstrated from time to time in Casino Royale before Vesper Lynd civilized him. I think that’s a good thing. The writing itself is good and very occasionally great; it relies more on spectacle and execution than stand-out scriptwork. The characters are expected but enjoyable: M is the strict boss who nevertheless indulges her best agent; Q is the elderly hand who has seen it all and knows more than he lets on, especially when Bond is raiding the supply cabinets; Greenway, a new character for game as far as I’m aware, is the stock “unwilling old mentor who starts out hating our hero because he reminds him of a bitter past, and is won over by the hero’s virtues well before he’s willing to admit it” type. As with Bond himself, IOI isn’t reinventing wheels here.

This brings us to the women; the Bond Girls. I do idly wonder if Amazon MGM sent over notes to IO Interactive about a specific body count Bond had to achieve in the runtime; so far in my playthrough Bond has had three liaisons, and while two of them served purposes in the plot, one was somewhat bolted on to the side of a mission that didn’t necessarily need it. Bond is reliably “charming” in that specific irritating and confident way that he is perpetually rewarded for across all film and media, so they’ve got that down; there’s a decent balance between him pursuing the women and the women pursuing him. There are no Sean Connery Bond moments here, thank god; interpret that one however you will, it’s true in every direction. Still, one can very much see the seams where the writing team clocks out and the meat market stuff with James and his various paramours begins; the music swells, the camera fades, the scene cuts and the writing staff clocks back in. No new ground is broken here, there is no character progression, and in classic Bond fashion these relationships are supremely casual; the only woman Bond has a real relationship with is Moneypenny, who per recent tradition he is not physically involved with -- but she’s in his ear and updating the mission objectives in the top right of the screen, so of course she’s the most important woman in his life.

The game has one real demerit in the course of all of this: Lenny Kravitz. Stunt casting celebrities is a time-honored tradition in video games, but less so in the Bond franchise; Kravitz would stick out like a sore thumb here even if he was good at portraying pirate king Bawma, and he very much is not. The look is a bit aggressive, but the Bond franchise isn’t averse to flashy villains, especially if they’re wearing gold; it’s the vocal performance that sinks his part. Very unfortunate to hear that he’s going to be the main concern of the post-launch story content for the game; frankly, he does a poor enough job they should dub him out entirely.

Thankfully as far as the base game goes, Bawma is in and then out of the picture; I can unreservedly recommend the game as a whole to anyone with even a mild tolerance for James Bond’s bullshit and who has enjoyed any of the games mentioned previously in this piece, from Hitman to Watch Dogs to Star Wars Outlaws. And when the story’s complete, IO Interactive has taken the lessons from their Hitman franchise and made a modular run-based mission mode where you can go back and play the meat of the game again with different modifiers and objectives active; they’re adding even more stuff to this mode in their post-launch updates. The game plays well enough that I will probably find myself messing around in there long past the credits rolling. A strong recommendation, with the caveat I still have an hour or two left in the story.
