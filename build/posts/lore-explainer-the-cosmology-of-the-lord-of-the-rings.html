Lore Explainer: The Cosmology of The Lord of the Rings

October 10th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Tolkien’s universe has a relatively complicated internal cosmology that’s only really glimpsed from a distance in The Lord of the Rings, and doesn’t really feature at all in The Hobbit - although JRR Tolkien had been working on his “Legendarium” since World War I, it’s only really elaborated upon more fully in the Silmarillion, published in 1977, four years after his death, and expanded in the later anthologies Unfinished Tales (1980) and the History of Middle Earth (1983 - 1996), along with various unpublished material (Children of Hurin et al) compiled and edited by his son Christopher.

A lot of people don’t like The Silmarillion, calling it “impenetrable” and “impossibly dense”, but I believe that these people are wrong. In this article I’ll expand on the cosmology of Middle-Earth and answer questions like, “What is a Maiar?”, “Where do Dwarves come from?” and “What are Ents, anyway?”

Eru Iluvatar

Eru Iluvatar is, to all intents and purposes, the Tolkienised version of the Christian God - an eternal, omniscient, and omnipotent creator who existed before everything else and will continue to exist forever, regardless of what happens in the world, named Arda (of which Middle-Earth is a part). Illuvatar is the possessor of the Secret Fire, the flame that grants existence and life to everything else, and ultimately everything answers to him.

Illuvatar resides in the Timeless Halls, a place similar both to the primordial void and heaven, and from there he created the Ainur as his servants, formed from Illuvatar’s mind.

Ainulindale by Anna Kulisz Yoo

Ainur

The Ainur are immortal angelic beings, unsurprisingly similar to the Christian angels (this will be something of a running theme throughout this explainer), each born from a fragment of Ilúvatar’s mind - despite being ethereal beings born from the thoughts of the creator god, each of the Ainur nevertheless has a gender and familial relations, each determined by how Ilúvatar thought of them, brothers and sisters by dint of vibes, basically.

Once all the Ainur had been assembled in the Timeless Halls, Illuvatar began the most important aspect of the creation of a new species, teaching them how to make music. The newborn Ainur sang and sang their little hearts out, at first alone or in small groups, and later through larger ensembles. Through this singing and listening, each of the Ainur began to understand the minds of the other Ainur, and therefore formed a more complete picture of the mind of Ilúvatar. Not all the Ainur were happy campers, eager to please Illuvatar, though. An especially powerful Ainu named Melkor maliciously introduced discordant notes into the song (unmusical people discriminated against once again). When he was satisfied that the Ainur were decent enough singers, Iluvatar tasked them to collaborate with one another to create the Music of the Ainur, called the Ainulindalë in the later language of elves. The Ainulindalë was a complicated piece with three movements, and each of the Ainur were free to improvise around the themes set out by Iluvatar, weaving their own ideas into the great work. The music started out harmoniously enough, powerful and concordant, but the pride of Melkor caused him to add his own discord to the theme, causing conflict with the rest of the choir - some of the other Ainur took up Melkor’s discord and joined him in his rebellion against the perfect harmony of Ilúvatar’s vision, for Melkor desired possession of the Secret Fire and the power to create his own creatures to rival those of Ilúvatar.

The conflict between Illuvatar’s theme and Melkor’s discords built over the second movement, creating a turbulent sea of sound, crashing this way and that and causing dismay amongst the Ainur, many of whom stopped singing altogether in despair. One of the Ainur, Manwë, became a sort of principal proponent of Ilúvatar’s theme, taking the fight to his brother Melkor as they struggled for dominance over the music. Ilúvatar, acting as a sort of conductor, rose from his seat between each of the three themes to signal that it was time to change. The third theme was beautiful and sorrowful, and although Melkor rose up with a pompous, grandiose theme of his own in response, and although the discord between the themes shook the heavens, he was unable to overwhelm the beauty of Ilúvatar’s theme. Finally, Eru Illuvatar rose from his seat for a final time and ended the music with a deep and thunderous chord of his own.

As the Ainulindalë ceased, Eru Ilúvatar led the Ainur into the void and said to them, “Behold your music!” At once the Ainur were given a vision of the world and everything in it, including the Children of Ilúvatar, of whom they had no knowledge, and who had been added into the music with Ilúvatar’s voice alone. Following this vision, Ilúvatar said “Eä, let these things be!” and the material universe sprang into existence like the result of a colossal magic trick. Eä is the name given to the material universe, wherein sits Arda, the world of which Middle-Earth is a part.

Ilúvatar gave the Ainur permission to enter Eä to continue their work in shaping it; the mightiest who entered Arda are known as the Valar.

Valar

The Valar are essentially greater or arch-angels, powerful beings that are nevertheless subordinate to Ilúvatar himself. They are explicitly not gods or a pantheon in the polytheistic sense, although they may appear that way, and do have traits and characteristics similar to the pantheons of our own universe. Although they are spiritual beings and thus incorporeal, they can “wear” a physical form known as a Fana when they wish to appear to the children of Ilúvatar and other mortals in Arda.

They consist of six spousal pairs (yes, technically they’re all brothers and sisters of a kind, but they’re spiritual beings so it doesn’t count) and two unwed others:

  • Manwë and Varda
  • Aulë and Yavanna
  • Mandos and Vairë
  • Oromë and Vána
  • Lorien and Estë
  • Tulkas and Nessa
  • Nienna
  • Ulmo

As a subcategory, the most powerful of the Valar are known as the Arantar, or High Ones; these are Manwë, Ulmo, Aulë, Oromë, Mandos, Varda, Yavanna, and Nienna.

Let’s go through them one by one, shall we?

Manwe Sulimo, Lord of the Breath of Arda - Credit: Sepi Donne

Manwë

“Manwë is dearest to Ilúvatar and understands most clearly his purposes. He was appointed to be, in the fullness of time, the first of all Kings [...] his delight is in the winds and the clouds, and in all the regions of the air, from the heights to the depths, from the utmost borders of the Veil of Arda to the breezes that blow in the grass.“

Husband to Varda, Manwë is considered to be the king of the Valar, and is something of a Zeus character, although considerably less randy. His purview is the sky and the winds, and the birds are his servants. Some of the Maiar who came into his service took the form of mighty, sentient eagles that could speak and acted as messengers between Manwë and the people of Middle–Earth. His name in Sindarin is Aran Einior. When he appeared to mortals, his fana was that of a great king.

Varda Elentari, Credit: Sepi Donne

Varda

“With Manwë dwells Varda, Lady of the Stars, who knows all the regions of Eä. Too great is her beauty to be declared in the words of Men or of Elves; for the light of Ilúvatar lives still in her face. In light is her power and her joy.“

Manwë’s spouse and queen of the Valar. She’s the Valar referred to most often in the main Lord of the Rings books by her Sindarin name: Elbereth. Her beauty is beyond compare, her affiliation is with the stars and starlight, and the elves love her more than any other. Of all the Valar, Varda is the one that Melkor fears the most, as her gift of insight led her to be the first of the Valar to understand his nature and reject him. Manwë and Varda are a bit of a yin-yang power couple; through Varda’s light, Manwë is able to see through mist and darkness, and through Manwë, Varda is able to hear all the voices of creation. The phial with which Sam defeats the giant spider Shelob contains a tiny fragment of her light, which he activates by chanting a hymn to Varda known as A Elbereth Gilthoniel; Frodo also invokes this hymn at Weathertop as protection against the Witch-king of Angmar.

Aulë

“But the delight and pride of Aulë is in the deed of making, and in the thing made, and neither in possession nor in his own mastery, wherefore he gives and hoards not, and is free from care, passing ever on to some new work.”

The Silmarillion, "Ainulindalë"

Husband to Yavanna, Aulë is the Valar associated with materials, crafts, and invention. After the Ainulindalë, Aulë was responsible for the construction of Arda, which often brought him into conflict with Melkor, who envied Aulë’s creative ability and sought to deface his creations. In the time before the waking of the elves, Aulë created his own race of beings with attributes similar to his own, though as he did not possess the Secret Fire himself, Aulë was unable to give his creations true life independent of his own will. Illúvatar saw Aulë’s efforts and rebuked him, as the Ainulindalë was quite clear that the first to wake on Arda must be the first of Ilúvatar’s children, the Elves. Rather than show defiance like Melkor, Aulë submitted to the will of Ilúvatar and sadly raised his hammer to destroy his creations. As he did so, they cried out and cowered in fear as Ilúvatar, taking pity on them, imbued the Children of Aulë with true life, essentially adopting them as his own. This new race, known as the Dwarves, was thus put into a deep sleep, only to awaken after the emergence of the elves.

Yavanna - Credit: Mathias Moor

Yavanna

“She is the lover of all things that grow in the earth, and all their countless forms she holds in her mind, from the trees like towers in forests long ago to the moss upon stones or the small and secret things in the mould.”

Wife of Aulë, Yavanna is known to the elves as the Giver of Fruits, for her responsibility is to everything that grows from the ground (seems like a pretty massive responsibility; she must be very busy). Her domain and demeanour are similar to that of Demeter. Only slightly lesser in power and reverence than Varda, Yavanna is said to be the planter of the first seeds in Arda, and the plants her conceptions and creations. When she wears a fana, she often appears in the form of a tall woman in green crowned with the sun, or as a tree. When her husband Aulë created the dwarves, she feared that they would cut down all the trees in Arda, becoming even more alarmed when her husband informed her that Ilúvatar’s children (Elves and Men) would also need to cut down trees. She took her concerns to Manwë, asking in despair whether any of her creations would ever be free of the dominion of others, exclaiming: “Would that the trees might speak on behalf of all things that have roots, and punish those that wrong them!” This utterance of Yavanna took root in Manwë’s mind, and as it unfolded, Ilúvatar responded to her plea by promising that he would create beings to defend the forests and shepherd the trees, the Ents.

Yavanna and Aule had an odd relationship. After receiving Illuvatar’s promise that the trees would have their guardians, she went to him at work in his smithy and said, “Now let thy children beware! For there shall walk a power in the forests whose wrath they will arouse at their peril,” to which he replied, “Nonetheless, they will have need of wood,” and kept on smithing, an odd pair indeed.

Ulmo - credit: Nahar Doa

Ulmo

“Ulmo is the Lord of Waters. He is alone. He dwells nowhere long, but moves as he will in all the deep waters about the Earth or under the Earth.”

Ulmo is essentially a kind of Poseidon/Oceanus analogue, responsible for the seas, bays, rivers, and waters of Arda. Along with Manwë, Aulë, and Yavanna, Ulmo took part in the physical creation of the world, fabricating the oceans and the rains that water the land. He’s an unusual Valar in that he has no spouse and doesn’t live with the others on solid ground, preferring to spend his time in the deep oceans of his creation. Ulmo rarely wears a fana, but when he does, he appears as a terrible oceanic apparition “like a mounting wave”, wearing a dark foam-crested helmet and mail that shimmers between silver and green. When he speaks, his voice is as deep as the ocean. Although Ulmo might seem like a bit of a misanthropic loner, he has a deep love for the Children of Ilúvatar and has never forsaken them, acting as a protector even when the other Valar seemed to have abandoned them. The elves say that Ulmo’s waters are the veins of the earth, and therefore news comes to him of the needs and grief of the children that even Manwë cannot hear. He’s also the best musician among them, and elves believe they can hear the distant echo of the Ainulindalë in the waters.

Mandos - Credit: Eduardo Dekamaster

Namo and Vaire

Namo, also known as Mandos, is the creepiest of the Valar. He and his brother Irmo form a duet known as the Fëanturi, whose domains are the mastery of spirits. As a quasi-Hades analogue, he is the keeper of the House of the Dead and the Valar who knows everything that has happened and almost everything that is going to happen, except that which Iluvatar has yet to decide. Although Namo’s duty is a grim one, he is neither malicious nor evil. The domain of Mandos functions as a kind of cosmic waiting room, where the souls of slain elves are gathered to await their eventual reincarnation. Not much is written about his wife, Vaire, the Weaver, but the halls of Mandos are decorated with tapestries woven by Vaire that depict everything that has ever happened, although presumably she leaves out the parts where the characters go to the toilet.

Nienna, by Antonio Vinci

Nienna

Nienna, the Lady of Pity, is the sister of Namo and Irmo, and is the Valar concerned with grief, sorrow, and the endurance thereof. She’s a maudlin sort, although she does not weep for herself, she mourns rather the tragedies and sorrows of others, and the hurts of the world. From the beginning, Nienna was sorrowful, mixing a thread of lamentation into the Ainulindale, preemptively mourning the sorrows yet to come. She seldom visits other Valar, save for her brother Mandos - when she visits his halls, she cries with the spirits of the dead and provides comfort and strength as they wait for their turn at reincarnation.

Oromë the Hunter - Credit: Daniel Govar

Oromë and Vana

Oromë, also known in Sindarin as Tauron, is the huntsman of the Valar and is tasked with hunting and killing of monsters and fell beasts that plague the Children of Ilúvatar in Middle-earth. He was the first of the Valar to discover the elves after they awoke, and gave them the name Eldar. His wife is Vana, Ever-Young, the younger sister of Yavanna, who is most closely associated with flowers and youthfulness. She’s a bit of a Disney princess character; it is said that all flowers open and bloom at her coming, and all the birds sing.

Irmo and Este

The brother of Namo, Irmo, also known as Lorien, is given the title Master of Dreams, and as you might expect, his halls are a pretty trippy but relaxing place. He imparts visions upon the elves to guide them. Este, given the epithet “the gentle”, is the wife of Irmo and the Valar concerned with the healing of hurts and rest. So great are her powers that the other Valar visit the halls of Irmo seeking relaxation and refreshment.

Tulkas by Nahar Doa

Tulkas and Nessa

Tulkas, the Valiant, is a sort of Hercules-like figure. Of all the Valar, he’s physically the strongest and loves to engage in feats of strength and prowess, fighting with no weapons other than his hands. He doesn’t ride any kind of steed, since he can run faster than anything and apparently has infinite stamina. Although he is extremely strong and violent, Tulkas is not “dreadful”. Instead, he is a merry and boisterous character, laughing loudly while fighting. He embodies the purest Himbo qualities: he is a stout friend and not easily roused to anger, but neither learns from the past nor thinks of the future and is described as “of no avail as a councillor”, yeowch. During one of the wars against evil, Tulkas wrestled Melkor and threw him down on his face. Badass.

Nessa is the wife of Tulkas and sister of Orome. Not much is written about her, except that she’s a very fast runner, deer follow her about, and she’s great at dancing.

Maiar

The Maiar are a lesser kind of Ainur compared to the Valar, angels to their archangels, and as such are the servants and helpers of the Valar. Unlike the Valar, the number of Maiar is unknown, but safe to say there are quite a lot of them, often occupying niches subordinate to the major Valar they serve - for example: Ossë is a Maiar known as the Master of the Coasts, who delights in storms and the roaring of waves, and as such is the vassal of Ulmo, along with his wife Uinen, who is beloved by mariners for her ability to calm the waters stirred into rage by Ossë. Other notable Maiar include Balrogs and the Istari (known by men as Wizards).

Credit: Sepi Donne

Istari

The Istari is the name given to five Maiar dispatched by the Valar, with Eru’s consent, to assist the peoples of Middle-Earth in resisting Sauron. The last time the Valar themselves went to war against Melkor’s forces, they smashed the place up like a rough pub on a Saturday night, so sending some subordinates was probably a smart move. The Istari were clad in the physical forms of elderly men, not just fana, so they were able to feel pain, greed, joy, hunger, and everything else that makes a human. In addition to this kind of forced empathising with men and elves, their physical bodies could be killed. In case you were thinking that the Valar placed too many restrictions on the Istari, they were also forbidden from attempting to force men to do their bidding, instead relying on their abilities to persuade and advise. Honestly, we could write a whole article about the Istari by themselves, so I’ll just give a short précis for each here.

Saruman

The White, originally known as Curumo, was a Maiar pupil of Aulë (just like Sauron; Aulë really had rotten luck with his choice of disciples). Saruman was described as the wisest of the Istari and their chief. Saruman was proud and resentful of the apparent favour bestowed upon his subordinate Gandalf, first by Varda and then by the elf Cirdan, who entrusted to him the ring of power Narya. Fails in his mission to defeat Sauron after becoming corrupted by a desire for power.

Gandalf

The Grey, then the White, originally known as Olórin, was a Maiar pupil of Irmo who became associated with Varda, Nienna, and Manwë at various points. Olórin was chosen to journey to Middle-earth as one of the Istari but was reluctant, as he was scared of Sauron (with good reason), but Manwë ordered him to go anyway, saying that his fear made him an ideal candidate to assist the Elves and Men. Travelled far among men and learned much from the people of Middle-earth, ultimately using both his wisdom and empathy to assist in the destruction of Sauron and the salvation of men.

Radagast

The Brown, originally known as Aiwendil, was a Maiar pupil of Yavanna and was included in the Istari’s mission at her insistence, which was probably a bit embarrassing for him, like your mum insisting that the other kids let you play with them. As you might expect from a disciple of Yavanna, he’s very, very interested in plants and animals, less so in elves and men. Although he assists Gandalf in a few ways, ultimately he fails in his mission after becoming too distracted by his special interest.

The Blue Wizards

A weird bit of ephemera, the only indication that the remaining two wizards exist in the text of The Lord of the Rings comes when Saruman makes mention of “the rods of the five wizards”, suggesting that there must be two more wizards than the three previously mentioned. That’s it! That’s the only mention at all in the main body of the story, outside of paratext. In the paratextual “Essay on the Istari”, Tolkien mentions the two “Blue Wizards” travelled much farther east and perhaps south than the other wizards; however:

“What success they had I do not know; but I fear that they failed, as Saruman did, though doubtless in different ways; and I suspect they were founders or beginners of secret cults and “magic” traditions that outlasted the fall of Sauron.”

Their names and associations are a bit murky; in some writings Tolkien refers to them as Alatar and Pallando, and suggests they are Maiar of Orome (which perhaps makes them suited to range farther than the other wizards, since Orome was also very widely travelled in Middle-earth), while another connects Pallando to Mandos and Nienna. Further writing in Unfinished Tales suggests that Alatar was selected by Orome, and took Pallando along as a friend, which I think is quite nice.

Even then, Tolkien changed his mind often on who and what the Blue Wizards were and their particular deeds. In later writings, he calls them Morinehtar and Rómestámo, Darkness-Slayer and East-Helper and changes his mind as to whether they “failed”; in one of the last things he wrote, Tolkien states:

“Their task was to circumvent Sauron: to bring help to the few tribes of Men that had rebelled from Melkor-worship, to stir up rebellion ... and after his first fall to search out his hiding (in which they failed) and to cause [?dissension and disarray] among the dark East ... They must have had very great influence on the history of the Second Age and Third Age in weakening and disarraying the forces of East ... who would both in the Second Age and Third Age otherwise have ... outnumbered the West.“

So it seems that rather than being abject failures, Tolkien decided that the two Blue Wizards played a great role in keeping a great chunk of Easterlings out of the wars altogether as provocateurs, diplomats, and destabilisers. In doing so, he massively adjusts the chronology as well; in this later telling, the Blue Wizards didn’t arrive with the other Istari at the start of the Third Age, but had been active in Middle-earth since the middle of the Second Age. In short: who knows? Even Tolkien didn’t seem to know.

Luthien Singing Before Morgoth - Credit: Sepi Donne

Melkor

Melkor is the most powerful of Ilúvatar’s Ainur, and yet he rebelled against the creator and was booted out of “heaven”. Sound familiar? That’s right: in so far as Ilúvatar is the Christian God, Melkor is essentially Middle-Earth’s version of John Milton’s Satan, the fallen angel.

After Ilúvatar and the rest of the Valar gave him a dressing-down for introducing discord into the Ainulindalë, Melkor corrupted a bunch of the lesser Ainur (Maiar) into following him in his rebellion. Once Ea had been created, Melkor was one of the Ainur who begged permission to enter the physical world and shape it - though in secret, Melkor desired power and to dominate the children of Iluvatar when they awoke. In retrospect, the alarm bells should have started to ring when the Valar arrived on the earth (Arda) and Melkor declared himself to be the ruler of the world (uh oh, never a good sign). Manwë, his brother, was a little perturbed by this declaration and summoned a bunch more Ainur to shore up their defences, including his wife Varda.

Melkor was (understandably) afraid of his Sister-in-Law and bounced off to the fringes of the world and spent a while in the distant, remote parts of the world, brooding and scheming. As the Valar began to shape the world and make it beautiful, Melkor spent his precious time trying to sabotage their works in whatever way he could, much to their chagrin. 

Since the sun and moon wouldn’t exist for a long while, Aulë built two very large lamps to light the dwelling-place of the Valar, an island called Almaren. These ginormous lamps on ginormous poles lit up the world with a perfect, even light, and the Valar lived happily on their island. Meanwhile, Melkor, together with his Maiar and monstrous creations, dug out a huge fortress called Utumno, from where his aura of corruption seeped out into the world like a bad smell from a rotten carcass.

Being a dickhead, Melkor took it upon himself to destroy the lamps. Waiting until Tulkas was asleep and the Valar were having a party, Melkor and company attacked both lamps at once, knocking them down and extinguishing the lights. The falling lamps made such a mess of the world that it was shattered, leaving behind big craters that eventually became inland seas. The Valar were, understandably, cheesed off by the destruction of their lamps and their houses, so they upped sticks and moved to the western continent, leaving Middle-earth to Melkor for the time being. With his dominance over Middle-earth secured for the time being, Melkor started pumping out his corruption, along with monstrous, perverse mockeries of natural creatures; presumably, he was laughing and twirling his moustache the whole time.

When the elves awoke beneath the stars, some of them ran away from Oromë; Melkor snatched a few of them and dragged them back to Utumno. It’s a big debate, and Tolkien never really settled on an answer in his lifetime, but plenty of readers believe that these captive elves were subject to immense cruelty at the hands of Melkor, who used a combination of torture and forced breeding to turn them into Orcs, as a mockery of the Children of Iluvatar and in mockery of Iluvatar himself.

Eventually, skipping over a lot of intervening drama, the Valar, fighting alongside the elves and men, managed to capture Melkor (or Morgoth, as he became known), chain him up, and throw him through the Doors of Night, casting him into the eternal void, there to do no more harm to the Children of Iluvatar. Even so, prophecy says he’ll come back at the end of days to cause trouble once again.
