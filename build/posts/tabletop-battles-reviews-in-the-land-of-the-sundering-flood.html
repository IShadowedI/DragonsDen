Tabletop Battles Reviews: In the Land of the Sundering Flood

September 15th, 2026 via Goonhammer

For various virtually unbelievable reasons, I’ve been spending a lot of time walking in the footsteps of William Morris, the great artist, craftsperson, visionary and theorist of the arts and crafts movement*. Morris has inspired my work in the hobby for a long time, but often forgotten is his foundational inspiration of what we now call fantasy, largely through his posthumously published novel The Sundering Flood.

*to the extent all photos in this article are of Mammoth Miniatures models photographed in the grounds of the William Morris Museum.

The Sundering Flood is one of the first fantasy novels as we’d now understand it, a foundational work that caught the imagination of Tolkien and all that followed. If it’s the grandfather of modern fantasy, it’s also the ancestor of everything we do here, the wellspring of a lineage that sees us deluged in plastic and metal to this day. Acknowledging and honouring that debt is a hard task, but after a successful Kickstarter in 2025, Nic Evans of Mammoth Miniatures has shown he’s more than up to it, with a fantastic melding of miniature gameplay, Morris’ fantasy and the philosophy of the arts and crafts movement.

That’s the big man’s house in the background In The Land of the Sundering Flood. From Mammoth Miniatures. Credit: lenoon

In the Land of the Sundering Flood takes the form of a small (in all ways) rulebook describing a character driven skirmish game, that focuses as much time and page count on the work of William Morris as it does the core rules. It’s a great example of one of my favourite things in modern miniature gaming, where a designer takes an idea, a theme, even a philosophy, and tries earnestly to translate everything about it into a gaming experience. I think it’s done admirably here, and strongly recommend picking up In the Land of the Sundering Flood as a result.

Gameplay

In the meat of it, this is a skirmish level game where a good sized force will include 4-10 models. It’s a granular and individual model driven game (no squad rules here!), where each model will have its own kit, rules and actions to take. Dice rolls are skill checks, with each character given a dice pool and target values to hit for successes, often with a corresponding opposed roll. It feels detailed - each model moves and acts independently, equipment lists are short but meaningful and models have limited numbers of actions per turn. The focus is very much on what an individual model can do, supported by careful design that makes everyone feel unique. The raw bones of it would work very well as the entirety of an RPGs combat system, especially one where you’re there to tell stories before you fight.

In The Land of the Sundering Flood. From Mammoth Miniatures. Credit: lenoon

Fighting - what you’re going to use this for - is quite RPG styled. You make a skill check to hit (and defend), and, weapon dependent damage is dealt, effects applied, enemies are pushed back and life pools depleted. It’s satisfyingly clear, with visually defined dice pools and obvious target numbers. Two non-combat themed characters trying to swing fists into each other will feel pretty pillowy - but that’s what range (and weirds) are for. There’s no batch or fast rolling here - a combat is a back and forth of manually rolled dice, where elements form patterns that repeat (the pattern might be a giant squashes a skinner over and over again, but whatever the pattern is, it’s still a pattern). It’s all by hand, with a clean and clear process that’s fast enough to play but not fast enough to make combats a blur of “4s then 3s and 5s to save”. That’s all very Morris-y (not very Morrissey, an entirely different and shitty thing), the gaming equivalent of carefully lifting and placing print blocks or moveable type - deliberate, calculated, on theme but not archaic or laborious.

That’s a good line for all of it really, because for all the retro styling, the gameplay experience is modern and straightforward. There’s very little rerolling and no play by intent, fast rolling is out (nothing makes you roll enough dice to need it), but it’s not a clunky, slow, look-it-up-in-the-rule book play experience. A read through gives you enough to get going, and then scenario play gives you more than enough variety to dig in. Assembling armies is pleasantly loose but not horrifically open to abuse. You can take mighty heroes, but you’re also going to take the guys that tag along with them, who feel neither meaningless nor superpowered. You can (and should! Feels right) hand write your lists out, and games last long enough for collaborative storytelling without feeling like DnD with even more minis.

That’s quite an important point, as in the land of the sundering flood is explicitly “a stand alone knightly combat game or part of a larger narrative adventure game”. It’s written as the combat section of an adventure - terrain is taken as a given to be themed (“it is admirable and joyous to construct a true landscape in which to play”), and provided scenarios give a flavour of different ways you can (should? Must? Are called to?) play the game. The presumption is always that there’s a reason your models are where they are, that they have a job to do (or weird to fulfill), that each game is a slice of time within the land of the flood.

Theming

This is a game clearly written and designed from the ground up with the source material in mind. There’s a lot of games that claim that, but few stick to it. You can certainly play MESBG to the battles of the books and the films, but mechanically it doesn’t reflect how the characters operate in the fiction. Aragorn can (and will) scythe through Uruk Hai, but he lacks a mechanism to make his speech at the gate of the deep. Here though, the base mechanics and the wyrd combine to make this the game of the book (TM) - deliberate, crafted and occasionally magical. Hero characters will mow down opponents until they run into a suitably destined fight, the kind that a chapter might lead up to. The fey manipulate the skein of reality for purposes unknown and unseeable, and their effects are tricksy. This kind of design permeates everything in the book, from the look down to the simplest mechanics. Movement is a good example - characters have compound movement values, a knight will have a speed of L (9cm), while a wily fae could be S+S (10cm). A model can move up to its full movement, or partially, but the sequencing is important. You move the knight once (L), but you must move the fae twice (s+s). It’s a small difference but a subtle one, where a creature that flits across the field and a storming warrior feel different as they move. The player interacts with them in different ways, a materiality of play that lends both thematic and para-ludic meaning to the action. The craftsperson takes deliberate time to move, twice, by hand. All very William Morris, and a testament to the attention to detail put into the game.

In The Land of the Sundering Flood. From Mammoth Miniatures. Credit: lenoon

This carries on beyond the rules, into the look, feel and language of the game. This is, very simply, a gorgeous book. Illustrations are beautiful, a vintage fantasy styling drawn by Nick at Mammoth Miniatures that pulls Morris’ work through the lens of Ian Miller. Character illustrations are exceptional - Mammoth Miniatures always has good form here - with striking and occasionally arresting use of black and white to bring forth evocative resonance of creatures and heroes unknown. Rules and background pages have absolutely gorgeous borders that create a touch of grimdark fantasy to the Morris and Burne-Jones style. Even the text - set in (what else!) Morris and Co type. The amazing thing, really, is that none of this gets in the way of clarity. Rules are presented well, the game is straightforward to understand and stat blocks are gorgeously clear and visually obvious. It’s a hell of a compliment to give, but it looks, feels and plays like something the arts and crafts movement would have come up with. I can see those strange, blessed, visionary men and women sitting in the Red House late of an evening - someone pulls out the board (exquisite, of course), and the dice. Perhaps they talk a little of socialism and hope for the future before the first dice is rolled and the game begins.

It might be a bit of an indulgence, but I think that setting is important. This game is designed to play among friends who are into it, into the whole thing, who will adore the typesetting and the marginalia, and who will ignore the fact that one of the scenarios cuts off the forces so you don’t have a strict points limit. This is a good game, mechanically speaking, but it won’t appeal to everyone. If your hobby is all about systems mastery, shooting angles and being crowned champion of champions, then it isn’t really for you. You might like it, of course, but this is game as story, as art form. It’s a couple of lovingly painted miniatures (hand sculpted, of course!) playing over a meter square of terrain, telling a story of Knightly combat, faerie caprice and sinister villainy. It’s the kind of game where you interrupt your turn to give a speech, or you play as the culmination of a weekend’s collective painting and discussion. It begs to be played in a drawing room by the fire, or in the park in the footsteps of the man himself.

Delving and Spinning

In The Land of the Sundering Flood. From Mammoth Miniatures. Credit: lenoon

Land of the sundering flood is a game that hews close to the source in telling a story through structure and mechanic, a place where the how and why of writing is equal to the output. Fittingly, the rules, models, story and feel of the game is artisan - boutique, but mass producible, overall, handmade. There’s a joy in the handmade and I think there’s something here that speaks to it. I don’t tend to agree with the strict oldhammer position - everything should be sculpted in green stuff and milliput, because I think design can be computer aided in the same way it is aided by brush or pin - but to some extent I do sympathise with it. It is a rejection of the modern; a rejection of the insidious influence of capital, of growth, of the idea that a miniatures company can - and is - somewhere in the FTSE 250. It can hark back to a golden age with all the nostalgic poison of fascism, sometimes, but it can also celebrate a love of the game as opposed to of the release schedule. There’s definitely a good way and a bad way to draw from the past, where we can drown in nostalgia or reinvent through a modern lens - here, mammoth have managed the latter. Land of the sundering flood speaks to the process of capital in the miniatures hobby much as the man himself spoke to it in art and design. It is ok to slow down, to move by hand, to accept the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune without reroll or probability smoothing, to spend your time playing a story that could have come from the prose Edda instead of Abnett. Our luck is that we can look forward, much as Morris did, and take what is good and just about the present and combine it with what is worth remembering about the past, and luckily we have games like Land of the Sundering flood to show us how. We have gained so, so much from the way the modern hobby, its rulesets and models, operates, and we live in a better time for hobbyists that ever before as a result - but games like in the land of the sundering flood remind us not of what we have lost, but what we still have before us. Art, beauty, peace, pleasure. Morris, I feel, would be proud.
