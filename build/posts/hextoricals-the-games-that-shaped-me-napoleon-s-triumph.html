Hextoricals: The Games That Shaped Me - Napoleon's Triumph

July 30th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Welcome to the first iteration of Games That Shaped Me. This is meant as a series of essay-style articles that showcase some of the games that had the biggest impact on my tastes of board wargames. We will start with Napoleon’s Triumph, an incredible game that simulates the Battle of Austerlitz, one of Napoleon’s most important victories during the War of the Third Coalition.

I’ve always been somewhat of a collector of board games; when I start getting into a particular series or genre, I have a voice that compels me to buy every single possible iteration. Many that have those collectionist instincts tend to buy games as display pieces, proud of the fact that they have perfectly shrink-wrapped copies of a game, and that to open them would taint that perfect state, as well as hurting their potential resale value. My own philosophy is that games are objects to be played and interacted with and not just looked at longingly, and thus my own ritual is that as soon as I get a new game, I will go through the process of sorting pieces, clipping counters, bagging all the required materials in the hope that one day the game will get played, even if that possibility is remote.

This, of course, instantly plummets the resale value of a game, but to leave the game untouched and unexplored seems to be a bigger heresy to me; their pieces should be ready to be used at a moment’s notice, in my view. An unprepared game loses that exciting potential; the possibility space of “well let’s just try a few turns” instantly evaporates if the game is not ready for play. Such a lost opportunity would haunt me more than the potential for a game to be permanently damaged, or lose a crucial piece.

So, what does the above have to do with Napoleon’s Triumph? Napoleon’s Triumph is somewhat of a collectionist’s dream: it was a small press game, published by a visionary designer in 2007, with high quality components and an extremely small press run, even for a board game. A pristine copy of the game, still in shrink, hovers around 300 dollars at the moment, with even copies listed as “Good” or “Very Good” selling for around that price. If I'd kept my copy in shrink, I probably could have made a profit on it. My copy, however, is beat up, the corners worn down by the countless times I have bagged the game to bring it to a friend’s house, with some of the stickers on the metal flags peeling and badly applied, a consequence of the difficulty that I had applying them, more than a decade ago. And yet, this game is still one of my most valued possessions, and I would not part with it under any offer, no matter how high.

The initial setup and full map of Napoleon's Triumph. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

Napoleon’s Triumph is a design by Rachel Simmons, who prior to this game had only released Bonaparte at Marengo, highlighting another of Napoleon’s great victories. One of the interesting things about Rachel Simmons is that her games do not attempt to be historically accurate: there are no detailed orders of battles in her games and most of the pieces tend to be interchangeable and generic. Her interest therefore tends to be two-fold; showcasing the aesthetics of a battle, as well as trying to elicit a specific type of feeling within the players.

The latter is achieved by the differing objectives in each of her titles: Bonaparte at Marengo (and its remake, Triomphe a Marengo), starts with the Austrian crossing the Bormida, forcing the French to defend at all costs, in order to prevent a breakthrough by the superior Austrian forces, before reinforcements arrive and the tide swings in Napoleon’s favour.

Austerlitz has a similar ebb and flow, where the victory conditions require the Allied forces to make a desperate attack against the French. The French player, however, can bring their reinforcements in at any time, but if they do, they are then forced into the offensive, with their opponent now only needing to hold their position in order to win. This drastic change in objectives makes what in other games would be a pre-programmed reinforcement into a strategic decision for the French player: can they defeat the Allied forces without bringing in reinforcements, or will they need to storm the Pratzen Heights in order to secure their victory?

The Allies threaten the French troops holding Sokolnitz and Telnitz. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

This level of decision making is present throughout the design, including the initial disposition of your forces. Napoleon’s Triumph is known as a block game, and one of the features of these types of games is that the strength of your units is hidden from your opponents and only revealed as part of the attack resolution. During set up, each side has a number of units, ranging from 1 to 3 strength, and divided into infantry, cavalry and artillery; you have absolute freedom to set these up as a wish, with the only limitation being that every corps has to be assigned a certain number of blocks.

This means that there are elements of bluffing even when setting up your troops: a flank might look strong just due to the sheer number of blocks that are present, but they might just constitute the weakest forces in your army. There’s also choices to be made in terms of where you place your best units; the 3-strength heavy cavalry and your guard infantry, both of which incur a morale penalty when used. Is it better to stack them in a single powerful reserve corps to be used only in emergencies, or sprinkle them throughout your army so that they instantly help a troublesome location?

I’ve already outlined how the game breaks many of the standard conventions of board wargames, but Napoleon’s Triumph goes one step further, removing a component that many wargamers tend to take for granted; the ever-present die. So how is the randomness of combat adjudicated? Many games have eschewed dice for other methods, including card play and chit pulls, but Napoleon’s Triumph does something that many might consider heretical; it is entirely deterministic, and equal inputs will always provide the same outputs, no matter what.

The Allies pressure the center of the French lines. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

By any right, the game shouldn’t work: the combination of simplified orders of battle and deterministic combat shouldn’t lead to an exciting or fulfilling game, but two elements in the design allow the game to work as a simulation of the battle. First of all, the map; as you might have seen from the pictures in this article, Napoleon’s Triumph does not use a standard hex map, but instead divides the battlefield into locales of various sizes. The different sizes reflect the ease of movement within that locale, with towns and hills being relatively small, and open flat valleys having proportionally larger locales. The sides of the locales (called approaches within the rules) also have specific modifiers, making them either impassable, easier to defend or impracticable for cavalry.

The other important element is that the game as a whole is focused on command and control; at the start of the game, your units will all be part of a corps, making them easy to maneuver, but eventually through both your actions and the ones of your opponent, you will be forced to split off units in order to cover certain approaches. Each side can only command up to a specific number of corps and independent commands, making it impossible to react to every possible threat. The seamless way that the game models the breakdown of command and control as the battle progresses is one of the highlights of Napoleon’s Triumph.

The Allies break through, but the French reinforcements arrive at the scene! Credit: Riccardo Fabris

Another seamless feature of the game is the way that the differences between cavalry, infantry and artillery are modelled. Cavalry has an easier time retreating, able to scarper away from danger without too much trouble. It is also able to declare an attack and then call it a feint, which stops the combat from occurring but can still potentially draw defenders away from the main axis of attack. This means that cavalry is well positioned to act as a screen for your forces, as well as holding the flanks, doing quick feints down the sides in order to break down your opponent’s command and control. Infantry, on the other hand, has a much more defensive role, getting bonuses if they cover the approaches to a locale. Finally, artillery can be used to bombard your opponent and weaken their forces without them being able to reply, although it can be time consuming to set them up in the correct location.

Combat in Napoleon’s Triumph is thus a mixture of trying to figure out where your opponent is weakest, trying to divide his forces through feints and flanks and performing decisive attacks that will rout your opponent, making it even more difficult for them to control their troops. It also highlights the importance of reserves: you might have been able to blunt the attack of your opponent and draw their forces away in order to set up a devastating counter-attack, but if all your forces are spent and weakened, it won’t be possible to exploit the opportunity developing in front of you.

The French left flank is on the attack, surrounding Constantine's corps. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

I’m not a professional historian, and the Napoleonic Wars have never really been my primary focus, but it is incredible to me how many elements of Napoleonic warfare the game is able to elicit with such seemingly counter-intuitive rules. When I first read the rules for Napoleon’s Triumph, they seemed disjointed and replete with exceptions (and, if I do have a critique of the game, it’s that the rulebook is weirdly laid out and difficult to parse), but once I truly learnt the system, it coalesced into a consistent whole. This game is completely unlike anything else I’ve ever played before, and this alone really opened my eyes at what a wargame could achieve.

The rules alone aren’t the only thing that captivates me about the game, however. One of the design elements of Napoleon’s Triumph is that the combination of the map, the blocks and the little flags that act as the corps markers are all meant to elicit a very specific aesthetic, a look and feel that should be familiar to anyone that has opened a history book about the Napoleonic Wars. The bold blue and red colours try to mirror the lines that we so often see in pictorial representations of Napoleonic battles, giving Napoleon’s Triumph an incredible visual impact. This elevates the game, at least from my perspective, into an art piece.

The French smash through, routing Constantine's corps. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

This is one of the reasons why Napoleon’s Triumph is one of my most treasured possessions: it feels like a living art piece, but one coupled with interesting, engaging mechanisms that are designed to make you feel like the generals on either side, rather than a full and accurate representation of the battle. For some people, this will be a point of criticism; the lack of detailed orders of battles and its deterministic combat will, in their eyes, make this a game themed around the Battle of Austerlitz, and not a true simulation of what exactly happened during the contest. They might even be right on this point, but I think the wargaming hobby is big enough to allow space for designers to go beyond the assumptions and traditions of the genre. Striking, bold and daring, the game is truly a triumph, and well-named as such.
