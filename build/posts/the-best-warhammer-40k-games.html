The best Warhammer 40k games

June 4th, 2026 via Wargamer

From tabletop-flavored strategy titles to grungy first person shooters, Games Workshop's grimdark universe has spawned over 70 videogames since Warhammer 40,000 first hit tabletops in 1987. Many of them have, sadly, sucked - but we 40k fans are nothing if not persistent. Gathered for your edification, these are the best Warhammer 40,000 games you can play right now, from recent releases to retro classics.

To dive into our experts' top rated Warhammer 40k videogames - chosen from team Wargamer's all time favorite strategy, action, and story driven 40k games - just read on. Alternatively, we've got the latest info on upcoming 40k games like Total War 40k and Dawn of War 4 - and finally the complete list of all 40k videogames released since 1987, for the history buffs. Just use the links below to find what you need!

The best Warhammer 40k games are:

  • Space Marine 2
  • Rogue Trader
  • Boltgun
  • Darktide
  • Chaos Gate Daemonhunters
  • Dawn of War
  • Battlesector
  • Battlefleet Gothic Armada 2

1. Space Marine 2 (2024)

Saber Interactive / Focus Entertainment

We waited 13 years for a sequel to the 2011 third-person shooter Space Marine, and it wasn't in vain. Warhammer 40k Space Marine 2 hits like a 121-tonne Thunderhawk gunship, offering by far the most visually spectacular 40k videogame ever seen, and femur-shattering combat that
takes the original's genius gameplay and turns it up to 11 (or XI, in Warhammer terms).

Blasting and tearing through hordes of Tyranids and warp-teleporting warbands of Thousand Sons Chaos Space Marines is an ultraviolent delight, and the varied weapons offer a rich selection of methods for eviscerating them. Missions are almost uniformly fantastically designed, and every boss fight is a challenging treat.

The main story is a damp squig, with plenty of explosive set-pieces but not much charisma from the heroes or the villains. A combo of built-for-multiplayer design and dumb AI squadmates make the campaign a real trial to play on your own - we can't recommend this for single player purists.

But if you've got pals to play with (or don't mind matchmaking with randos) SM2's interwoven modes of co-op campaign, PVE Operations missions, and PVP multiplayer matches are fantastic. We really can't overstate how much fun that core combat is, either. This is by far the best Warhammer 40,000 action game yet made.

And the DLC has all been really good - well, except for that one voice pack so bad they refunded everyone who bought it. Battle passes aren't time-limited, so there's no real FOMO, which is by far the most consumer friendly way to monetise this kind of content. It's also been accompanied by a trove of free missions, weapons, and even gameplay modes - Space Marine 2 continues to get better and cheaper.

2. Rogue Trader (2023)

Owlcat Games

No other game captures the immense scale of the Warhammer 40k universe like Rogue Trader. This isometric, party-based CRPG puts you in charge of a city-sized spaceship, and tasks you with building your own dynasty among the stars in a subsector ravaged by warpstorms.

Your custom character is one potential successor to a veteran Rogue Trader, a space-faring merchant queen with a divine mandate to expand the borders of the Imperium through trade and conquest. You won't wait in the wings for long, though - treachery and Chaos plots put your forebear out of the picture and you into the Captain's throne soon enough.

The adventure that follows will take you at least 100 hours to complete. To begin with, you'll be concerned with getting your city-sized void-ship into peak running order. But beyond that you have the conspiracy of your predecessor's murder to unravel, trade deals to broker, adventures tied to your NPC companions' backstories, and an empire to build.

A gamut of systems have been put in place to reflect your status as a profoundly wealthy and powerful individual. Building your trade Empire sometimes happens in story beats, like main-story-missions to make contact with lost worlds and kick their planetary governors into line. But there's a lightweight colony management minigame, in which you'll found settlements, extract resources from them, and shape their development as crises arrive.

Combat happens both in the void of space and in head to head ground fights. The ground combat is particularly visceral, enhanced by appropriately cacophonic audio design for iconic weapons like the boltgun and chainsword. It's enjoyably deep, too, with as many technical options to manipulate the battlefield and layer buffs and debuffs as there are OTT weapons.

To realise a game on this scale, developer Owlcat limited the scope in some areas. Rogue Trader's graphics are effective but unexceptional. The game launched with plenty of bugs, though these have mostly been squashed. It takes a traditional approach of thoroughly separating combat, dialogue, and exploration, and the environments are mostly set-dressing compared to the interactive arenas of Baldur's Gate 3.

But the heart and soul of the game is spot on. Rogue Trader has thousands of lines of dialogue, and the narrative leads absolutely get Warhammer 40k - the horror, inhumanity, and pathos of the Imperium of Man. This shines through particularly well in your NPC companions, who are written to be believably human, yet distinctively alien.

3. Boltgun (2023)

Auroch Digital / Focus Entertainment

If you want to feel like an unstoppable juggernaut of destruction, play Boltgun. A first person shooter inspired by early FPS games like Quake and Doom, it's a game in which you go fast and shoot hordes of enemies into teeny tiny bits - and that's it.

The retro graphics, with sprite-based enemies and low-poly 3D arenas, are a mission statement: this is a pure retro FPS experience. Wearing the big stompy boots of Ultramarines Sternguard veteran Malum Caedo, you'll blast, jump, and chainsword your way through thousands of the followers of Chaos.

You're here to shoot stuff, and boy does shooting stuff feel great. The game's armory is realised with loving care and attention. They're lovingly realised as pixel models, but the audio feedback is where they really shin. The meltagun roars like Cerberus, the heavy bolter thunders like an industrial press, and destroyed enemies come apart with a sound like a beef carcass being thrown into an industrial fan.

It's not just vibes, though - there's surprising tactical depth behind your choice of weapons. Just like the tabletop game, weapons have a Strength score and enemies a Toughness score - you'll do only marginal damage if you attempt to hit a baddy with an outclassed weapon. Each enemy has a distinctive role in combat, from the hindering nuisance of the Nurglings to the killer deathrays of a Lord of Change.

While the graphics are simple, the level design is excellent, both visually and as combat arenas. Colossal forge complexes, Chaos transdimensional gateways, and the warped interior of a corrupted space-ship represent the massive scale of 40k beautifully - though an in-game map would have been very helpful on some of the more sprawling levels.

You get to explore those locations at high speed, with some movement conveniences that the Doomslayer and Mr Quake never had access to in the '90s. An automatic ledge grab and mantle climb make jumping a little easier; the bullrush charge lets you close rapidly on distant shooters and splat smaller foes entirely; and your chainsaw attack can help you clear even greater distancers by pulling you magnetically towards your target. It all feels great.

4. Darktide (2022)

Fatshark

If you're a fan of both co-op multiplayer FPS games and Games Workshop's grim, dark, bloodstained far future, Darktide will blow your mind (especially when playing as the psyker character, who can literally blow enemies' minds by exploding their heads with concentrated warp energy).

Warhammer 40k Darktide is effectively a 40k-flavored reincarnation of its bestselling co-op slash-em-up Vermintide 2, which was based in the Old World fantasy setting. You play a 'reject' part of an Imperial Inquisitor's warband, completing various missions in a hive city that's overrun with servants of the Chaos god Nurgle.

Each of the classes offers a different core ability and playstyle, its own variety of guns, armor, and wargear, and a different library of conversational voice lines that'll play out in procedurally generated conversations between the characters as you fight through waves of chaos-warped enemies, complete challenges, and fight disgusting bosses.

The voice lines can start to grate a little over time, but there's enough variety that it still works well (and it helps that the voice acting and writing are both excellent, with superstar Black Library author Dan Abnett on the team).

The game's environments are frequently stunning, an impressively faithful, atmospheric rendering of 40k's unique aesthetics. You'll be fighting through the spires and flying buttresses of futuristic gothic rooftops one mission, and trudging through oppressive, grimy industrial undercities the next. Until Space Marine 2 came out, it was the best simulation of the Warhammer 40k world we've seen in a videogame - and when it comes to really delving deep into the grotty underbelly of the Imperium, it remains unbeaten.

And you're not just constrained to the underhive any more! The recent Beyond the Hive expansion added the new Expedition mode which sends your rejects into the Deadside radzones. These massive open environments are almost a complete new game in their own right - open ended (dare we say 'open world?') environments with more than a hint of Helldivers about them. And there's a touch of extraction shooter in here too, as you'll have free reign about when you call the Valkyrie to haul your asses out of the fire. 

 

5. Chaos Gate Daemonhunters (2022)

Complex Games / Frontier Developments

Warhammer 40k Chaos Gate Daemonhunters just steals the crown from Mechanicus as the best game like XCOM set in the 40k universe. In Daemonhunters, you take command of a strike force of Grey Knights - an elite, secretive chapter of Space Marines that are all psykers - and use their psychic might and advanced wargear to combat incursions by Chaos Daemons.

You'll build a base, train and equip a team of soldiers, and deploy them on various missions to hold back an oncoming, apocalyptic threat - a new, deadly galactic plague cooked up by the Chaos god Nurgle.

The various different classes of Grey Knights available for your roster (as well as the Duty Eternal DLC's Venerable Dreadnought and Imperial Assassins in the Execution Force expansion) each offer different skill trees to upgrade and specialise throughout the game - so long as you keep them alive.

Combine that with a ton of unlockable weapons and wargear items, plus cosmetic customisation options coming out the wazoo, and you've got a 'war-barbie' experience second only to collecting and painting miniatures in real life.

Like others in the genre, Chaos Gate Daemonhunters' race-against-time campaign is a tough strategic challenge, forcing you to manage limited resources and prioritise your missions carefully against a stronger, better-resourced, ever-growing opponent.

In a twist on the XCOM DNA, your superhuman Space Marines' shots will always hit their target (no soul-crushing percentage hit chances here) and you're given a lot of handy contextual information to inform your tactics before you commit to a movement.

But every few turns you'll hit a Warp Surge, which grants your Nurgle-loving enemies a random chaos boon. These range from temporary damage boosts to mid-game reinforcements. Missions reward quick thinking, aggressive tactics, and canny use of specialists' abilities; it's excellently done.

Like XCOM 2 before it, Daemonhunters can be an exhaustingly intense experience, where losses are all but inevitable. But the blend of strategy and tactics is on point, the core gameplay is very satisfying, and the Warhammer 40k lore well realised. If you like both XCOM and Warhammer, this is essential.

6. Dawn of War - Definitive Edition (2025)

Relic Entertainment / THQ

Back to the classics now: 2004's Dawn of War is a giant which still stands up as one of the best RTS games ever made. Though it pioneered many concepts that would drive the RTS genre forwards in more heroic, tactical directions, it remains rooted in the base-building RTS era.

Developer Relic struck gold by simplifying and streamlining many classic RTS mechanics. Infantry are spawned in easy to control squads infantry, not individual figures that have to be cursor-lassoed together. Resources come from taking and holding battlefield strongpoints, not faffing with ancillary farmer units.

Base-building is lightweight, strongly focused on unlocking military units and upgrades. This game, like its tabletop counterpart, wants you to leave the admin at home, get out there, and make war.

More importantly than all of that, it gets the feel right. Dawn of War has just enough lore-appropriate flavour to sate players with a shelf full of lovingly painted Dark Angels right above their monitor, but not so much as to confuse others, or bog down the game's inherent strengths as a fast-paced, aggressive RTS.

Over its lifespan, Dawn of War acquired three chunky expansion packs, first Winter Assault, and then the expandalone Dark Crusade and Soulstorm. These added new singleplayer campaigns, new forces for multiplayer, and even a lightweight grand strategy layer that saw you battle for control of a whole planet. The game also attracted a sterling modding community, adding in missing armies and the largest, most ridiculous units - Imperial Titans.

There's a rock-solid gameplay foundation, but the original game is quite crunchy on the eyes. The memory limitations of the original 32-bit game engine also couldn't keep up with the ambitions of modders. But you don't have to play the original game any more - in 2025 Relic Entertainment released the Definitive Edition of Dawn of War.

Definitive Edition follows the original game so closely you might wonder what it's done - other than bundle in all the original expansions, and provided a welcome but hardly revolutionary graphical upgrade - to justify a modern price point.

That would be the complete rework of the game code to make it compatible with modern 64 bit computer systems. Not only does this future proof the game to keep it running on modern systems, it lets the game take advantage of the huge memory in modern PCs. Since the game is so close to the original version, it is largely compatible with existing mods, and becoming more so as the developers at Relic and mod makers work to close the gap. If there was ever a modding community that could use more ram, it's the Dawn of War scene…

7. Battlesector (2021)

Black Lab Games / Slitherine

As incurable tabletop 40k megafans, we are suckers for the concept of a turn-based PC wargame that nails not only the 40k vibe, but also the gamefeel of building and fighting tabletop armies - and that's exactly what the wargame maestros at Slitherine have created in Battlesector.

We had our issues with it on release, but there's no doubting its finely tuned, compulsive core tactical gameplay, as you carefully advance, position, and direct your specialized units, managing a careful balance of buffs and abilities to obliterate as many superbly recreated, grimdark baddies as possible every turn.

In the three years since release, developer Black Lab has tweaked and improved a massive amount of Battlesector's rougher edges - not to mention adding five full new playable factions, from the pitiless metal Necrons to the high-tech mech-loving Tau Empire. If you want to play real-life 40k across a range of lovingly realized, atmospheric battlefields, Battlesector is the closest you're going to get - just get ready for many unplanned hours of 'one more turn'…

8. Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2 (2019)

Tindalos Interactive / Focus Home Entertainment

In the grim darkness of the 41st millennium, there is only war. But the vast majority of 40k games tend to focus on 'grounded' fighting, whether that be on the surface of embattled planets, inside the tossing, red-lit corridors of a freshly-boarded warship, or deep within the pitch-dark, drifting belly of a Space Hulk.

Not so amid the cosmic naval warfare of Battlefleet Gothic, first launched as a spaceship fleet-based miniatures game in 1999, and revived for PC by developer Tindalos in 2016, three years after the dwindling tabletop line was finally canned by Games Workshop; and the 2019 sequel is the best execution of strategic void warfare yet.

You take command of a flotilla of 40k's preposterous spacefaring warships: colossal, flying gothic cathedrals with more buttresses, spires, and crenellations than a small European city. You'll through fleet-on-fleet battles on a fixed horizontal plane, against vessels from a selection of humanity's many deadly enemies.

On top of that, there are three fully fledged campaigns in the base game. There's a certain level of 4x gameplay here, as you secure control over different systems and muster your armies, and while the Imperial campaign is the 'default', the Tyranid Hive Fleets and robotic Necrons both offer very interesting variants. The strategic value of a planet if fundamentally different when all you want to do is eat it, after all.

With their slow turning circles and prow-mounted broadsides, there's a lot in common between the Imperial Navy and 19th century ships of the line. But the many fleets of the Imperium and its enemies - both servants of chaos and alien xenos races - wage war in the void in starkly different ways, from the nimble and fragile Aeldari to the ram-fronted Ork ships, and as well as the three campaign factions there are loads more to tussle with in multiplayer or skirmish mode.

9. Mechanicus (2018)

Bulwark Studios / Casedo Games

The Adeptus Mechanicus is a faction of technology-worshipping cyborg mystics from Mars, whose prevailing mission is to amass hoards of technological wisdom and understanding - primarily by scouring the galaxy for rare, complex or esoteric gadgets, stealing them, and taking them home to tinker with in the garage for the rest of time.

This, in a nutshell, is the plot of Kasedo Games' 2018 turn-based tactical squad battler, Mechanicus. You take command of a small but growing squad of Adeptus Mechanicus tech-priests, tasked with exploring worlds in search of hi-tech plunder. So far, so good.

Once you get down to the surface, though, things start getting all spooky, geometric, green, and glowing real fast. Those subterranean treasure vaults you saw on the scanners were, it seems, actually stasis crypts full of Necrons - soulless, ancient robotic killers - and they are none too pleased to be woken up by a bunch of half-organic grave robbers trying to nick their laptops.

So begins your distinctly XCOM-inspired progression through a series of increasingly challenging - but quite similar - grid-based maps, carefully managing your tech-priests' limited action points to complete objectives, while avoiding vaporisation in the neon green beams of Necron gauss weapons.

Mechanicus is here in no small part because of its strikingly faithful, vivid, and unapologetically weird portrayal of the Adeptus Mechanicus - a lovably eccentric but fairly high-concept sci-fi faction that it would have been so easy to deal with in a half-arsed way.

From the characters' campy, cyborg-ish names made up of jumbled numerals and Greek letters, down to the fizzling data-blurts of their part-sonic, part digital language, Kasedo did the AdMech properly.

If you like the sound of XCOM-like gameplay with a few smart twists, a surprisingly excellent narrative, battling cyber-mummies in a dusty crypt, and having USB sticks for fingers, this is the one for you.

10. Space Hulk: Tactics (2018)

Cyanide / Focus Entertainment

Games Workshop's original Space Hulk board game released in 1989 to some acclaim, pitting the Space Marines' elite Terminator warriors against scythe-clawed alien fiends in a lightless, terrifying cosmic shipwreck. It was "heavily inspired by" (lovingly riped-off from) James Cameron's Aliens, translated into the 40k setting, and in that regard was an absolute success.

Subsequent editions in 1996, 2009, and 2014 sold well to a growing cult audience, especially 2014's fourth edition, which arrived at the very start of Warhammer's recent rennaissance. Space Hulk on the tabletop, therefore, has earned its place as one of Warhammer 40k's most distinguished and popular games. The digital adaptations? Not so much.

There are no fewer than eight videogames bearing the Space Hulk name (if you count its 2005 outing on the Nokia N-Gage), but none has made an impact anywhere near the scale of their board-based grandpappy - none, that is, except developer Cyanide's breakout 2018 adaptation, Space Hulk: Tactics.

The core of Tactics is a direct remake of the board game, adding the characterful and well-observed 3D graphics to turn out a perfectly portable PC and console port. Around that core is a new system of cards with which further customise your chosen team of Blood Angels' abilities, and hopefully turn the tide of battle at the game's crucial moments.

Add to that the twin campaigns - one seeing you control the Blood Angel Space Marines against the Genestealers, the other flipping the table to control the 'Nids against Ultramarines Astartes centuries earlier on the same ship. It's a single-player feast.

If you like 40K, slick digital board game adaptations, and spending multiple hours scant grid squares away from slavering alien bioweapons that want to eat your organs, you're in the right place. Recommended.

11. Dawn of War 2 (2009)

Relic Entertainment / THQ

In every Warhammer 40k videogames thread, a divide quickly forms between those reformists who prefer the squad-level, RPG-inspired narrative campaigns of Dawn of War 2, and the base-building competitive RTS purists, for whom the OG Dawn of War is a masterpiece, and the sequel an ill-considered damp squib.

Both the original Dawn of Wars are superb in their own right - but each lends itself to a very different style of play, and aims to create different kinds of experience for the player. Dawn of War 2 distils, streamlines, nips and tucks the original to create an altogether different beast. Sprawling, wide-open maps are squeezed down into graphically lusher, but completely linear, railroaded environments.

The quickly-snowballing hordes of units you once flung across the map with abandon are replaced with just a couple of persistent squads, led by named characters, with RPG-like equipment upgrades, and skill progression mechanics. Base-building is all but erased from the game, buildings reduced to inert terrain pieces from which to respawn your (very limited) support units, should they fall.

But, despite what the Dawn of War 1 purists say, in this case, less is in many ways more. DoW2's narrower focus gives it more room to expand from the original's faithful, yet somewhat superficial portrayal of the 40K universe, baking more detailed storytelling, character interactions and dialogue into the mission-to-mission gameplay, rather than just CGI cutscenes.

You're able to select from a choice of squads to take on each sortie, decking each out with different wargear and skills each time to mix up their in-game tactics, and finding out what works best for you, which - frankly - is a whole ton of fun.

It'll soon have you picking your favourite squads over others, getting a feel for their abilities, their tactical utility, their weaknesses, and their leaders' occasional witty banter, both on- and off-mission. This draws you into the game's storytelling in a way that's entwined with the gameplay, rather than tacked awkwardly onto it, as is so often the case in classic RTS campaigns.

We could go on for pages about the innovative storytelling in DoWII, its beautifully-constructed missions, and the ingenious, maddeningly addictive and shockingly ahead-of-its-time Last Stand horde mode, but, honestly, your time would be better spent just playing the game. Just go and get it, right now. We mean it.

12. Gladius - Relics of War (2018)

Proxy Studios / Slitherine Studios

Where Warhammer 40k the tabletop miniature wargame puts you in charge of an army, Gladius - Relics of War puts you in charge of a complete military industrial complex. It's the only Warhammer 40k 4X game so far.

If you've not heard of 4X, it stands for Explore, Expand, Exploit and Exterminate - your four key tasks as you establish, then grow, your 41st-millennium planetary empire, developing cities and training stacks of Necron Warriors or Tactical Space Marine units just as you might Spearmen or Archers in a game of Sid Meier's Civilization VI.

The city-settling, infrastructure-building, 'one more turn' model of strategy is a slightly odd fit for the violence-first world of Warhammer 40k.

But, on the whole, Gladius works surprisingly well. The visuals are gorgeous, if let down by some unremarkable and slightly immersion-breaking janky animations (it ain't no Civ in that department) and, while those hoping for hardcore wargame levels of mechanical depth will come away hungry, there's enough meat to the factions' tech trees and progression to provide a respectable, if quite easy-going strategy package overall.

Gladius has a surprising amount in common with Endless Legends. Similarly to that game, the map is divided into regions, each of which can support one city - less free-wheeling than Civ, and one that makes the founding (or conquest) of a city a particularly big deal. As a city grows it will expand into neighbouring hexes, eating up their resources and producing specialised nodes. The birds-eye view of tiny 40k cityscapes is a treat - as it blowing them up.

If you're a 40k fan who cut their gaming teeth on Civilization (on a lower difficulty setting, at least) don't sleep on Gladius; there's a lot of fun to be had here.

13. Space Marine (2011)

Relic Entertainment / THQ

Warhammer 40,000: Space Marine, the epic, gore-drenched odyssey of Ultramarines Captain Titus achieves something Warhammer computer games almost never can: it actually makes us care about its main character. Not that this is a complicated tale - you go to a human factory planet besieged by Orks, kill thousands of Orks, realise it was Chaos' nefarious doing all along, kill the Chaos leader, the end, then it's straight home for tea and nutrient paste.

No, we care about Titus because he's the most impressively realised, most relatable and believable Space Marine warrior ever in a videogame, and, by placing us in his massive ceramite boots, this game gets closer than any other to recreating on screen the peculiarly gripping power fantasy of being a transhuman Astartes warrior.

Titus' 8-foot-tall, 6-foot-wide bulk towers over human-sized NPCs and scampering greenskin enemies alike, making you feel like one of the magnificent gods of war so reverently described in Black Library novels. Every decibel of his voice - the inimitable and perfect booming tones of Mark Strong - projects the grave, dutiful assiduousness of an Ultramarine.

His every animated step, lunge, swing and leap is perfectly tuned to feel impossibly powerful and weighty, conveying an uncanny sense of superhuman heaviness that makes Gears of War seem a bouncy, air-filled platformer by comparison.

The game's atmosphere is treacle-thick, bloody, and intoxicatingly 40K. While the environments are an endless succession of near-identical corpse-strewn industrial zones, it really doesn't matter, because all you'll care about is the banging of your boltgun's explosive shells and the rhythmic roar of your chainsword swinging up and down, carving Orks into green-and-red jelly.

Yes, Space Marine 2 is out now, and outstrips the 2011 original in most ways - but the first game has seen a bump in sales since SM2 came out for a good reason. It's still required gaming for 40k fans because, more than any other title before it, it gets, on a deep level, the particular escapism baked into Games Workshop's pre-eminent sci-fi 'heroes' - gigantic shoulder pads and all.

But there are two blemishes on this otherwise deeply agreeable game. First, the original version of the game - now sold as Anniversary Edition - retails for a frankly silly price given its age. Then there's the slightly more expensive Master Crafted edition which, at time of writing, was less stable than the original version of the game.

14. Sanctus Reach (2017)

Straylight Entertainment / Slitherine

Another turn based battler 2017's Sanctus Reach is a clear forerunner to Battlesector, in that you'll be assembling tabletop-style army lists, deploying your forces, and fighting it out over objective points. To be clear, it's not as new and pretty as Battlesector, its campaign missions and tactical mechanics aren't as varied or interesting, and there's less faction variety (counting DLC, Battlesector now boasts six to Sanctus Reach's four).

So why do we still recommend it as one of the best 40k games around? Firstly, it has some features Battlesector doesn't, which make it a different and worthwhile play. There's destructible terrain and cover that changes the battlefield as you go - and you'll also be ramping up to slightly larger battles, including titanic units like Imperial Knights and Orky Gorkanauts and Morkanauts, adding a sense of scale Battlesector lacks.

Secondly - and maybe we're just wearing rose-tinted specs here - as a 2017 release, it's one of the last 40k games to release before 8th edition, the return of Roboute Guilliman, and the advent of the Primaris Space Marines. Firstborn marines, in all their 90s-style stumpy glory, play the starring role here, alongside the classic Castraferrum 'box' Dreadnoughts that Games Workshop has since so cruelly slashed from the codex and its stores.

Oldmarines are still plentiful in Battlesector, and they're hardly done dirty - all have important tactical roles - but they're sometimes upstaged by your shiny new Inceptors, Aggressors, and Invictor Warsuits. If you love Battlesector and want something similar, but with a bit more scale and more of a retro 40k feel, Sanctus Reach is right up your alley.

15. Final Liberation: Epic 40,000 (1997)

Holistic Design Inc.

It's an empirically proven fact that Warhammer 40k videogames have gotten markedly better after the year 2000 than they were before it (we think it's because of y2k, but we can't prove it) - but there are exceptions to every rule, and Final Liberation is one of them.

Released in 1997 (that's after the printing press, but before the breakdown of the western liberal-democratic order, history fans), this is a strategic level computer wargame based on Epic - GW's late 90s 40k spinoff, which simulated much larger-scale battles and campaigns by using teeny, tiny 6mm scale figures, rather than the standard 28mm or 32mm-high guys.

Channeling that same scale in digital form, Final Liberation sees you marshal a huge combined force of Ultramarines, Astra Militarum fire support, and tower-block sized Titans, and direct it across entire continents to fight off a colossal Ork Waaagh! by means of deadly but rewarding turn based tactical battles.

The 1990s are often remembered fondly as a 'golden age' of Warhammer 40k, driven by sheer bombast and silliness, rather than the mass market manipulations of today, and this game fits that bill - it's required gaming for new 40k nerds wanting to sample the wildly different vibes of the before-times. But it's also, fundamentally, a compelling large-scale turn-based strategy game that'll have you hooked once you know the ropes.

If you try out Final Liberation and love fighting those larger scale battles, you should also check out Legions Imperialis - GW's new iteration of Epic, set in the Horus Heresy era and launched in 2023.

16. Shootas, Blood & Teef

Rogueside

Shootas, Blood & Teef is the least serious, most delightfully slapstick Warhammer 40k game on this list. This is a side-scrolling run and gun multiplayer platformer in the style of Metal Slug, Broforce, or even Castle Crashers. Up to four allied Orks set out on a violent quest to batter everything in their path with bullets, lasers, explosions, and weaponised squigs.

Developer Rogueside honed their craft for this type of gameplay with the Guns, Gore, and Cannoli series, and the look and feel of SBT is absolutely spot on. The chunky cartoon art style matches the over the top character of the Orks perfectly, and the huge Imperial war machines that appear in some boss fights, like the Baneblade or Knight Valiant, are fantastic.

We have to put a very big caveat here. The story campaign is short, probably around four hours long. There is team-based Ork on Ork multiplayer, with a variety of modes, but that relies on a stable player population.

However, the Steam version of the game has the great 'Remote Play Together' feature: only one player in a multiplayer group needs to buy a copy of the game for their friends to join a session. It's a lot easier to recommend this as a short-lived but high impact experience for a group of chums - you'll get more hours entertainment for much less cost than buying cinema tickets.

17. Eternal Crusade

Behaviour Interactive

Eternal Crusade is a contentious entry, as it is both shutdown and something of a disappointment. The first version of the Eternal Crusade website advertised "third person persistent online action in the first MMORPG of the 41st Millennium": but by the time of launch, the RPG part had entirely gone.

Eternal Crusade was a match based multiplayer third-person shooter, with faction wins factoring into control of an overworld - a bit like Planetside, but without the persistent largescale warzones. Matches played out like Battlefield, with large forces going to war over massive maps that required transport vehicles to traverse.

It was fantastic - sometimes. The rate at which players progressed and unlocked new equipment was snail like - unless they paid for microtransactions - and this was a serious balance problem when certain weapons were all but useless against vehicles.

But it had real highlights: the factions were extremely asymmetrical, the classes all felt like what they were supposed to be, and the netcode for melee duels worked remarkably well. Then there were the multi-stage sieges, which had an excellent rhythm, and always ended in a hopeless (or triumphant) last stand.

The official servers are long dead. But there is an active 'Eternal Crusade Resurrection' community, which operates a fan reimplementation of the game and organises matches via Discord.

18. Inquisitor: Martyr (2017)

NeoCore Games

Inquisitor: Martyr casts you as an agent of the Imperium's shadowy, pseudo-religious, paramilitary secret police force, the Holy Inquisition. Arriving aboard a benighted space station to investigate some suspicious signals, you'll soon embark on a broader quest that sees you travel to other parts of 40k's grim, dark galaxy.

In a manner very familiar to anyone who's played more than ten minutes of Diablo or Path of Exile, you'll gather up shiploads of weapons, gear, upgrades, and consumables from your enemies' mortal remains, upgrading your loadout by equipping the cream of the crop, and recycling all the rest.

There's more than enough abilities to unlock and try out, arranged in three different class trees (shooty, stabby, or psyker) to encourage multiple playthroughs and different character builds. While there are similar content seasons to Path of Exile or Diablo IV, and plenty of extra build options thanks to DLC, this game isn't a standout in its genre - but it gets the basics right, and has some fun ideas.

A lot of thought has gone into making ranged combat genuinely interesting in an ARPG, and a dedicated cover system, and mechanics for suppression fire, do a lot to distinguish it from its peers. While it's always true that multiplayer games are better with friends, that's especially true here, because a party with mixed abilities will let you enjoy your specialisation to the fullest.

If you're a 40k fan who fancies some Diablo-like action, or a Path of Exile grinder who's on a bit of a 40k kick at the moment, this is definitely worth a look. It's regularly on sale, so we don't advise buying it for label price.

Mechanicus 2 (2026)

Mechanicus 2 is the long-anticipated sequel to the XCOM-like strategy game Mechanicus. Released in May 2026, we haven't tested it yet - preparations for Warhammer 40k 11th edition have kept all our writers rather busy this spring. Hence we haven't given this a rank yet- we don't know where it belongs! But it's a priority for us to try out, because we adored the first game, and we came away from our preview session eager to get stuck in.

Mechanicus 2 expands on the original's formula with a hugely expanded scope. The Necrons join the Adeptus Mechanicus as a playable faction with their own campaign and distinctie progressoin mechanics. The cast of memorable characters from the first game take on a larger role, joining your forces in battle and changing how your force fights. They even get fully voiced dialogue this time - though there's an option to return to Lingua Technis technobabble if you prefer.

Upcoming Warhammer 40k videogames to watch

Honestly, 2025 was rather quiet year for 40k videogame releases. The only prominent new licenced titles were, er, not new - they were remasters of the original Dawn of War and Space Marine games. Both were competent and welcome, in our view, but neither offered anything fresh to savor.

Looking ahead to the rest of 2026 and beyond, though, we have a TON to look forward to. Here are the big names to keep in mind and trailers to watch…

Total War: Warhammer 40k

Release window: To be announced

If you thought we were in a golden age of 40k videogames before, you ain't seen nothing yet. After three blockbusting Total War titles in the Warhammer Fantasy world, and nearly ten years of constant fan debate as to whether Creative Assembly could ever pull off the same feat with GW's grimdark far future, the carnodon is finally out of the bag. Total War: Warhammer 40k is real, it's in development, and it's looking pretty damn superb so far.

There's no confirmed release window yet, and we'd suspect it's still a ways off. But, encouragingly, Creative Assembly is already far enough along that its Announce Trailer, released on December 12, 2025, features actual gameplay.

And oh, what gameplay it is. We see dozens of units clashing across a multi-laned ruined hive city map; close ups of Space Marine drop pods smashing down amid orky melees; huge buildings collapsing under fire; and an orbital lance strike nearly evaporating a Stompa. Then, the camera zooms out to give us a coquettish hint at the widening scale of campaign gameplay: first the orbital sphere, then the whole star system.

Across various interviews, as well as CA's Developer Roundtable video released a few days after the initial trailer, we've learned a fair bit about the game's fundamentals. Total War: Warhammer 40k will launch with four playable factions - Space Marines, Astra Militarum, Orks, and Aeldari - but CA intends to add in every tabletop army post release (no word on Zoats or Hrud yet, but give it time).

Planets have multiple cities and sites to conquer, which figure as different types of ground battles. While the exact scale of the campaign isn't entirely clear yet, CA's Steam page for the game describes it as a "galactic sandbox", so it seems likely we'll be fighting over not just planets or systems but, at the very least a whole region of space, perhaps a subsector or two.

There's a huge focus on customization, with both the trailer and other dev appearances highlighting the work CA's doing to let you paint your Astartes chapter, Waaagh!, Regimentum, or Warhost in your choice of colors.

The one area we're still really curious about is how (or even if) voidship battles are going to make it into the game. CA has shown off Imperial ships in the trailer footage, and beautiful concept art for Aeldari and Ork vessels too - but there's no detail yet on whether we'll have real time space naval battles to play, or if that aspect of 41st millennium warfare is taking place off screen for now.

Either way, there's a vast amount still to learn about this title - but it's almost certain to be one of the most momentous Warhammer 40k videogame releases of all time. You better believe we'll be watching this one like a Psyber-Eagle.

Check out Total War: Warhammer 40k on Steam.

Dawn of War 4

Release date: September 17

It's coming. It's finally coming. Announced at Gamescom 2025 in August, Dawn of War 4 is being developed by Iron Harvest studio King Art Games, and we've seen more than enough of the game since then to be extremely excited about what we're going to get.

The initial announcement trailer hits hard.  The CGI cinematics, made by Polish studio Platige Image, feature nostalgic callbacks to the iconic DOW1 intro cutscene (screaming Orks and a tattered Blood Ravens banner) but also a cavalcade of the new, from ravaging Necrons to - trigger warning - the sudden death of recurring series character Cyrus. To learn more about how it was made, read our insider interview with Platige Image.

Right from that first trailer, Dawn of War 4 confidently showed gameplay footage to leave us in no doubt that this game is aiming to be a successor of DOW1, not the more RPG-ish sequel or the deeply unpopular third one. It's a big-armies RTS with base building, y'all!

We also know DOW4 will launch with four full factions: Space Marines (including Dark Angels specific units), Orks, Necrons, and Adeptus Mechanicus.

Since early 2026 King Art has begun dropping more detailed gameplay trailers focusing on each faction. The short but sweet Dark Angels reveal above gives us in-engine glimpses of a few iconic Calibanite units, including Deathwing Terminators and Ravenwing bike squadrons, as well as modern, Primaris era Space Marine units including Repulsor main battle tanks. Oh, and their playable primarch Lion El'Jonson, of course - for about five milliseconds.

January 2026's four minute long Orks Faction Trailer goes into more granular (Waaaghnular?) detail, showcasing a bunch of classic Ork units fans would always have expected and deserved in DOW4. Accompanied by a very fancy British voiceover, we see huge blobs of Boyz and Stormboyz doing their thing, and a closer look at the game's Squighog Riders - a relatively new unit for Warhammer 40k whose models only came out in 2021.

Shootas, Flash Gitz, Deff Dreads, Battlewagons, Gorkanauts, we get a short look at them all firing their guns and punching things. We also learn that there are two force leader options in the Ork kampaign: the Bad Moonz' Gorgutz 'Ead 'Unter (first seen in DOW1's Winter Assault DLC) or Snakebite newcomer Guzcutta, a Beastboss who'll likely work best with the Beast Snagga style units (fast moving melee and squigs aplenty).

In May, the Adeptus Mechanicus Faction Trailer revealed the servants of Mars to the world. The Mechanicus are predominately a ranged force, with long ranged Skitarii Rangers and radioactive Skitarii Vanguard dashing between the legs of nimble Ironstrider Balistarii and lumbering Kastelan Robots. The biggest and most revered weapon in their arsenal is the Knight Castellan, a walking avatar of the Machine God's wrath - an ornate mech suit absolutely bristling with guns.

Leading the Mechanicus are two commanders, Techno-archaeologist Potentia Delta 9, or the Skitarii Marshall Sek-Ix-23. Potentia is more technical, able to despatch servo skulls to scout the landscape ahead and with varied upgrade paths, while Sek-Ix can buff nearby units - and can even be attached to his own infantry squad.

At the Warhammer Skulls preview in May 2026, as well as telling us when the game would release, King Art Games announced that it was bringing back the beloved Crusade mode from the original Dawn of War's Dark Crusade expansion - a globe-trotting campaign of conquest with persistent forces. Check out our interview with the studio's Creative Director Jan Theysen to learn more.

Check out Dawn of War 4 on Steam.

Dark Heresy

Release window: To be announced

Owlcat's Rogue Trader CRPG was a landmark, the first ever 40k CRPG, and the follow up is going to excite OG 40k roleplayers even more: it's an adaptation of Dark Heresy, the original Warhammer 40k tabletop RPG!

In Warhammer 40k: Dark Heresy, you take the role of an acolyte in the service of an all-powerful Inquisitor, tasked with rooting out heresy, corruption, and the insidious plots of aliens, mutants, and heretical cults. As in Rogue Trader, there'll be options to pursue the dogmatic approach of a Puritan, or use the weapons of the enemy and risk becoming an outcast Radical.

Owlcat has promised the game will feature an improved combat system compared to Rogue Trader, and also features a dedicated investigation subsystem. As I put it in my preview of the Beta for our sister site PCGamesN, "it's a detective game that actually makes you do the detective work, lets you get it wrong, and forces you to handle the consequences".

Check out the game on Steam.

Warhammer Survivors

Release window: 2026

Announced in early November 2025, Warhammer Survivors is a bullet heaven game with low-fi pixel art and a choice to play in either the Age of Sigmar and Warhammer 40k universe. Just like the genre-defining Vampire Survivors, your little hero will face off against waves and waves of encroaching enemies, turning them into skull-shaped experience gems that you can then turn into level-ups. Winning means selecting the right upgrades at the right time.

Some fun touches have already been revealed. One of the 40k characters is Malum Caedo, the one-man apocalypse Space Marine character from Boltgun. Not all of the weapon upgrades are entirely serious - you can lob pots of nuln oil over the enemies. Looks like silly fun!

For more on Warhammer Survivors, see the Steam page.

Space Marine 3

Release window: To be announced

Given the runaway success of Space Marine 2 since its release in September 2024, it was kind of inevitable that we'd get Space Marine 3 at some point - and publisher Focus Entertainment Space Marine 3 would happen on Thursday, March 13, 2025. We even got a teaser video (watch it below), but don't get too excited - unless you really like rain.

Unsurprisingly - given the game is most likely at least a year or two away - neither Focus, nor developer Saber Interactive, has shared any hard details about the game, but we do get a few hints. The March 13 press announcement promises SM3 will have "an immersive campaign, a multiplayer mode, and innovations that will redefine the standards of third-person action games".

And Focus' Deputy CEO John Bert says the upcoming third instalment will "take the genre to new heights by introducing large-scale battles that are even more spectacular". That sounds very much like further improvements to the studio's 'Swarm technology', which made massive seas of moving Tyranids possible in Space Marine 2.

Warhammer 40k games FAQ

Games Workshop has been making (or at least licensing other people to make) Warhammer 40,000 videogames since 1992, when George Bush Senior was US President and cellphones were like small bricks. Consequently, there's a lot of gaming trivia for Warhammer fans to dig into - here are a few fun facts to go with our top picks above.

What was the first Warhammer 40k videogame?

The first official Warhammer 40k videogame was Space Crusade, released by Gremlin Interactive in 1992. It's a direct digital adaptation of Games Workshop and Milton Bradley's classic 1990 Warhammer board game of the same name, which sees several Space Marine squads explore a spaceship, battling Genestealers, Orks, and Chaos Marines controlled by a single game master player.

It's now available to download and play free as abandonware, too - so give it a try if you want (but beware, it can be absolutely lethal for the Space Marine players).

What's the most popular Warhammer 40k game?

We'd say the original Dawn of War is the most popular Warhammer 40k game of all time - in terms of lasting popularity, fan loyalty, and impact on the gaming community.

When it was released in 2004, it introduced huge numbers of PC gamers to the Warhammer 40,000 universe for the first time, and is still revered today as a landmark real-time strategy title that hordes of 40k fans remember fondly - even though its sequels decreased in popularity.

However, Space Marine 2 reached a truly enormous audience, and has the potential to introduce even more people to 40k. Give it time, and we're confident it will overtake its big brother.

How many Warhammer 40k games are there?

A total of 56 officially licensed, full Warhammer 40k games have been released on PC and/or console since 1992. You can find a full list of them above.

That's not counting mobile-only games like Tacticus, or standalone DLC expansions like Dawn of War 1's Soulstorm and Winter Assault.

Including mobile games and standalone expansions, the total goes up to over 70.

What's the best Warhammer 40k mobile game?

The best Warhammer 40k mobile game right now is Warhammer 40,000: Tacticus. We don't include it in our main list, which focuses on PC and console games - but if you're after a slick, playable arena battling game you can play on your phone, Tacticus is worth a go.

Tacticus' basic schtick is roughly equivalent to Plarium's mega-hit Raid: Shadow Legends; you collect characters via gacha mechanics, then form them into teams to fight turn-based grid battles in a variety of modes.

What's the best Warhammer 40k card game?

If the arena battler genre isn't your bag, and you prefer trading card games to gacha hero fights, there are a handful of different free-to-play Warhammer 40,000 digital card games across mobile and PC.

On the whole, we think the best Warhammer 40k card game is Warpforge - the most recent 40k digital card game, which emerged from Early Access in October 2024. There's a wide variety of different faction decks on offer; the gameplay is sophisticated enough to allow many of the classic TCG archetypes you may recognize from Magic: The Gathering and others; and the slick graphics on both PC and mobile deliver a nice, authentic grimdark atmosphere.

If you prefer the Horus Heresy setting, its predecessor from the same developer, Horus Heresy: Legions, is also a good shout.

Their cousin, Warhammer 40k: Combat Cards, offers a slightly different, less traditional TCG style of play, with three lanes of combat and a rock, paper, scissors dynamic for ranged, melee, and psyker characters. Cards also feature proper photos of GW 'Eavy Metal painted minis, rather than original artworks - which could be a plus or a minus depending on your taste.

That's it for our compendium of Warhammer 40,000 videogames - for now. If you've got a favorite or hidden gem to champion from that list (or just want to chat about all things grimdark) come join the Warhammer 40k discussion in our Discord community!
