Warmachine Army-Agnostic Tactics: Interacting with Scenario Elements

November 19th, 2026 via Goonhammer

As the competitive season of Warmachine comes to a close and we all wait with bated breath for the January update, the Warmachine team at Table Top Battles decided we should spend some time talking about tactics and behaviors that aren’t going to change.  This week we will be covering army agnostic tactics, primarily diving deep into placement on and around objectives – something any Warmachine player should be paying attention to regardless of the army they are playing and that won’t be rendered obsolete with an update.

Objectives are a core part of Warmachine, and positioning models to maximize their value while committing the least of your forces is the first step in developing an attrition lead.  Players start learning about objectives as soon as they open up Tales of the Frontlines, and get to know them well when playing Steamroller.  Interacting with objectives is something that skilled players have mastered; if you ever wonder why top players do well with a brand new faction, it is because they already know these positioning tricks.

When it comes to placing models on or around objectives, just placing models close isn’t always enough.  The placement of those models depends entirely on what the goal of those models is.  Here I will outline several different models positionings and why you might want them.

Pre-Engagement

Threatening the Zone

Image Source: Wartable

If you walk away with one thing from this article, this should be it: When two armies are positioning on turn one and two, not engaging yet and you don’t know where to go – position your units to threaten the back of the zones.  Here the cavalry models can charge in and engage any models that are placed in the zone.  If your opponent doesn’t put anything in the zone, the following turn you can commit and score it.  When it doubt, this is where you should position your units.

Blocking Landing Zones

Image Source: Wartable

Colossals and extra large models take up a lot of real estate.  If you have the opportunity to use the objective to block landing zones, it can force an opponent to grossly underutilize their tools.  Look for instances where terrain is near an objective.  Here, a unit of Mechanithralls has positioned so that a Trikhymaerax has very few options on where to land.

Image Source: Wartable

The Trikhymaerax is unable to engage any of the models it wants to charge following this charge path.  In game, you would place a 120mm proxy base on the table, measure the melee range from that proxy, then position your models accordingly.

Even when your opponent can successfully charge, an objective can still greatly diminish the charge's impact.  Here, only one Mechanithrall is engaged by the Trikhymaerax – its 5 initial melee attacks become a total waste.

Image Source: Wartable

When trying to block landing zones while positioning your models, remember that almost every Colossal has Bulldoze and can push models out of the way.  In that case, position models in base to base with the models blocking the landing zone to ensure you are not bulldozed out of the way.

Scoring

Minimal Commitment

Image Source: Wartable

In this scenario you have placed models that control the objective, barely scoring the terrain piece.  You choose this placement when you are confident you will not be pushed out but still want to score the terrain.  The models are placed as far from your opponent as possible, forcing them to commit furthest forward to remove them.  You would NOT want this layout if your opponent had an easy way to move or push your models.  If they have access to those tools, they will simply push one model out of the zone and you will no longer score it.

Scattering

Image Source: Wartable

Here we have another layout that would be used into an army with limited shooting with a unit that has reposition.  This layout forces multiple units to commit to remove the unit since they have spread out to all the different sides of the scenario element.  A single unit can not charge in and engage more than 2 (or sometimes even 1) model.  This forces your opponent to commit way more resources than they would like.  Once again, you would not use this if your opponent has easy access to pushes, as they would simply push a single model out of the zone and ignore the rest of the unit.  Spreading out does not protect you from guns; however, it doesn’t carry any additional risk.

T-Shape

Image Source: Wartable

This layout that looks like an upside down T is designed to reduce causalities when fighting other small based models.  When a unit charges, all other models must be placed within 2” – and, the second part players often forget, those models must be place so they have line of sight to the model that moved.  The layout above makes it very difficult to place models that both have line of sight to the moving model and have melee range to model #1, helping keep the unit alive and, if not scoring the objective, at least contesting it.

Limiting Access

Image Source: Wartable

This option is designed to place models such that enemy models have limited access by using the objective itself to block access to models in the unit.  Here, an opponent will struggle to attack model #1 because the objective blocks a large portion of the available landing zone.  It also prevents your opponent from placing exactly where the objective is, which means placing models within 2” and still being effective is much harder.

Dog Pile

Image Source: Wartable

Dogpiling an objective is the most common way to score it.  It takes the least amount of thought and works into the unknown.  It’s not optimized into certain lists or board states, but it also has no Achilles heel.  Here the models are placed as close to the objective as possible.  This is to prevent your opponent from moving models out of the zone with a push or control effect.  Many pushes or control effects only allow for a 2” or 3” move, so by placing base to base with the objective, it is not possible for the models to be forced out of the zone.

Contesting

In Warmachine you score points on both your turn and on your opponent's, so having a plan to contest zones with the least amount of resources is always important.  The models you contest with will die; but, those models will prevent your opponent from scoring on your turn.

Sacrificial Model while Contesting

Image Source: Wartable

In this scenario, a unit with reposition will send one of its models forward to die, while the rest of the unit will move backwards to safety.  This is best planned out with a proxy base.  Place the proxy base 3” + the models reposition distance from the objective, then move and place the moving model 2” away from the proxy base, a single model on the proxy, and the rest of the unit as far back as possible.  The unit then repositions to safety and to contest at the same time.  If done correctly, you will have a repo” x 2 + 2” (usually 8”) gap in your unit.  Most of the unit will be safe and able to threaten the zone the following turn, while preventing your opponent from scoring the zone on your turn.  You will have committed the fewest models possible.

This tactic is most applicable on a flank where you are at a disadvantage.  A unit that is scoring a zone will be able to charge and kill the contesting model and score, but they can’t pursue your unit since doing so would leave the zone unscored.  My personal favorite unit to do this with is the Shades in Khymaera; I will send one model in to die for 3 turns in a row.  While I may not win the flank, I minimize the amount of points my opponent scored.

Final Thoughts

Objectives are a core part of Warmachine – and interacting with scenario elements is something players should understand.  These tactics have held true over time, and even as steamroller has changed – how to best interact with scenario elements has stayed the same.  If you have any tricks for interacting with scenario elements feel free to throw them in the comments below!
