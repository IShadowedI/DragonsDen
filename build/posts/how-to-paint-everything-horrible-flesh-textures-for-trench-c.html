How to Paint Everything: Horrible Flesh Textures for Trench Crusade

August 21st, 2026 via Goonhammer

The art of Trench Crusade pushes the boundaries of grimdark through the use of textures we usually rely on model sculpts to achieve. Flayed skin, open wounds, rotting flesh, seeping blood - it's all found on just about every page of the Trench Crusade core book, but when it comes to modelling we're usually dependent on the kindness (or warpedness) of a particular sculptor to see them on our models. I don't think that's right - not for something as steeped in Catholic ideas of the mortification of the flesh, at least - for Trench Crusade. So I've been experimenting with ways you can up your grimdark painting game in colours, textures and effects. This has all been done mainly with an eye for painting up Heretic Naval Raiders for the forthcoming Carcass Front starter set, but it works just about everywhere. In a world where sentient plague flies sink their proboscis deep into the brains of saints, and living flesh is warped into horrible umbilici that pipe fevered dreams of gluttony and lust into the souls of the faithful, why the hell are we relying on boring old paint to do the job?

This is the last technique talked about in this article, but it's gone well enough I'm going to show it here. Horrible Flesh Textures

Before we dive in, there is - of course - another content warning. Just like last time, and the time before that [Editor's Note: This is your warning that this is the toe tank link], the idea here is to create something that looks disgusting. The highlight of this process has been that several of the photos were flagged on Discord as sensitive content - what an honour for a grimdark painter - but Norman says it's chill, so here we go!

Rotting Flesh

There’s a lot of contexts in trench crusade where you want rotting skin in varying amounts. Terrain, trench pilgrims, any and all demon factions, takwin monstrosities, the list goes on, and that’s not even mentioning the entire rotting flesh faction. So, something that looks altogether more putrid than a green wash over light tones is much needed. Multi layered rotting flesh is time consuming but it looks fantastic - but isn’t quite as slow as you might think.

The idea is to add multiple layers of very thin paint in irregular shapes, building up a blotchy, multicoloured set of colours that mimic rotting flesh really well by selling the illusion of depth and rot acting on different layers of organ and skin. Between each layer, add some form of protection for the previous one, and repeat this until you’re satisfied.

There are two ways you can do the technique presented here but both of them end up the same way. The model example uses both - on (stage) left, acrylic washes, on (stage) right, isopropyl alcohol. I don’t recommend the iso method - it’s poisonous, headachey and a solution in search of a problem. The idea with using iso is that it can reduce the surface tension of paint allowing it to bloom across a low-friction surface. The exact same effect (science!) is achievable by putting a drop of hand soap in your paint water, so do that instead.

Step by Step Rotting Flesh

Prepare your model by painting the skin areas in two very light, similar tones. I used dark arts altar ivory over an off white primer. Lighter the better here, and don’t worry about using a skin tone.

Our big ogre with the first blotchy colours. Horrible Flesh Textures

Next, add a thin blotch of watery paint. Shades, Inks, Contrast all work well. I used the following colours in rotation across the remainder of the process:

  • Citadel Druchii Violet
  • Citadel Ultramar Blue Contrast
  • Citadel Snakebite Leather Contrast
  • A Citadel Purple Shade/Wash whose label has long since worn off
  • Dark Arts Brown Ink
  • Army Painter Dry Blood
  • Army Painter True Blood

The yellow is a little different as, after many years of trying, I've found that all yellows sold by everyone aren't quite right for me. Instead, it's a teaspoon of tumeric mixed into Army Painter Stabiliser (acrylic medium). The nice thing about this is that it goes on very very yellow, then fades with exposure to sunlight, giving you auto-shadow-and-highlight, as well as being easy to remove and paint over. The whole model reeks of tumeric after, too, which might be a positive or negative thing depending on how you feel.

Before that paint dries (count to ten seconds), use a fine brush with soapy water (or iso) on it to agitate the blotch roughly. Aim for an irregular fringed shape. Some of the paint will have dried to a fine crusty line, while the rest will bloom across the skin in its own way. Don’t try to control this, just let it go! Do this in a couple of places across the model but don’t overdo it - leave plenty of base tone showing.

First colour Layer - Horrible Flesh Textures

Next, add a layer of protection that will (slightly) repel paint - brush on, airbrush or rattle can gloss and satin varnish works, if you’re using ISO use superglue, or a very very thin layer of PVA. If using PVA give it at least a couple of hours drying time.

After a few coats. Horrible Flesh Textures

 

Then repeat the paint/bloom process using other colours. Keep the paint watery - past milk texture into squash levels of dilution. Once dry, add another layer of protection.

Really getting there now! Horrible Flesh Textures

You can repeat this infinitely, though three layers will give you a fantastically deep, rich look. Changing the colours from darker to lighter looks good too, but there are no rules here - experiment and see what you come up with!

Transparent Skin

Once you’re happy with the colour pattern you’ve created with your decaying flesh, it’s time to make it look pellucid, creepy and horrifying. This is incredibly simple - the magic of PVA!

A thick layer. Horrible Flesh Textures

Once all the paint is totally and completely dry, add a relatively thick layer (primer layer thickness is fine) of PVA all over the model. Once that’s dry, use a fine brush and trace the occasional blood vessel (dark blood, black or purple works best) over it.

Add another layer of PVA, this time with a very, very small amount of your base light tone added in, and a little water to help it mix. You want the mixture to look like this:

Mixed PVA and Paint Horrible Flesh Textures

And you’ll need a fair bit of it, because you want to do at least two layers of it! This forms a horribly translucent, semi-realistic glaze of both a skinlike texture and colour over your blooming bruises and haemorrhages, and the more layers the better. Once you’re done, don’t throw that mix out - spread it out over a shallow container and leave it for later.

Horrible Flesh Textures

The water added in also means that the drying PVa will pull tighter than usual across the model, which can pull up some delightful textures.  If you want a bit more skin tone over your rot, add another layer rather than more paint, you don’t want to obscure all your hard, disgusting work.

Open Sores and Wounds

The multiple layers of PVA we've added to the skin allows us to create incredible looking wound effects with nothing more than a pin and a few washes. Scrape a pin or modelling knife across (horizontally at first) where you'd like to add a wound.

Dragging some holes into it. Horrible Flesh Textures

Tease the edges of the PVA into the shape you'd like, and firmly press down on the wound once it's in the shape and size you want.

Establishing some ragged shapes. Horrible Flesh Textures

Moving quickly, use a small brush to flick watered down paint - try blood effects, black washes, or deep purples - under the edges of the open wound. The paint should wick up under one or more layers of PVA, creating a subtle bloom and adding depth to the effect.

Horrible Flesh Textures

Whack on another very thin coat of PVA to hold any trailing bits of skin in place, and to stop them catching and peeling further.

Once all that's dry, add a darker tone (brown works, though dried blood is also good), then a lighter one (bright blood, flesh or green). Agitate this mix with a pin, and rake up the lower layers of PVA while you're at it. If you'd like to expose bare muscle, do so in a pattern (vertical or horizontal stripes), while if you want liquified flesh bursting out just mix it up!

Now that's grim dark! Horrible Flesh Textures

You can return to the raised flaps of skin with a little highlight colour (Altar Ivory again in this case) if you wish, but go very easy and light on it as a major part of why this technique looks so grim is that the PVA retains its transparency. There's an immense amount of fun you can have with this technique. Ragged holes look rotted or torn (amazing for tooth and claw damage!), while taking your time to roll the PVA away from the area looks like sloughing skin or - and I haven't tried this yet - you could go really big with it. Ever heard of degloving?

You can see a first try at degloving on the belly wound. Horrible Flesh Textures

Flayed or Peeling Skin

One of the most metal pieces of devotional art in the world is in St Barts-the-Great Church in central London. St Bartholomew, the flayed saint, was often depicted in medieval art holding his own skin and flensing knife, which is generally very cool looking, and his statue in St Bart’s the lesser is a modern phenomenal rendition of it:

Whatever you think about Hirst, this absolutely rocks. Via Wikipedia

I wanted to do so thing similar for Trench pilgrims, but unfortunately plastic sculpted flayed skin always universally looks shite. It’s too think (the skin on flayed ones, to scale, would be a good couple of inches thick), it’s too plasticky and it’s usually too rigid. This is trench crusade so we want better (worse?) for our models.

Making draped and flayed skin is fairly easy, and luckily it’s a byproduct of the PVA deep skin process. Remember when I said don’t throw out that PVA and paint mix? If you’ve left it to dry naturally it should look something like this:

Dried PVA flesh. Horrible Flesh Textures

Pick a hole in that and drop a little water on the hole. It’ll run underneath the pva - once you’ve done that leave it again until it’s dry. Add a skin tone layer to the top - I’ve sponged on a very light mix of dark arts masque flesh and altar ivory and, if so inclined, freehand on some tattoo shapes and/or scars. This is going to be your skin to drape artfully wherever it’s needed.

When dry to the touch, use a sharp modelling knife and cut lines into the skin surface. This allows you to pull away strips rather than patches. You can cut around a whole shape or just two/three sides. The more you cut the more even the final result will be. Pull the pieces out with tweezers very carefully.

You can see the pieces end up very fine, which is ideal! Horrible Flesh Textures

There will be a lot of stretch and movement in each piece of skin, so you can drape, pull, roughen and essentially do whatever you like with them! They work great on models and slightly less well on banners and decoration. With two points of contact between model and skin, the pieces won’t curl up too much - if you’re going for single point of contact they won’t stay looking as they do for long. Attach them with pva and paint the interior side with your choice of horrible blood, gore, slime and rot effects. Paint on both sides helps rigidity, strength and longevity, as well as looking awesome.

Use at least two points of contact and the PVA skin will drape beautifully. Horrible Flesh Textures

While not as fragile as they look due to elasticity, They will remain much easier to damage than plastic, so handle with care.

Now That's Grim Dark!

There's a lot more to go here with the Drowned Metachrist, but here he is so far! Horrible Flesh Textures

Using these techniques is incredibly simple, and adds a whole lot of horrifying, trench crusade appropriate texture. This isn't the kind of game where highlights, shadows and base coats do the job. If you're still here, get on it, get the PVA out, avoid the Isopropyl alcohol and bloody well go for it! Next time we'll be looking at hair, fur, scourgemarks, lice, flies and all the other horrible stuff you need to do trench crusade the way it could (and perhaps shouldn't!) be done.
