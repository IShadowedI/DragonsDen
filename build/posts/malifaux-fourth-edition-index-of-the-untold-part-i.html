Malifaux Fourth Edition: Index of the Untold, Part I

August 10th, 2026 via Goonhammer

The first expansion book of Fourth Edition is here! Previously, Wyrd has followed a tried-and-tested formula for expansion books: a new Keyword for each faction, plus some new models for existing Keywords, and maybe a cycle of Versatiles. This time, though, we're doing something different. Index of the Untold does not have a single new model's rules in it. Or, from another perspective, it has billions of new models' rules in it.

That's because, for the first time since Second Edition, we've got an honest-to-Burning Man campaign book!

By now, seasoned wargamers are familiar with this formula. Whether you're talking about dedicated campaign-structured games like Necromunda and Trench Crusade, or well-developed campaign modes like Crusade and Path to Glory, the basic outline is well-trodden. Malifaux is in some ways uniquely ill-suited to this type of game mode, given that all of the Masters are specific named characters (and so are about half of their followers). So Wyrd has really swung for the fences here and created something incredibly cool that brings all the customization and narrative of a long-running campaign to the world of Malifaux.  I'm not normally a big narrative player and I am hugely excited to play a Malifaux campaign, so let's shuffle up our Fate decks and see what we've been dealt here!

Setting the Table

The book opens by describing the structure of a campaign, which will have seasoned narrative players nodding along.  A campaign is played in a series of games over several weeks. After each game, each player has an Aftermath step, whereupon you suffer lasting injuries, buy new equipment, add models to your roster (known as your Arsenal), and so on.  Every campaign has a meta-currency; Malifaux's is Scrip, which you use for buying sweet new Equipment and hiring on new fighters.

There are a few things that I'll mention here in passing but that you could probably already guess. You have a Campaign Rating, which is an overall measure of crew strength (as measured by injuries, equipment, and so on); the player with the lower Campaign Rating gets bonus Soulstones as a handicap in games. Game size is not fixed, but is capped at 6 stones higher than the total of the smallest roster involved in the game (six being, conveniently, the maximum number of starting Soulstones).  Wyrd reminds us that Malifaux really should not be played above 50 Soulstones, which is what it's balanced for, but hey, it's your campaign. Oh, and you can't ever gain abilities that affect hiring (for example, Wrath's Jam Session ability that lets him hire Crossroads models).  What you've picked is what you've got.

Wyrd also notes that this mode really only works if everyone is on the same page and is not rigorously balanced for matched play; basically, be cool. You are going to, hopefully, play these people again and again, so try not to twist the system until it snaps.  This is Rule 1 for pretty much any kind of miniature wargame campaign mode, so kudos to Wyrd for spelling it out.

"wait! i have one more book to return!" Credit: Wyrd Games

Gathering Your Forces

Starting at the beginning, you pick a Faction and two Keywords. At least one of your Keywords must be from the chosen Faction, but the other one can be from anywhere. The chosen Faction dictates your out-of-keyword and Versatile hiring pool, but that's it. Your two Keywords are combined, and models from either Keyword are treated as having both Keywords. This opens up some fairly insane combos, to begin with; I like the idea of hiring Dr. Meredith Stanley opposite pretty much anyone who repeatedly summons Peons (how about Jebediah?) but I bet you can think of some fun tricks on your own.  Remember Rule 1!

Once you've got your keywords, you have 25 Soulstones to hire your starting Arsenal. You can hire any model in your Keywords that has a Soulstone cost (so no hiring Witchling Thralls, for example). But you can't hire a Master or a Totem, and you don't get a Crew Card.

Why not?  You're building them!

That's the core of the campaign system: custom Masters and Crew Cards. Masters start off relatively weak and Totemless, but gain Advancements over the course of the campaign, including the ability to add on Totems and add abilities to their Crew Card.  By the end of the campaign you'll be playing custom Masters more broken than anything Reddit could come up with!

To begin with, you pick one of five Master archetypes, each of which sets your Master's Df, Wp, Sp, and Health, and decides its starting Actions and Abilities. Rather than fully custom Attacks and Abilities, your Master gets to borrow from other models in your Keyword pair (regardless of whether you've hired that exact model into your Arsenal). For example, a Heavy Hitter master gets to pick one Attack action from an ally model of cost 10 or less, plus one of its triggers, and one Tactical action from an ally of cost 5 or less. A Schemer, by contrast, gets just a single Attack action from an ally of cost 5 or less, but two different Tacticals from allies of cost 8 or less, and a front-of-card ability from an ally of cost 8 or less.  A Lucky Upstart even gets a piece of Equipment for free, which you can never lose!

You can pick your Master's base size (30-50mm) and Size (1-4), with no cost or penalty for going in either direction.  This is a bit odd to me, since both size and base size are very mechanically relevant in Malifaux, but I think this is an area where Wyrd expects you to be governed by Rule 1. You can choose traits like Construct or Living the same way (and the same comment applies). You also choose Bruiser or Strategist as an archetype, which affects only how your Master advances between games.  We will return to this later.

Next, you get a starting Crew Card, all of which are decidedly meh (by design - they'll get better!). There are 12 starting Crew Cards, each of which has exactly one ability. Sometimes this is an Action, sometimes it's a "when this model activates" or "when this model kills an enemy"-type ability, but in each case it costs a Soulstone: either to take the action or trigger the ability. These are designed to make any Keyword functional: one lets you attach Keyword-locked upgrades like Ophelia's guns, while another lets you create special Keyword Markers like Tide Markers and another lets you gain special Keyword Tokens like Voyage Tokens.  None of them is particularly efficient, but that's fine.  For now.

The Master archetypes mostly seem balanced to me, although it heavily depends on your Keyword. Picking Apex Amalgam encourages you to make a huge Heavy Hitter and borrow the Malisaurus's jaws or the Desolation Engine's, uh, horribly flailing thing, but Family crews might be fine picking a Generalist and borrowing Nino Ortega's sniper rifle.

Let's Get This Show on the Road

Once you've got your baby Master, your 25 stones of flunkies, and your starting Crew Card, that's it!  You're ready to play!

For the most part, setting up and playing campaign games is pretty easy; you just follow the standard rules for picking a Strategy, Deployment type, and Schemes. There's one modification to reflect the ongoing nature of the campaign: a Strategic Withdrawal. At the start of any turn, you can willingly bottle out - there's no check to see if it "succeeds" and it never happens unless you declare it, so it's a fully voluntary choice.  If you do so on turns 1 or 2, you skip your entire postgame Aftermath step except rolling for injuries.  Otherwise it's just a normal loss; your opponent can spend the rest of the game (probably) maxing out their score, and if you somehow still win the game even though you fled, your score is automatically set to one lower than your opponent.  No rewards for cowardice!

Once the game is done, either normally or through Strategic Withdrawal, you move into the postgame step.  And that's where the magic happens.

yessss... that's right... give me all your scrip... Credit: Wyrd Games

Last Laugh in the Aftermath

The "Aftermath" is where the bulk of the campaign rules are located, and it fittingly takes up the majority of the campaign section of the book.  (There is another section of the book, though, as we will see!).

An Aftermath step is broken into six phases, and we'll tackle them one at a time.  They are:

  • Draw Aftermath Hand
  • Payday
  • Barter
  • Advance Leader
  • Back-Alley Doctor
  • Determine Injuries

A note as we proceed: The term for "permanently removed from your roster" is Annihilated, which can happen to Injuries, Equipment, and even models, most notably when they acquire their third Injury. The first time your Master is annihilated, they avoid fate and cross off their third injury (if they have one); the second time, they're done, and so is their crew.  You are invited to start fresh.

Draw Aftermath Hand

Simple and straightforward.  This is Malifaux - we're not sending you into the wilds of Random Number Generation without a few cheats in your pocket.  You shuffle up your deck and deal yourself a hand of variable size: one card for completing a game without Withdrawing, and then one card for each Scheme you completed, to a maximum of 3.  Just scoring one point from a Scheme counts as completing it, but you can't get four cards this way by going 1-1-2-2, so your endgame hand caps at 4.  These cards can be cheated for any postgame flip: barter, advancement, back-alley doctor or injury flips.  You can't empower, mind you: these flips are never at a +twist.

You also have to do all postgame flips one at a time, fully resolving each before you move on to the next.  There's an element of tension there: you may want to save your cards if your flip isn't that bad, but if it turns out your later flips are better, you can't go back.  You have to make decisions with the information you have.

Once you draw your hand, you do not shuffle again.  What you've got is what you've got, and it's got to last you to the end.

Payday

Another simple one.  You earn one one Scrip postgame for every 3 points you earned, rounding up (so 4 points = 2 Scrip).  You get one more if you won, and one more, win or lose, if you had the lower Campaign Rating.  Maxing out your score and winning with a lower Campaign Rating, then, is worth 6 Scrip.

Scrip persists week to week, so don't feel compelled to spend it all at once!  One final note: adding new models to your Arsenal is done at the start of a week, before you play your first game, not as part of the Aftermath. Hiring a model into the Arsenal simply requires you to spend its Soulstone cost in Scrip, plus 1 if it's an out-of-keyword, non-Versatile model from your declared Faction.  You must hire at least one new model each week (barring your first week - the step is skipped for that week), but the first model you hire each week gets a steep 5-Scrip discount.

Barter

Now we're talking.  For everyone but your Master, this is where advances come from. (Your Master can barter, too, but they also get a special advance table). Barter is where you spend hard-earned Scrip on Equipment.

Bartering opens with a Barter flip - a single card off the deck, which tells you what's in stock this week.  The Equipment table is massive, and consists of four "always available" entries plus six entries per card value, 1-13.  These are further separated by suit into "ram or mask" and "tome or crow," so if you flip, say, the Four of Tomes, you can buy the Fool's Gold, Useless Generator and Gatling Gun. And remember you can cheat this!

That means you typically have seven options available to you each time you flip, and you can buy as many as you have Scrip for.  None of the items are unique, with one qualified exception.

Equipment is a dizzying array of buffs, impossible to summarize.  Broadly, you can separate it into three categories: items that give you a new action, items that give you a new passive ability, and consumables.  Consumables can be Annihilated to have a one-time effect; for example, the always-available Lucky Gremlin's Foot lets you no-sell a single Injury for the low cost of one Scrip.

Many of the actions and abilities are familiar - I can see Juggernaut (from the Guardian's Shield), Don't Turn Your Back (from the Trickster's Mask), and Lure (from the False Face).  But the majority of them are brand new, and some are extremely cool.  Special shout-outs go to the Quick-Draw Holster, which lets you pitch a card to treat any action as a Signature; the Dead Man's Switch (which gives you a 3" pulse, 3 irreducible damage Demise); and the Strange Portal, which can be Annihilated when a model activates to let you flip a card (which cannot be cheated) and resolve an effect based on the result.  Flip a crow and your model "dies" (without flipping for a postgame injury), only to appear, in exactly the state he or she is in now, on turn 1 of the next game you play.  Flip a joker? Your opponent summons an exact doppelganger of your model (presumably with a natty goatee), and you get 1 VP if you take out the imposter during the game.

Rosie with Arcane Sense and Quick Draw Holster enables some extremely funny plays. Credit: Wyrd Games

If you flip the Black Joker, you simply don't get anything (aside from the Always Available stuff).  But flipping the Red Joker lets you flip on the special Those Who Thirst table, a table of rare and powerful relic weapons. Note that only flipping the Red gives you access to this table; cheating it merely gives you a 13 of the suit of your choice.,  You can only have one of Those Who Thirst, so you flip the red again later in the campaign you "ignore this result"; personally I would just treat a second flip on the table as a cheated Red (a 13 of the player's choice), but I'm not your dad and I can't tell you how to live your life.

Those Who Thirst are pretty cool, though.  The Book of the Dead is a long-range, damage 3 magical attack that summons a Mindless Zombie onto the target, plus one for each raise you get. The Medusa, meanwhile, does just one point of damage but Slows, Stuns and Entrances the target.  All of these relic weapons come with a crow trigger, Laid to Rest, that causes anyone taken out by them to make two injury flips and apply both results - this is brutal, but it's sufficiently rare that I don't mind seeing it.  There's even a special result on the Those Who Thirst table for flipping "either joker," by which I guess Wyrd means the Black Joker, since you have to have flipped the Red to even be here.  Anyways, the Red-into-Black result is Omen's Mark, an equipment you must attach to someone every game and which causes its bearer to take an additional damage from all sources.  The kicker is that when the model is killed, the model that killed it takes up the Omen's Mark, and your crew loses it - and this is the only way to get rid of it, as it cannot be discarded or annihilated.

Once purchased, Equipment sits in your Arsenal.  At the start of any game, a piece of Equipment can be attached to any of your models, and it's never locked to that model - as long as you have the thing, you can pass it around between games as much as you want. Equipment can be discarded in various ways, but unless it's Annihilated, it always returns to your Arsenal to be used again.

I love the Barter table.  It's colossal and a ton of love and thought has clearly gone into making these entries exciting even for veteran players.  I will say that I do not love that cheating the Red does not give you access to the relic table; this is a persistent theme through the postgame steps, and I understand the purpose is to make the "Red Joker table" results rarer and more exciting, but in practice it means that drawing the Red in your postgame hand kind of sucks, as it locks you out of getting these cool results.  At minimum, I'd probably want to play with a house rule that flipping or cheating the Red Joker results in it being immediately shuffled back into the deck, if only so the "Red into Joker" results will happen more than once a century.

Advance Leader

Once you have done all your shopping, it's time for your Master to advance!  Remember when you picked Bruiser or Strategist?  Now is the time for it to pay off - after each game you get experience points, as follows:

  • One for showing up, which is half the battle;
  • One if you are a Bruiser and killed one or more non-Peon models during a game;
  • One if you are a Strategist and resolved at least one Interact within 6" of the enemy Deployment Zone; and
  • One if you lost the game.  Failure is the best teacher!

That means the maximum you can get is 3 points after a game, although there is Equipment that grants experience points under certain conditions.

you're gonna need all the experience points you can get, buddy. Credit: Wyrd Games

There's a Master advancement track, which consists of 39 sequential boxes; 24 are blank, and 15 have a number from 1-4 in them.  When you get experience points, you just start markin' boxes, and when you mark a box with a number in it, congrats!  It's advancing time!

When you get an advance, you simply flip on the appropriate table.  There are four tables, although you have some flexibility here: you can flip on a lower table than the number in the box you just checked.  I will note that the first three boxes on the track are marked 1, 1, 2, so it doesn't take long for Masters to start differentiating themselves, although advances get more sparse with time.

The advance tables come in four categories, although they have some elements in common.  They don't have Suits like the Barter table; instead, whatever number you flip, you can pick any entry of that number or lower.  Given that the tables have 2-5 entries per number, that can give you a pretty staggering range of choices.  There are two tables in each tier 1-3; Tier 4 is special, and we'll get to that one. When you flip, you can pick either table in a given tier to flip in.

Tier 1

Tier 1 consists of Attack and Tactical action modifications, which add triggers to your existing actions, increase your Skl, or make an action Signature. You can apply these triggers to an action you gained from Equipment, although doing so means your Master must be the one to use that equipment in every game from now on (no sharing!) and if you lose the equipment for whatever reason, the advance goes with it.  In addition, when adding a trigger to action with 2 or more triggers already, you must pay 2 scrip for the privilege.

The new Action triggers are mostly pre-existing, although there are some fun new ones, like Wild Toss (for one Soulstone, place the target within 5" and deal a point of damage to it). The Joker triggers are neat as well: for the Attack actions, one is just Good for a Laugh, but for a Soulstone, but the other one can be declared when killing the enemy Leader and gives your Master +1 experience points after the game.  Good show!  There are also Joker Tactical action triggers, one of which lets you ditch your Scheme and pick a new one without telling your opponent what you ditched.

Tier 2

This Tier of advances gives you new abilities, either Actions or front-of-card passives.  The Actions are, as before, not new, but they're really good ones: I see Ulix's Hellfire Shot, Zipp's Zipp Zapper, and old favorites like Steamroller, Leap and Secret Passage.

The Abilities, meanwhile, are mostly workhorses like Butterfly Jump, Serene Countenance, Flight, and Trample, but there are some spicy ones here: I particularly like Entourage and Fortify the Spirit.  The "always available" abilities are new too: they all cost a Soulstone to use, but include a free 4" teleport at start of activation and a Soulstone-powered defensive ability that reduces damage by 2 to a minimum of 0.

Flipping a Joker here lets you pick any action/ability from a model in either of your Keywords with a cost of up to 10, just like at the start of the campaign, which is both wild and hilarious.

Tier 3

This tier is a bit different from the others. For one thing, you don't get to pick any result equal to or below your flip: what you flip is what you get. For another, the second table doesn't even let you flip at all.

The first table is Totem Advancements. The name is slightly misleading: the advancement is getting a Totem at all.  There are, of course, 15 options, none of which map directly onto any existing Totem; each one comes with a set of stats (Df, Wp, Sp, Health), and some abilities, Attack actions and/or Tactical actions. Once you earn the Totem you get it fully formed, and there's no point flipping on the table again unless your Totem got Annihilated or you really don't like the one you got.  You pick your Totem's base size, Sz, and traits (undead, living, etc.), up to two traits, at time of hiring. From then on, when your Master would earn an Advance, you may choose to Advance your totem instead.

I have to say I like some of these a lot. I am a special fan of the Willing Vessel, a fat sack of HP with the Emissaries' Tempting Fate ability, plus an Attack and Tactical Action that both heal the Vessel.  There is a special place in my heart, though, for the Sniveling Coward, the Black Joker totem; he sucks at everything, but if he damages an enemy model and then survives to the end of the game, you can permanently replace him with any non-Joker totem, keeping any Advances he's earned.

The Red Joker totem is a bit disappointing: he just gains an Action on the printed stat card of a Master that shares a keyword with yours (i.e. a real Master, from Malifaux), but without any triggers, and with no other abilities, and also you can't copy actions that list a model by name or transform the user. The idea of copying the .950 Nitro Express is very funny, but realistically, the best use for this is to just copy a summoning ability.

sadly, this one doesn't work because her summon names Seishin. Credit: Wyrd Games

The second table is Summoning Advancements.  You can only ever pick this once, and you don't flip - you just choose an option from a table.  These advancements give you the ability to summon a model, and since most keywords don't have models with Summon TNs, the abilities on this table work a little differently than the summons you're used to.  There are 7, each of which gives you the ability to summon a model of your Keyword pair - typically of cost 5 or less, but one ability is locked to cost 3 or less and another only summons peons.  There are a variety of different conditions here: remove an enemy marker, remove a friendly marker near an enemy, remove a friendly token from an enemy model, etc.

I understand the strict limits on Summons: it really breaks the game to have good repeatable summons, since you don't care if they die but your opponent really does care if they lose models.  Summoning models whose entire purpose is to fight and die can inflict serious damage on your opponent's crew even if you lose the game. There are enough safety valves on the Summon Advancements that they're probably fine, but it does just highlight how the Mini-Master totem can break the mode: if you're in a Keyword pair that includes at least one high-powered summoner (DUA, say, or Witch Hunter), you can just focus on breaking your opponents' kneecaps with summons and catching up later in the campaign season.

Tier 4

Tier 4 is Crew Card upgrades, and there's no table here at all.  Instead, when you choose this result, you simply pick ONE effect printed on any crew card associated with your Keywords (typically 4 crew cards total - one for each title of each Master - although some weirdos like Kastore have more), and add it to your crew card.  There are some rules:

  • No power bars and no rules that swap the crew card for another one.
  • One "effect" is one ability, action, or trigger.  That's it.  And you can only pick a trigger if that trigger is listed individually on the card (the example in the book is Crack Skulls on the Excessive Violence crew card).
  • Actions come with their associated triggers.

You can also choose one of the starting campaign crew card rules to add to your crew card, though I think you will rarely want to do this.

I have to say this is a little disappointing.  I agree that it was important to unlock base-game crew card rules, since so many of them are integral to how their Keywords function, but it would have been really nice to see a little table of custom crew card effects. Some crew cards also have two-part rules where each individual part does nothing - think Audit Reality/Contaminated Theories from Footnotes of Madness, or Astronomical Observations/The Stars Shift from Study the Stars.

Back-Alley Doctor

After you've spent those precious experience points, it's time to visit the ol' sawbones!  Note that this comes before the Determine Injuries step, so you'll have to play at least one game with your boo-boo before you can have the nice man kiss it away.

Actually, a kiss might have better medicinal value than what ol' doc here is working with. You spend 1 scrip to try to heal an injury and then flip a card. Naturally, the Black Joker results in you getting another injury for your pains (which will be agonizing), but a 1-8 does nothing, and a 9 just swaps your injury for another, potentially worse one. On a 10 or 11 you heal your injury but end up Undead ("That still counts as healed") or a Construct ("Took some spare parts, but we got there"), while a 12 or 13 is an unqualified success.  Flipping (not cheating) the Red Joker gets you a flip on the Lucky Miss table, which we'll see in a moment when we get to the Injuries section (but it's your stock "actually, this injury was a good thing, and this isn't blood in my mouth but victory wine" table).

Not too much to say here; I like that getting rid of injuries isn't easy, but in practice you'll just save your high cards and cheat the result you need here.  If a model is really messed up, though, you might as well take a swing at healing them.  What have you got to lose (besides your scrip)?

Injuries

Ahhh, yes.  Here we go.  Everyone's favorite part of campaign play: now you figure out what happened when your favorite character was shot, stabbed, set on fire, and pushed off the roof.

Peons can't be injured, but everyone else who died during a game has to flip. This table follows the same "masks/rams, crows/tomes" setup as the Equipment table, but has just one result per card.  If you double up, you just skip getting an injury, so... lucky you!  Can't lose your right foot twice.

As noted, once you get three injuries, you're toast!

no, that's - nevermind. Credit: Wyrd Games

In a reversal of fortune, 1s and 2s here are good - you don't get injured - whereas 13s mean instant death.  Everything else is varying degrees of bad.  Models with a Dulled Edge suffer -1 Df, while Senseless models must discard a card every time they activate or gain Slow, and models with Brittle Bones give everyone (mostly enemies, but sometimes friendlies!) a +twist on all attack flips against them.

There are a few very cool injuries, I have to say. Blood Debt lets you spend a Soulstone while in the opposing deployment zone to Annihilate the injury permanently - but if you don't do that during your game, your opponent can Annihilate it at the end to give themselves an extra VP!  The Red Joker lets you flip on the Lucky Miss table, of which more in a second, while the Black makes you a Traitor - the model is Annihilated from your roster (unless it's a master or totem, who can't turn stag), and your opponent adds it to their Arsenal with all of its injuries and equipment intact!

The Lucky Miss table is, as you might expect, all buffs. They're really good ones, too: Secret Directive gives you a bonus VP if that model ends the game within 6" of the enemy deployment zone, while The Hunter lets you infuse a Soulstone every time you kill an enemy. Flipping the black on this table gives you a Doppelganger: you get a copy of whatever model it was added to your Arsenal, with all injuries and equipment intact.  Yes, this can copy your Master.  Yes, then you get two Masters.  Yes, the second copy is free.

It's fine.  This is gonna come up once in every 50 campaigns and it's going to be hilarious when it does.

On the Warpath

That's it!  That's all of the campaign rules!  Go forth and conquer!

Wait, you want to know who wins?  Everyone wins because you had fun with your friends!

Ok, fine.  There are Competitive Campaign rules, and a bunch of other optional twists and modifiers.  There's also an entirely new way to play Malifaux in this book.  But that'll have to wait until Part II of this review, which is already almost 5000 words long.  Until next time, Mali-folks!
