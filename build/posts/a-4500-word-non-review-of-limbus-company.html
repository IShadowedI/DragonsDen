A 4500 Word Non-Review of Limbus Company

September 17th, 2026 via Goonhammer

I would say that this review is one of the hardest I’ve ever had to write, but I’m not even sure that I’d quantify this as a review, frankly. If you want to know if I recommend Limbus Company, the answer is unequivocally yes that you should give it a try. But the reasoning behind why it is so hard to talk about and why it feels a little vulnerable to espouse my love for the game is that the game means many things to me, but those things are not likely going to match up with someone else’s expectations. Something I’ve learned in my time as a writer, educator, and simply a fan of things is that there is a certain barrier that will exist between the things you enjoy and the things other people enjoy, and that no matter how ardently you love something, it can be nearly impossible to convince others that they should love it too. And when someone loves something a lot, that disconnect or even rejection can hurt, because there’s a level of vulnerability to making the fact that you love something so much public and having it discarded by someone else, even if they don’t mean to be mean spirited; they simply just don’t love that thing as you do.

So when I say that this review, if we’re going to all still pretend to call it that, is 3 years in the making, it isn’t entirely wrong. It is even a bit longer than that, because Limbus Company isn’t what made me fall in love with developer Project Moon’s creativity and narrative strength, but it is the game of theirs I love the most, which involves simply the passage of time and the continued development of the game, whereas Lobotomy Corporation and Library of Ruina are static objects; I can and do love them and play them, but Limbus Company’s bus is ever moving forward, and every time it does, I love it more, to the point that I simply hope I get to see where and how it all ends. On the eve of the newest Canto, Canto X: PUNCTUM, I decided it was finally time to explain why I love this game; whether you decide to try it after you read this or not, I’ll know I’ve at least tried to finally put into words what makes it so special to me.

Also, because I think the narrative is so important to experience without spoilers, this not review contains as little spoiler as possible to the point of being almost obtuse about a game with 3+ years of character development to its name, so dear reader, please don't fear that you'll have the game ruined for you by going through this article to the end.

The Divine Comedy of Struggle

Dante's very bad no good first day.

Limbus Company is a game that has made me cry more times than any other game I have ever played, and it will likely continue doing so. I have cried out of frustration at character’s stories and struggles, out of laughter at some of the funniest situations and dialogue I’ve encountered, out of emotional resonance when the narrative cuts deeper than I was expecting, and even in celebration at overcoming something I had been struggling to overcome in the game itself. I will say that I do not think Limbus Company is the most well-written or highest quality writing of all time, but it does something that a lot of “higher” writing often fails at: it hits the drive off the green every time, and continues to get better at it every time it has to tee up.

In Limbus Company, players take on the role (sort of) of Dante, a non-gendered person who wakes up after something that has taken all of their memories of their life up until that point, and also replaced their head with that of a giant, seemingly flaming, clock. Before Dante can truly grasp what is even going on or how they ended up in this situation, they are thrown into conflict and meet the 12 main cast and playable characters in the game, known as the Sinners. Shockingly, the Sinners are actually extremely weak, dying almost immediately, revealing Dante’s seemingly only real ability: by rewinding the Clock that is now their head, the Sinners can be brought back to life endlessly, at great pain to Dante every time it happens.

Already somewhat off the rails, players are then introduced to the only other 2 constant characters they’ll have on their journey: Vergilius, the group’s surly guide who is a Color Fixer (more on that later) and Charon, a girl who speaks in a monotone and strange way that drives Mephistopheles, the bus that houses your team and also runs off of a fuel extracted from spinal cords and brains. Still with me? Great! Because it only gets weirder from there. Dante and the Sinners are tasked with gathering items known as Golden Boughs, which when fully collected for their employer, Limbus Company, the Sinners, Vergilius, and Dante, supposedly, will get the answers and promises made to them in their ominous contracts.

The Sinners, from left to right: Rodya, Yi Sang, Heathcliff, Ishmael, Sinclair, Gregor, Don Quixote, Ryoshu, Faust, Hong Lu, Outis, Meursault

This is easier said than done, because every fight in the game represents an actual conflict that the Sinners are dealing with, often at great physical and mortal injury to themselves, and continued suffering for their Manager, Dante, who continually rewinds the clock to revive them. Without going into any other real story spoilers or narrative, each Canto is specifically tied to a Sinner, and each Sinner is based off of and themed around the work of literature in which their namesake is the protagonist of: Gregor of the Metamorphosis, Rodya (AKA Rodion) of Crime and Punishment, Faust of the same named title, Ishmael of Moby Dick, Sinclair from Demian: The Story of a Boyhood, Don Quixote from the same titled novel, Yi Sang of Korean author Kim Hae-Gyeong’s The Wings, Meursault of The Stranger, Outis from the Odyssey, Heathcliff from Wuthering Heights, Hong Lu from Dream of the Red Chamber, and Ryoshu who is based on Hell Screen. That leaves the overarching narrative to Dante, of The Divine Comedy, which is also where the various game chapters take their Canto and Intervallo theming from.

Having played their other games, I knew Project Moon’s head writer and CEO Kim Ji-hoon was fairly well read, as both games made fairly heavy use of literary tropes and references from other works, but Limbus takes the concept further by using each specific novel as the backbone of character arcs; that alone attracted me, but beyond that, I’ve since been amazed at not only how faithful and well understood each work of fiction has been through its transformation to a Canto, but also the new ways and directions Project Moon takes the stories. In the easiest way possible to explain it, knowing the stories makes them more engaging as the twists or turns you expect are played out in new ways, not even really “subversively”, which was the thing I also didn’t expect; in a media literacy age where it seems like people are increasingly unable to engage with art but also expect all art to be filled with “subversion” or “meta”, Limbus Company just asks: What if Ishmael was a redheaded woman (and also a lot of other things I won’t spoil)?

Because the literary references and artistic engagement don’t end solely with the main cast, and range from highbrow to lowbrow, with everything from Gordon Ramsay’s Hell’s Kitchen to Antoine de Saint-Exupery’s The Little Prince. And then there is just the literature of Project Moon itself, whose world-building is perhaps the most unique aspect of the entire package. But to talk about that, we need to talk about The City.

Do You Love the City You Live In?

At least you don't live here.

All of Project Moon’s games take place in the dystopian nightmare location known as The City, a cyberpunk amalgamation of Districts, all divided up by various letters, from A-Z. Each District is controlled by a Corporation that usually bears the name of the District it represents, such as T Corp, G Corp, L Corp, and so on. Each of these nightmare capitalist gone wild fever dreams generally involve some form of thematic element linked to the “power” that particular District controls; T Corp, for example, controls Time, literally having inhabitants divided into the poor who can only afford “four hour days”, and the wealthy, whose days can last for 40+ hours. W Corp controls “Warp Trains”, technology that allows travelers to cross seemingly huge distances in the blink of an eye; each district has a unique Cyberpunk flavor, all of them are absolute nightmares, and their creativity and dark whimsy drive what makes Limbus Company work so well. The City is one of the most realized fictional locations I’ve ever had the joy of playing around with, and the more you know about it and the source material it references, the more enjoyable it becomes to interact with. It also means that things that may not be understood initially can and often do become understood later, written in a way that make it inviting for players to invest their time in actually learning about the world Project Moon has created.

And then there are the denizens of the City; there's the mysterious Head, who oversees the entire thing, organizations like The Fingers, Wings, and others who enforce various rules and punish Taboo breakers, and the aforementioned Colors. Colors are people so powerful they've been titled with a "color" to denote their power, such as Red Mist, Black Silence, etc., and who are often Fixers, essentially a mercenary that will happily lend their abilities for the right price. These more human concepts are juxtaposed with Abnormalities, inhuman beings that run the gamut from a living representation of the Brazen Bull to a vending machine that might get you stuck on a fishing boat, "living" abstractions born from the human mind that can only be suppressed. All of this is to say that there are many, many facets to the Project Moon universe, all of which work together to make it richer and more interesting, but also which somehow work even if you know very little about it at all.

For example, while knowing the various literary sources for each Sinner can make engaging with them more exciting, players can also bring knowledge from playing Lobotomy Corporation (which is essentially a prequel to this game) and Library of Ruina (similarly a prequel and possibly sequel), which inform and continue to exert various levels of influence on Limbus Company’s growing narrative; in the lead up to the newest Canto, Canto X, a key character from Lobotomy and Ruina became heavily referenced, but Limbus has built in various ways to “know” this character even with no other exposure. Perhaps due to the small team size and passionate investment of KJH and the Project Moon team, I really can’t think of a game (and maybe not even just game, frankly) that tells stories as well as Project Moon does. In many games, the world is just an excuse for why certain things are happening, or at least, why the player can do things; for example, I always think on how no matter how much I enjoyed Dishonored, the world it builds is shaky and at times illogical, filled with rampant sexism that no character seemingly addresses, because it was seemingly included but not thought out. Limbus Company doesn’t really work that way; things that are happening, or will happen, can happen, are linked, a web of narrative connections that has at this point spanned half a decade if one includes the starting point at Lobotomy Corporation.

The City is fascinating, providing a unique Cyberpunk Dystopia from a Korean point of view that makes its approach to capitalism, corporatism, and other dystopian staples feel refreshing and also oddly gut-punching in ways that, say, Cyberpunk 2077 or Deus Ex don’t really grapple with; at the very least, neither of them ever created a nightmarish Santa Claus, last I checked.

The Game

Before I talk a bit more about the narrative and the world and why Limbus means what it does to me, let’s talk about the game from the business standpoint. Limbus Company is a F2P game with a microtransaction gacha mechanic, and if that alone makes you check out, then that’s fine. Before you do, though, let me explain a few things that make Limbus an oddly unique game in this space: every single character you can ever obtain in this game is obtainable through farmable material known as Shards, with 3 Star characters or EGOs costing 400 shards of that specific Sinner identity. And you might ask “oh but I bet it's really hard to farm them, right”? And the answer is no, if you play the game; before the newest Canto, I sharded 10 characters and EGOs from Canto IX without spending a dime other than the battle pass, which I bought last year. And before you go “aha! See! A battle pass,” Limbus’s Season Pass runs for roughly 9 months at a time. Canto IX, The Unsevering, was released on New Year’s Eve 2025, and ended on September 16, 2026. In that span of time, just about any other game that runs a “Battle Pass” has changed it 9 times, but Limbus doesn’t, and after completing the pass, each level over the cap gains you 3 boxes for Shards; to sum this up fairly quickly, you can spend about 20 dollars every 9 months and then never spend a dime on the game otherwise, which roughly means about 20 dollars a year. There’s no skins, there’s nothing you can buy with microtransactions to “win more”, and as you gather more EGOs, they get removed from the pool of things you can get, and pulling more than 1 copy of a character does literally nothing, meaning that there’s even diminishing returns to pulling on banners.

And there aren’t any limited* characters/EGOs you can miss* either. Oh, those asterisks? Okay. Well…

Limbus runs an event called Walpurgis Night every few months, roughly around 3~4 months at a time between them. During Walpurgis Night, the newest Identities or EGOs can only be pulled from the new banner, but every previous Walpurgis Night character can be sharded during that period; when the next Walpurgis Night occurs, the characters from the previous run become shardable. As far as the “missable” characters, well… okay, there was an Arknights collab last year, and so far no one knows if it will ever rerun or be available again, so there was that. If some of this sounds a little flippant, that’s because we have one other aspect to get into about all of this: None of this really matters. Why? Because Limbus Company is a single player game; it has no “meta”, so to speak, and generally who you use or why comes down to the type of team you want to build, and the Base 12 IDs you get to start the game can clear every piece of content in the game. And I don’t even mean that in a dismissive way, I mean that it is quite easy to play the game using just base IDs and experience the story that way, unlike other games where it is presented as a “challenge”. I have a deep love of Canto VII, and I've been using the team from that Canto for nearly 2 years since it was released. Are they still "meta"? Not really, but they get the job done, they're fun, and I love seeing them, so I use them. I highly suggest you play the game, if you choose to do so, with a similar mindset: learn how keywords work, build teams around them if you can, and then also focus on playing the Identities and synergies you like the most, simple as.

But that aside, what do you DO in Limbus Company? Well, Limbus company is a sort of tactical-RPG in which your team of Sinners face off against enemies. In most normal battles, you select a chain of actions that your Sinners can take out of two tracks, and in more complicated battles, you are tasked with selecting attacks to target or respond to enemy actions (or parts). Teams can be built around various Keywords, which stand-in for element types of styles of play, such as Burn, Poise, Sinking, Tremor, and etc. These all have various synergies and play styles that are what make Limbus’s battle system crunchy and interesting to experiment with, while also offering almost any solution to any problem; while newer units and “in vogue” keywords might create a new “Best” team, there’s really nothing stopping a player from just using the IDs they like the most and powering through the game that way as well. EGOs act as a sort of equipment that your Sinners can have, offering powerful but dangerous abilities that can help turn a battle around, helping win clashes that you may otherwise lose. Aside from the copious amounts of story chapters to play through, players can also challenge a roguelite style Dungeon for extra resources (this is primarily how you farm shards and play the game without going through the story) called Mirror Dungeon, as well as some leveling stages for materials.

I will provide you one piece of advice right now, if you do end up trying the game: the Tutorial is terrible. It may actually be one of the worst tutorials I have ever seen in a game, which is fascinating, because everything else the game does is great. Here is what you need to know: 9/10 times, smash Win Rate. In the 10 out of 10 times that doesn’t seem to work? You need to read what enemies are actually doing to you and how to best respond to that action. You will also, inevitably, encounter a boss or two that will be like slamming into a wall, and the best advice I can give about that is that once you read and strategize, they become a lot easier (or you can just look up how to defeat them, no one will know). It may even sound bizarre for a game I clearly love so much, but I actually think the gameplay might be the weakest part of the entire experience. Clashing and doing cool moves feels great and it rules seeing your Identities do all sorts of sick attacks, but sometimes it can drag and you just want to know what happens next; don’t worry, nothing in the game is insurmountable, so I promise that if you find it initially a little wonky, you’ll get used to it with a little more time and familiarity. The story is worth it, I promise you.

Characters Welcome

Oh yeah, the game is very bloody, by the way.

Why is it worth it? Because Limbus does something that no other gacha game, F2P or Live Service or however you want to call it, and frankly, very few AAA titles do: it has characters that actually want you to care about them, who go through vertical growth rather than horizontal. When the game starts, Dante knows as little as the player does about each Sinner, and the disjointed and ragtag group of 12 stumble their way through just about everything, often with hilarious results as often as it can be depressing. We don’t know who any of these people are, and none of them are seemingly forthcoming with information about themselves to 12 other strangers they have just functionally been told they have to work with and live with; they don’t suffer from amnesia like Dante does, they just simply have baggage, baggage that they don’t trust to really unload on people who are barely even familiar co-workers.

A lot of that comes from how the characters are initially presented, all of them falling into various odd little archetypes. For example, Ryoshu is a surly, foul-mouthed woman who talks in abbreviations, Sinclair is a timid wimp, Meursault is a stoic gigachad, Gregor is Depressed, Faust is an insufferable know-it-all, Outis is a corporate brown-noser, and perhaps the most infamous character, Don Quixote, is a quirk chungus cringelord who screams at the top of her lungs constantly. The Sinners are almost repellant in how difficult they are to engage with or understand, and none of them really seem to want to work with the others; Ishmael and Heathcliff start by wanting to beat each other to death, Hong Lu is so aloof he may as well be in the stratosphere, Rodya just wants to eat (and is depressed) and Yi Sang has Omega Depression.

And then there is Dante.

Dante is a particularly fascinating “gacha” character. In many F2P / Gacha games, the MC is a power fantasy stand-in that the player takes the role of in order to do sick anime fighting moves or get fawned over by incredibly attractive men and women. Often, players get to choose how the character is named, sometimes what they look like, which characters you can pursue and interact with, and various other mostly cosmetic options. Dante is none of those things, because while the player views things primarily from Dante’s point of view, it becomes very clear that Dante is not the Player. This primarily materializes in the fact that Dante’s main ability is essentially an excuse to allow you to continue playing the game, but also through the narrative voice of the character; players do not get to choose what Dante does or how they act, and Dante’s internal thoughts and feelings are their own, borne out of the experience of observing the journey that the characters are all currently on. While Dante is a blank-slate, they are not a blank-slate self-insert, and as Dante becomes more confident in their role of Manager, the character similarly evolves in ways parallel to that of the Sinners. And throughout all of this, Dante is a character who no other characters can understand except the Sinners; while everyone has voice acting, Dante’s voice is literally that of a ticking clock, yet somehow, has some of the most impactful dialogue in the entire game.

As observers, we get to watch as Dante and the Sinners continue their journey, and see how they change, how they grow, how they hurt and evolve. While trying to avoid spoilers as much as possible, many of those archetypes I mentioned above change, growing far more interesting, deep, or taking on new perspectives that change not just how the character acts, but how the character is meant to be viewed; a character who seems meek and timid might become something more than that over time, while a character that seems the loner type becomes more deeply invested in the people around them. Perhaps my words are failing me to properly explain why this is so magnetic to me, but the growth of the Sinners, of Dante, of even Vergilius over the three years I’ve been playing Limbus Company is something no other game offers. And I will be up front: some of the early Cantos are a lot weaker than the later ones, but even then they’re still very good, and from what I’ve just experienced of the latest one, I feel even stronger in my love of the game and also that it absolutely is worth the time and investment to make it to this latest chapter if the early ones grab your attention.

And then there’s the music. Oh, the music. Project Moon knows how to have music match their game perfectly, either in game itself, on the menus, or at the end of Cantos, all of which builds an aesthetic package that I can’t quite find anywhere else. The game makes some fascinating genre choices as well; for example, a boss theme I’m quite fond of is hard to describe in any other fashion than “Victorian Steampunk Dubstep Jazz”, while another is “Classic Orchestra Existential Body Horror Electronica”. Each Canto since Canto III has had a song performed by artist Mili, all of which exist to nail home the big narrative pay-offs to that particular chapter; Through Patches of Violet and Hero make me tear up whenever I listen to them, and even telling you those titles feels like I’m spoiling the game somehow. Every Canto also ends with a rendition of a song titled “Pass On”, sung by the voice actor of the Sinner that Canto is for (Heathcliff and Don Quixote’s are my favorite). Much of this is the bow on top of the package that is Limbus Company’s strength: the characters at the center of the story, whose voices we become acquainted with, whose themes and motifs we learn, whose interactions change and mature like real people’s would after learning to understand one another; it is almost aspirational at times to try and consider how to create a world as rich as Project Moon’s, to develop characters that are so shockingly human even when they are doing completely abnormal things.

A major theme of Limbus Company’s grander narrative is the bonds, chains, or other aspects we might try to use to express the connections between ourselves and others. As the game moves from the earliest Cantos into the newest release, these bonds make up much of the narrative web of the game: The characters bonds between one another, the world around them, the lives they’ve left behind or are returning to, the City that they live in, but also the web of connection between Limbus Company and the literature it references, the grander world around us, the players, that it and we are a part of. And while bonds may sound positive, it also explores the chains that bind us as well: the traumas, conflicts, experiences that we all endure and survive. Because despite the nightmarish world that Limbus Company takes place in, the scariest things the game presents us with are very human.

An Open Book

I mentioned earlier that I love Limbus Company, and Project Moon, and I don’t mean to undercut that by having tried being bombastic to make you, the reader, empathize with me and thus play the game I like. If not a single person plays the game, I suppose I will be sad, but as I said, this wasn’t a review. This was a profession of my deep love for the creative world that this game has created, and that it inspires my own attempts to create, to build, to write, and that I want to offer that as a way to perhaps convince someone else to take a look into the toybox the game provides. There’s not an easy way to quantify that love I have for it either; it isn’t like I can say I have bought the most in Limbus Bucks, or merchandise, or anything like that; the love I’m expressing isn’t really material in that sense.

Back in 2024, when Canto 7 had wrapped, I was gifted an amateur recording of the song “Hero”, and it remains perhaps one of the most meaningful gifts anyone has ever given me. Maybe some of this sounds silly, trite, or even lame, but that goes back to what I said before: expressing love for something makes you vulnerable, and that vulnerability allows you to be hurt by people, even when they don’t mean to. I’m sure I will see all sorts of reactions to the article, to what I’ve said, ranging from disinterest to insults or dismissal, to positivity or even commiseration. But I didn’t write 4000+ words about this game to worry about whether people will like reading it or what they will think of me after having done so. I wrote them because as the tenth chapter in my favorite game opens, I finally wanted to try and put into words something that I’ve struggled to express, in the hope that they might reflect on how inspiration can come from unexpected places; that a game I tried playing on a whim 3 years ago could have such a lasting impact on how I view and consider other media I encounter, but also on the things I try to make myself. Perhaps the actual point of this “review”, if we still insist on calling it that, is expressing why something can have such an impact on our lives can also be impossible to properly convey to another person, but it is at least worth trying. Did I do a good job of that? I don’t know. I’d like to think that I did, and perhaps rather than just telling you why you should play something or not play something, I can make you either take the time to appraise things you love yourself, or possibly even take a leap on something new.
