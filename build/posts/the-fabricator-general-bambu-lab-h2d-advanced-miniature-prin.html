The Fabricator General: Bambu Lab H2D Advanced Miniature Printing Review

November 7th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Half a year ago, I penned a long-form review of Bambu Lab's H2D 3D printer. It's a beastly dual-nozzle machine with some very interesting extra features, like laser cutting and pen plotting. That two-part review sets the context for this article, so it might be helpful to read it first. Part 1 is here, and Part 2 is here.

In the intervening time, I've been getting used to the H2D's fairly complex inner workings and worked my way to miniature printing. This was my goal since receiving the machine, but I wanted to actually get to grips with its capabilities rather than roll the very complex task of FDM miniature printing into an already-long review.

Where I left it off in my reviews, I assessed the H2D as a very capable printer that achieved its goals of material saving via two nozzles, and its secondary features as a blade cutter, laser cutter/engraver, and pen plotter also worked well without detracting from its core role as a printer. Those statements remain true. I also mused that the most interesting part of two nozzle FDM printing was the possibility to dedicate one nozzle to a support filament and one nozzle to the main filament. This addresses some longstanding issues with printing high-detail or multi-material projects on an FDM printer.

Before I discuss my experiences, two points:

  1. This article isn't really aimed at 3D printing beginners. The H2D isn't really a beginner's machine on the whole. I reviewed Bambu Lab's entry level A1 last year with a focus on beginners. Catch that review here and here.
  2. My H2D was provided by Bambu Lab for review purposes, although I've been sustaining it by myself. I thank Bambu Lab for providing the device while guaranteeing that Tabletop Battles retains editorial control over what we write.
Home-fabricating custom-size conversion parts for my miniatures has completely changed how I approach the hobby.

Miniature printing is a challenge for FDM printers because FDM machines extrude molten fluid into the open air. They're tailored for all-round performance with different molten materials, and cost-effective build volumes. FDM machines tend to give good value while building moderate-to-large objects. While they can achieve fairly impressive detail, they're not optimized for high-detail work the way SLA resin printers are.

The print processes of FDM are also less conducive to ultra-fine detail work. Molten material has a natural bit of ooze and movement. Lines and layers are adhered to each other while having microscopic imperfections. An FDM printed object is never solid because each line and layer will always have areas of imperfect contact. This isn't a major issue with anything thicker than a marker (unless you want load-bearing parts), but durability becomes a serious problem when you get to miniature details. Again, this compares unfavourably with SLA processes because SLA can create a much more solid and uniform object.

This doesn't make FDM printers the abject 'loser' in miniature printing versus their SLA counterparts. FDM printers are more cost-effective by build volume and material costs. They're less messy, and the materials are less toxic and don't need nearly as much PPE as liquid resin. FDM printers can also feed a wider array of materials. Bioplastic PLA is standard, but PET-derived PETG is also common. There's flexible/rubbery TPU, nylon, ultralight carbon fiber materials, wood-infused, numerous mixes. Resin printers support liquid resin, with the only differences being colour and mechanical characteristics.

In essence, comparing FDM to SLA is not a matter of winners and losers. As with all materials science, it's a matter of trade-offs and use cases.

This doesn't alleviate the challenges related to high-detail FDM printing, though. Most of these challenges are inherent to the manufacturing process. Making an FDM machine work with ultra-fine projects in a single material runs counter to its intended role as a device that cracks out uniform, mid-sized and larger prints in a variety of materials. So this is where the fun begins.

Goals and Expectations

The Starting Point

In my time with the H2D, I've come to know it as a mechanically complex, but capable and generally reliable machine. Its dual-nozzle feature isn't over-engineered. The internals are fairly accessible, and Bambu Labs' large amount of wiki documentation (with photos and videos) was helpful for navigating maintenance issues.

My compliments may sound dry or unenthusiastic, but from an engineering design perspective, the H2D is a very good printer. Its specifications are numerous: heated, enclosed chamber; dual-nozzle toolhead; toolhead that supports a laser cutter/pen plotter/cutting blade; sizeable build volume; powerful active ventilation; two filament feeds compatible with filament storage devices; real-time camera monitoring. These design specs make the H2D an inherently complex machine. Complexity usually worsens usability and maintenance.

The fact that the H2D's features work as advertised and are comprehensible to me is a note of praise for its design. Was I frustrated when I had to open up the extruder assembly to access a filament break behind the wheels? Yes. But I also noted that the extruder's design allows it to be opened with one allen key and assisted by simple, 3D-printable tools whose files were provided Bambu Lab. Maintenance procedures weren't enjoyable, but I was always relieved to see that it was easier than it could have been in a worse-designed machine.

2000 Hours and Counting

I began working toward miniature printing in earnest after putting about 2000 print-hours onto the H2D. By that point, I was familiar with basic materials (PLA, PETG) and had also used ASA and wood infused filament. I was adapted to the machine's maintenance workflows and mechanical processes. These lessons are useful to take in before embarking on a challenge like miniature printing. The more you know about the basics, the easier it is to work out if a new problem is a general issue or something specific to a more challenging task.

Despite inherent challenges to FDM miniature printing I listed earlier on, FDM printers like the H2D also have things working in their favor. For one, it's exceedingly easy to pause, cancel, or restart a print that's going poorly. Unlike resin machines, restarting a print is largely mess free. You just need to scrape the failed project off the bed and dispose of it. If you're in a hurry, it can be pushed over the build plate's for later disposal. It's also much easier to observe an ongoing FDM print by looking at it, whereas resin prints are submerged in a liquid bath behind a case. These characteristics are conducive to learning and experimentation because you'll spend a lot of time checking on things or trying again.

The H2D opens up advanced support techniques like using water-soluble PVA alongside a regular plastic.

A Question of Finer Details

I was able to achieve fairly decent miniature-scale prints on my A1 (some of which were shown in my review), but I looked forward to better results from the H2D. For one, I expected better because the H2D is a much more expensive and sophisticated machine than the entry-level A1. The H2D also has plenty of features that could theoretically stabilize the print compared to the A1. That should improve the success rate and consistency of prints.

When people think of miniature printing, they normally consider detail resolution first. A printer's ability to resolve complex detail and produce a complete, high-fidelity object is held in high regard among people interested in them for tabletop hobbies. I don't blame them—miniature hobbies are detail-oriented and attract like-minded people. But solely honing in on a printer's detail resolution ignores numerous other pros and cons. Printing should also match use cases. If each print requires lavish attention and finishing, you'll be hard-pressed to squeeze an army's worth of conversion bits out. If your printer has a high potential for detail but is a nightmare to use and you lose interest, the whole endeavor has failed. And so forth.

When it comes to consumer printers, resin SLA machines are the undisputed king of detail resolution. They make sacrifices in almost every other category to achieve it.

Coming from the A1, I wasn't expecting vastly improved detail resolution. Both machines peak at a 0.2mm nozzle diameter and while the H2D is meant to be precise and capable, it's also intended be an all-rounder. Its marketing materials refer to it as a 'home manufacturing hub'. A multi-purpose machine.

Instead, my hopes lay in miniature reliability. I've had plenty of prints fail on my A1 due to changes in temperature or air movement (a cold draft in winter, or suddenly turning on the aircon nearby). The A1's lone nozzle makes multi-material printing very inefficient. The A1's conventional bed-slinger design subjects the print to a lot of lateral force over the course of several hours, which can weaken bed adhesion.

The H2D's design addresses many of these concerns. Its fully enclosed chamber gives me total control over internal conditions, so a sharp breeze from the window shouldn't risk a sensitive print's cooling/first layer adhesion. The Core X/Y motion system ensures that the printed object is not being flung back and forth hundreds of times an hour. Two nozzles add redundancy by allowing me to maintain one nozzle in between prints and use the other. It also opens up possibilities involving multi-material objects (which I'll discuss later).

The Results of a Long Process

My discussion covers 3 miniature prints from the H2D. Three vehicle passengers (28mm heroic scale), a wheeled LAV vehicle (28mm scale), and an articulated Rocky figure from the Project Hail Mary movie. These three models cover the main miniature printing cases for 3D printing: infantry models, vehicles, and monster-sized models with or without articulation.

Rocky and the LAV are painted, and the infantry are shown unpainted as a reference for print quality before painting.

Wheeled Light Armored Vehicle

This modular wheeled LAV by Tabletop-Terrain is a good 3D printing reference for two main reasons. Firstly, it's roughly the size and dimensions of a typical wargaming vehicle — with a boxy hull, detailed addons, and weapon options. Secondly, it was actually designed for 3D printing. Printing file sites are saturated with files that are just a single 3D model converted to STL format and billed as print-ready. That's now how any of this works.

3D printing at miniatures is a measured task that needs experimentation and iteration. Trying to make a one-piece brick work usually leads to a failure-prone, low quality result. Supports will be positioned in areas that obscure detail. Support removal between crevices is a mess. Large parts held by spindly, unfitted structures are prone to breakage.

An STL/3mf file published by someone with the intention of 3D printing is a joy to lay out.

This LAV model avoids those problems by being a 3D printable kit. I printed out the individual parts, made size adjustments as needed, and built the model up over time. Separate pieces allowed me to arrange the parts for efficient and aesthetically conscious printing. If individual parts failed, I could adjust their positions or settings without compromising the whole model. Every part could be laid on the bed to conceal support scarring — the final model required less cleanup than a typical plastic kit because support scars were hidden and 3D printers don't produce mold lines.

Upon completion, the fit was excellent and it was customized to my desires. I opted for a tasteful autocannon turret with lots of explosive reactive armour plating, but the kit has many other configurations.

I'll always be impressed that something like this could be made from raw materials at home and painted like a store-bought model.

This was a well-designed file and the H2D knocked it out of the park. The pre-painting finish was already excellent and paint covered the vast majority of layer lines. It took primer and paint gloriously (like any plastic model) and was more comfortable to assemble than any injection molded kit because there were no mold lines or flash to trim.

I'm confident that with another two attempts, I could make a build plate arrangement that uses the H2D's sizable build area to make one of these vehicles in a single plate. Basically just start the file, let the H2D cook for a few days to a week per model, and hey presto, new vehicle for my collection.

Minor surface imperfections are visible in the final model, but they're unnoticeable from a couple feet away.

Actual print time is highly variable depending on your settings, but I opted for higher quality, lower speeds and it took around 6 days to complete my first vehicle — and this is with numerous adjustments and reprints in between to make the fit perfect. A week-long print sounds substantial, but that's only slightly worse than shipping times for a kit I'd order. And not many people can paint at a rate that matches a printer output.

I did not apply fine sanding or filler primer to the project. Doing so would have made it even smoother, albeit be time-consuming.

The model is weighty and could be made lighter or heavier with simple adjustments to the infill density and number of wall loops. It took to primer and airbrushing without fuss. Carefully applied enamel washes also work, but I had to pay attention to not drip them into areas that had more layer lines — those were reserved for airbrush or drybrush techniques.

Sloped or curved surfaces show more evidence of 3D printing. Layer lines are an unavoidable reality, but they're imperceptible unless the model is examined closely.

Unsurprisingly, the H2D did great when handed a well-designed vehicle kit file. The filament cost for such a model would be minor — easily under $9, even with experimentation. The energy cost on this print was negligible. It's also very repeatable and I look forward to trying out another vehicle in a different configuration.

Vehicle Passengers

Seated vehicle passengers are a rarity because they're specialized designs that represent very little monetary value for businesses. This is less of an issue for 3D printing, since their largest skill investment comes from their design. Once the file is up, the manufacturing costs are absorbed by anyone who prints it. I still remember the days when Forge World had Death Korps of Krieg passengers for their numerous transport vehicles — those models were packed with love and dedication to the art, but weren't financially sound.

This modular vehicle crew/passengers set by Davik_Designs is the exact kind of flavourful thing nerds like me crave, but aren't economically feasible for companies to mass-produce. I found them while searching for passengers for my Taurox kit. This Taurox came from an old Start Collecting: Militarum Tempestus box and I'm turning it into a Commissar Graves proxy. I prefer it over the the bedazzled commissariat halftrack that Graves normally comes in. A set of 3D printed wheels and suitably grizzled passengers followed.

3D printing has opened up conversions that would otherwise be impossible.

Davik_Designs' vehicle crew/passenger kit is also a good one. It's highly modular and designed for 3D printing. Hobbyists will be familiar with the rigmarole of putting arms onto torsos and picking their favorite heads. The joy of 3D printing such a modular kit is I can have as many permutations as I want and I'm not constrained to the sprue contents. I ended up with a commissar-like stand-in wielding a chunky pistol, 2 troopers with short laser carbines, and another seated trooper with a shotgun. All with different poses and sized to my liking.

These 28mm Heroic-scale infantry show had excellent detail resolution. Fine elements like camouflage foliage in helmets and jacket details were intact.

Speaking of sizing… Another perk of 3D printing is that you have freedom to resize. The original files weren't designed for the Taurox — I have no idea what they were designed for — so their legs were a bit too short to touch the floor. I addressed that by elongating the legs slightly. I also adjusted the torsos to better fit the chunky 28mm heroic scale that matched the 40K aesthetic.

Sizing the parts and experimenting to get a perfect fit is so much more feasible when you can print new figures on-demand.

Print quality was also excellent. The officer's fingers that grip the pistol and jacket details are wonderfully rendered. The shotgun trooper has foliage stuck in the helmet for camouflage and it too is present. The wrinkles in their boots are defined, as are the pouches attached to their outerwear. I'm actually astounded that this came off a filament printer. Yes, a resin printer would be a massive leap in quality, but these came off a build plate without any bad smell or toxicity.

The H2D rendered mino details like ridges under the pistol magazine, boot tread, and trouser wrinkles with little difficulty.

3D printing is a dream tool for converters and hobbyists because it lets us do tiny production runs of extremely specific models that precisely match our needs. I'm confident in saying that no company manufactured a modular gas-masked sci-fi vehicle passenger seat that perfectly fits a Taurox' passenger compartment. I could only get such a thing by finding an STL and resizing it to fit.

And turning a Taurox kit that was otherwise gathering dust into a compelling and relevant conversion project is a fantastic lease on life for the original model, too.

Articulated Rocky Figure (Project Hail Mary)

My best friend has been fixated on Project Hail Mary since seeing the movie. She followed it by listening to and reading the book, then imbibing a worrying amount of fanfiction. I figured a desktop-sized model of Rocky wouldn't go amiss. This articulated Rocky figure is based on the official model, but tweaked by Makerworld user sliceNDice for easier printing.

I printed it over the course of a week while experimenting with alternative support materials, then painted it in two evenings. It offered some interesting support configuration challenges and taught me a lot about the H2D's best feature: multi-material supports.

This articulated figure was a good challenge for printed supports, as its pieces have all-round surface detail.

The main challenge this figure presented is that it's covered in all-round detail. The model is also highly organic in shape — there are no perfectly flat, featureless faces I could allow to be scarred. The limbs are packed with texture and inscriptions that make traditional supports impossible.

When printing a supported part, the supports will leave unsightly scarring and marks where they touched the model. This is because most supports are the same material as the model so you're essentially printing excess material onto your final project that will damage the detail. The usual solution is to pick the least aesthetically important side and ruin that with support scars, but this is impossible here because every single part of the model has texture and detail worth preserving.

Enclosing the model in supports is only feasible when the support material does not fuse to the base material. This support configuration is unusual, but extremely stable and durable.

The answer to support scarring is multi-material printing. Supports can be printed in a different material that doesn't fuse with the main material. They'll still provide physical support, but they won't harm the project's appearance by fusing to the project. They're also much easier to remove. Multi-material printing with a single-nozzle printer is very time and material inefficient. Your print time is effectively doubled since the printer needs to switch between main material and supports each and every single layer. The printer may spend more time switching materials than printing. You also waste an enormous amount of material on reloads and purges.

The H2D addresses both problems with its dual-nozzle configuration. It can simply load a second material into the second nozzle and switch between the nozzles with only small amounts of time and material loss.

Rocky's printed pieces prior to support removal (note the one part supported by yellow PVA)

I tried two support materials. Well… in truth, I screamed, cried, and threw up trying to make PVA supports work and got a single piece of Rocky out to a good standard. And then I used PETG supports and it went quite well.

See, some manufacturers sell a water-soluble PVA filament that is designed for use as a support material. When the print's done, you can just throw it into a bowl of water and the PVA will partially dissolve for very easy removal. It's a dream. Except PVA is one of the most temperamental, awful filaments I've ever worked with.

Despite PVA's awful temperament, successful supports are a dream to remove. This piece peeled off the PVA supports without water and all detail was preserved.

PVA's main problem is that it's designed to dissolve in water. The atmosphere has a lot of water in it. PVA is so water-sensitive that it deteriorates in any environment that is not a specialized filament dryer. It will degrade in the air. It will degrade when stored in a ziploc bag. It will degrade in the AMS 2 Pro while printing in heating mode because it basically needs to be baked while printing to have a good shot of working. The AMS 2 Pro has a maximum drying temperature of 65° C. PVA does not dry effectively at that temperature. It would only dry properly inside a higher-temperature dryer like Bambu Labs' AMS HT. That's not a slight against the AMS 2 Pro — it's a very decent dryer for ordinary, low temperature materials like PLA and PETG. It's a slight against PVA because PVA sucks. Even with my AMS 2 Pro drying the PVA at max temperature while printing, I could see the filament steaming as it extruded from the nozzle. The stuff was designed to be water soluble and it will start dissolving in the open air. Awful.

Anyway, PETG supports went great.

Removing wraparound PETG supports was time consuming, but 'unwrapping' the supports to show preserved detail is highly enjoyable.

I configured the supports into a snuggly enclosed grid around Rocky's body parts. They held the printed part firmly and cuddled it during printing. This created a system of maximum stability. Enclosing your print in dense supports like this would be incredibly stupid if you were only using single-material printing because it would just fuse all the supports to your print and ruin all the surface detail.

But PETG is incompatible with PLA and doesn't fuse to it, so I was able to cut the supports away and leave Rocky's many details intact.

Rocky's signature etchings and surface texture remain crisp after painting.

The large, single-piece torso was the most challenging support removal because it was wrapped up tight. It took over an hour of work with pliers, side cutters, and gentle heat gun application to break them away. Ultimately, I got all of Rocky's supports off without damaging any of the precious surface texture.

Once freed from PETG Jail, Rocky was a joy to paint. Each limb was a sub-assembly and the PLA took primer and airbrushing without any issue. A healthy wash, drybrush, and some green accents completed the figure in record time. My best friend appreciates it greatly.

This textured and angular model was very receptive of drybrushing.

The goal of this project was to demonstrate multi-material supports with the H2D. That is a feature you can't execute with any practicality on a single-nozzle machine. It's also a critical feature for miniature hobbyists because we value detail and resolution so much. I genuinely think the H2D is one of the machines that is closing the gap with resin printers in the miniature realm. And it's not doing it by matching resin printers in resolution — that's not realistic. It's chasing them down by being a practical and mess-free way to get good-quality miniature prints.

Final Thoughts

I've put over 2000 hours into my H2D since my initial review and the machine's capabilities continue to surprise me. Its general performance exceeds the smaller A1 — which should be expected considering their comparative costs and where they stand in the product range. However, the H2D's miniature printing performance is also vastly better.

This improvement is weighted toward reliability and dual-nozzle features. An evenly heated enclosure does wonders to isolate my print reliability issues. That, and the dual-nozzles did meet my expectations because they opened up multi-material supports that would be impractical on a single-nozzle/single-tool machine.

During my testing, I was able to achieve slightly higher detail resolution with my H2D with a shorter learning curve than my A1 experience. I attribute this to the H2D's stability and comprehensive error logging features that made troubleshooting mechanical issues much easier than on the A1. In my eyes, the H2D is an undeniably effective printer for miniatures and tabletop gaming. It's certainly at the limit of details a filament printer can resolve at home. I don't see this changing until a major breakthrough in FDM detail resolution happens — like sub 0.2mm nozzles or some pretty incredible advances in filament technology.
