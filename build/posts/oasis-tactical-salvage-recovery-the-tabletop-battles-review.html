Oasis: Tactical Salvage Recovery - The Tabletop Battles Review

July 31st, 2026 via Goonhammer

There comes a time in every space cowboy's life when they've just gotta make a name for themselves. And when that day came, they came to you. You'd sign them up, slap them in a grav-tank, and ship them off to the Oasis, another galaxy filled with the ruins of innumerable alien civilizations and shot through with incredibly advanced--not to mention incredibly valuable--tech. Once they got there, they'd work together with a team under your command to extract as much of that wonder-tech to earn them their pay.

Of course, that was then, and this is now: The interstellar gateway that led from our Solar System to the Oasis is now unstable, and getting home with your crew's dubiously-gotten gains is no longer a guarantee. What was once a tense resource-recovery gig has now devolved into something just shy of outright war, and your crew will have to fight for their lives (read: Your profit) if they want to have a chance of being welcomed home.

A table for Oasis: Tactical Salvage Reclamation | Credit: Collins Epic Wargames

All of this forms the background for Oasis: Tactical Salvage Recovery, an upcoming 6mm scale wargame from Collins Epic Wargames. You'll field a crew of hard-sci fi-inspired vehicles, drones, and aircraft who have set out to stake a claim, grab anything valuable, and get out. And even if you can't find anything valuable on the field, that other guy's railgun might be worth a few bucks.

Thanks to Collins Epic Wargames for a preview copy of the rules and some review models.

Entering the Oasis

Oasis pitches itself as “Tactical Salvage Recovery,” and that’s honestly a very apt description. The goal of the game is to grab three salvage tokens, carry them back to an extraction beacon, and hope your opponent can’t steal them in the intervening turn. If that doesn’t work, you can just table them, though chewing through their entire list is almost always a last resort.

As for how to get salvage, you’ll generate it in two ways: Any time you kill a unit with 2HP or more, you can choose to drop some salvage tokens next to it. Alternatively, there are five “points of interest” on the board your units can survey for useful salvage. Four of them are “minor” points, each of which can generate a maximum of one piece of salvage, while the fifth one, a “major” point, can yield as much salvage as you’ve got units to dig for it. All five set up near the middle of the board, with the major bang-on center of the battlefield, and the four minors go in the middle 24” of the board wherever it makes sense.

Mount Up

Building a list is about as simple as it can be. You have 400 points, and you can bring whatever up to that limit with only a few restrictions:

  • You can’t bring more than 20 units in total.
  • You can’t bring more than 10 of any individual unit.
  • At least one of your units needs the Surveyor trait, which helps you dig up salvage from points of interest.
  • At least one of your units needs the Transport trait, which you’ll use to pick up the salvage you find and carry it away.
A Tigershark and some supporting units. | Credit: Collins Epic Wargames

You’ll also pick a pair of “Command Cards” that give you a few abilities you can spend your command points on over the course of the game. Each one has 4 abilities that cost varying number of CP, and they can have huge impacts on how you play the game.

Unit costs and sizes run the gamut: Smaller units like drones, recon vehicles, and lighter tanks come in around 20 points, and many of them are only an inch or so long. On the other end of the spectrum, the Tigershark Grav Frigate comes in at roughly half your total force allocation for a model that’s just over 8” long. There’s plenty of cool stuff in the middle, too: My personal favorite, the Zeus Superheavy Grav Tank, is a 2.5” inch long hovering harbinger of death equipped with a dual railgun that can threaten just about anything you point it at for a cost of 77 points. Thankfully, if you see a unit that speaks to you, there’s no need to worry: There are no factions or army lists here, so every unit in the game is available for you to choose from.

Unit Card for the Zeus Superheavy Gravtank | Credit: Collins Epic Wargames

A unit card tells you most of what you’ll need in the midst of a game, and Oasis’s cards structured similarly enough to ones in games you’ve likely played before. Each card starts with movement speed and defensive stats, then provides a list of the unit’s weapons, and wraps up with some special rules that help define the unit’s role on the table. The cards are thoughtfully laid out, and nearly all of the info you’ll need to use any given unit is right at your fingertips the moment you need it. Many of those units are fairly straightforward, but some of them have some really cool and unusual rules. Take the “Titan” Heavy Lift VTOL: Its Heavy Lift Transport rule allows it to pick up nearly any model on the table and start carrying it around.

Now, I’m going to stop you right there. I am worried you might have misunderstood me. I’m concerned that you just read “its Heavy Lift Transport rule allows it to pick up any of your models on the table and start carrying it around.” No? Well, if what you read was, “its Heavy Lift Transport rule allows it to pick up any of your or your opponent’s models on the table and start carrying it around,” you’re still wrong. This bad boy can grab units, salvage, extraction beacons, and even Minor Points of Interest, and start carting them around the table to a more favorable position. It’s a super cool idea, and one that won’t always play out in your favor, but having the option in front of you will have you constantly wondering if you could use it to make some crazy galaxy-brained play. And occasionally, it’ll even pay off.

Get Stuck In

Actually getting out there and into a scrap is very simple, as is resolving what happens once you’re there. Your battlefield is a 6’x3’ table, which is a table size I’m increasingly liking these days: The 6’ length provides plenty of room for maneuvering to make longer ranged weapons and faster units more meaningful, and the 3’ width is just wide enough to not feel cramped while still providing some space along the edge of the board for unit cards and other play aids on most tables. If you’ve got a table wide enough for a game of 40k, you’ll have 8” of space along the edge (or 4” on each side if you run the mat down the middle) to lay out unit cards or put dice or whatever.

Deployment is on the short edges, and you’ll want to put plenty of terrain out in between your deployment zones. Make sure you’ve got a good mix of varying types of terrain: Buildings and forests and the like are great and you’ll want to include some of them. But due to the strong “sci-fi frontier” theming shot throughout rules here, adding in some hills, cliffs, rock formations and similar stuff will not only let you take the fullest advantage of the terrain rules on offer, it also does a lot to sell the setting and themes. After my first test game to see how the rules worked, I found myself looking on line to see if I could find a heat cutter nearby to slice up some 1” foam to make some classic old-hammer stackable hills, as I think they’d be perfect here.

Moving around is simple for the most part, and it’s relatively easy to figure out what units can move over what terrain. Most terrain that’s 1” or shorter can be driven over by all your units, while units with the “grav drive” trait can hover over anything up to 6”, and proper aircraft can zip right over anything that’s a foot high or shorter. And if you find yourself ending a movement at the edge of a piece of terrain, don’t worry: Not only can you place a model so that it’s overhanging an edge, you can even have it sitting “tilted” over an edge, so long as it doesn’t fall off or roll around.

Knock ‘Em Dead

Combat in the game is resolved using custom dice with four separate symbols on them. And here I have to be honest: I’m of two minds here. The downside is pretty obvious: You have to have the dice, which means that the game is a little more difficult to pick up and play if you just grab the models. That said, there are a couple of ways to get around it: First, you can get some regular d8s and match your results to a “key” that tells you all 8 potential faces, or you can use the game’s dice-roller app. Having now done both, I prefer the dice-roller since it doesn’t require me to spend time matching numbers to symbols, and that time will add up over the course of a game. But there’s still nothing like rolling dice in a wargame, so if you don’t mind picking up the custom dice and can get your hands on some, that’s ultimately what I’d recommend.

The upside of those custom dice comes from actually using them. Six of the faces have some combination of three symbols—target, impact, and pierce—one is blank, and the last one has a single symbol: The Gate. To attack, you start by rolling a number of dice equal to your weapon’s ACC stat, then counting up the number of “target” results you got. If you meet or exceed your target’s Dodge stat, you landed a hit, and it’s time to see if you dealt any damage. To do that, you roll your weapon’s DMG stat and look for “impact” and “pierce” results. For the most part, these results are interchangeable, but some units can ignore “pierce” results unless the attacker has a particular rule. Roll enough successes to hit your target’s DEF stat, and you just dealt a point of damage. Deal the last point of damage on your opponent’s unit, and it’s destroyed, possibly leaving some sweet, sweet salvage if it was big enough—and if you’re willing to risk your opponent getting there first.

This might sound like a lot, but in practice it resolves very quickly. All you’re doing is rolling dice and counting symbols, then comparing them to a single stat. Since you’re not having to count dice pips or apply modifiers, moving from roll to resolution is fast and easy. And given that all faces other than the blank and the Gate have 3 symbols on them, you’ll rarely find yourself in a situation where a unit can’t do anything to an enemy. After all, even a Tigershark with its mighty 9 DEF can be wounded with a mere 3 dice, assuming you roll hot.

But what about that last symbol? Well, it does nothing on its own. But remember those command cards you picked when you were building your list? Some of them have rules that trigger off of a Gate symbol, as do certain unit abilities. Which ones are in play will vary depending on what units and command cards you picked, and the Gate isn’t always a good thing. But even the simplest effects that feature it can be very impactful, so keep an eye out for it.

An Albatross Gunship | Credit: Collins Epic Wargames

Cover is also streamlined and gives a solid set of situational bonuses. Solid terrain like buildings blocks line of sight like you’d expect, while other terrain that isn’t solid is an Obstruction that increases your target’s Dodge value if you shoot through it. Push a unit up to within 1” of an Obstruction, and it moves into cover. That means it not only ignores the Dodge penalty when attacking through that terrain, it also picks up a free Target result on ACC rolls to boot. Manage to take the high ground and you’ll get an additional Impact result on DMG rolls instead.

Ride Off Into the Sunset

All of this combines with the individual rules on your unit cards to make for a game that’s surprisingly deep for how accessible it is. Every individual step is quick to pick up and easy to understand, but the intersection between movement, terrain rules, and the various unit rules and command abilities frequently makes for a pleasant little brain-burner as you work out what your next play should be. And all of this is against the background of that deceptively-simple win condition: All you ever have to do is snag 3 pieces of salvage, and you can drag a victory kicking and screaming out from the jaws of almost certain defeat. It’s harder than it sounds, but once those salvage tokens start popping up, there’s a palpable tension in the air as you and your opponent both try to work out how to get them out without losing half your force in the process.

Oasis isn’t a game that’s going to consume your life over the next year or so, but that’s largely because it’s not trying to be. It’s dead simple to get to the table, and once you’re there it’s nearly as easy to play through a game to a satisfying conclusion. But that simplicity and ease of play doesn’t mean there’s no meat on this bone: You’ll find yourself checking sightlines, agonizing over positioning and facing, and double-, triple-, or even quadruple-checking ranges and firing arcs to see if moving out to grab that last piece of salvage could possible be worth the risk.

And all of this comes in a tight package: The preview rulebook I received is just 17 pages long, and between those rules and the cards for the units, I didn’t have any trouble working through a game. And given the 400-point limit and the costs of the varying units, pulling together a force large enough to play a full-strength game with a friend won’t take you nearly as long as for some games.

All in all, I’m excited to get Oasis to the table with some friends once it's out in the wild, and I’m already noodling over how I could put together a tableful of terrain that fits the harder sci-fi, frontier theming that’s shot through the rules and the models. If you’re looking for a solid skirmish game with a rough-and-tumble feel that absolutely refuses to waste your time, you’d be hard pressed to do better. If it sounds like the game for you, check out the crowdfunding campaign over on Gamefound.
