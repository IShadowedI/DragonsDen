Tabletop Battles Reviews Tenebris Somnia

October 17th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Arriving just in time for the last half of October, New Blood dropped the finalized version of the vaulted "FMV Survival Horror" Tenebris Somnia, teased quite a while back with a pretty impressive trailer and demo nearly two years ago. The game especially caught my eye because of New Blood itself, a publisher that's produced some of my favorite games in recent memory (Ultrakill, Fallen Aces, and FAITH), but it wasn't exactly explained how the game would combine the live-action FMV elements with the gameplay itself, which seemed to feature pixelated side-scrolling action. So when the game released earlier yesterday, I took advantage of some of the extra time I had on my hands to dive in. I figured if it didn't end up impressing me, I'd simply refund it, and if it was at least decent, I could maybe finish it over the weekend.

As I put this review together, it is currently 5:30 AM in my timezone, and I realize that I spent much of my day playing Tenebris Somnia, watching other people play Tenebris Somnia, taking a little break to do my household tasks while thinking about Tenebris Somnia, and then finally having felt that I digested the game enough, sat down to write a review about Tenebris Somnia.

The concept now of even having considered thinking of a refund is quaint.

Full Motion, Live Action, All Gas

To address perhaps the biggest thing the game made waves with, Tenebris Somnia features live action cutscenes that are primarily acted out without voice acting; while not exactly silent films, the music, sound effects, and the occasional panicked noise or gasp or growl is all that fills the space. The initial "FMV" tag was something that I think stands out as something I'm not entirely sure I'm sure is the correct tag to use, but also there really aren't many other proper ways to use them. For example, the famous trailer footage used of the demonic presence lunging at our protagonist, Julia, and in the game you'll see that same scene as well as quite a few others. The issue I've had as I sat on the concept is thinking back to FMV games like the absolutely abysmal Night Trap or the amazing X-Files game for the PS1, among many others: the FMV aspect isn't directly involved in the gameplay, but instead takes the place of cut scenes and important plot points for the game. I don't think it is wrong to call it FMV, but if you're thinking of FMV games like the above mentioned adventure/point and click style, there's a bit of a difference here.

That said, the live action scenes are amazing. The acting and cinematography show some very fun craft and consideration of how to represent the ideas or concepts without relying on dialogue, but as practical effects fan, I really have to applaud the team here for what they've managed to put together; there are some truly gnarly practical effects throughout the game that blew me away when they occurred, and I don't really want to spoil them but let's just say that the monster reveals get increasingly awesome and intricate as the game progresses, with their FMVs really selling them; the endings also make really great use of the live action juxtaposition (there are 4 endings, by the way), and by the time I was done with the game I had really come around on the mixture of pixel art gameplay and live-action horror.

Surviving the Horrors

The actual gameplay of Tenebris Somnia is that of a survival horror game, played out entirely in 2D; you can move Julia along the cardinal directions (which is to say, up, down, left, and right) in the scenes in which you control her, but there is really only the flat 2D plane of the game. You'll be doing a fair bit of backtracking in the game, as the somewhat restricted environment also plays into survival horror expectations; instead of a police station or rural, foggy city, Julia spends the game exploring a twisted version of her ex's memories, primarily taking place at a film school.

Something I found fairly of interest early on is that the game does have a fair amount of written dialogue and terminology that tells the story of what is actually going on in this alternative reality and the mystery of Julia's ex (and her own issues), but there isn't a lot of reading required to complete the game, instead often having objectives explained through visual and color cues. I know it is an odd thing to praise a game for, perhaps, but I enjoyed that the game did not rely on huge flashing signs going DO THING HERE, while instead letting the visual language and storytelling do the work and hoping the player can piece things together.

Like most survival horror games, the main gameplay loop comes down to exploration and combat, and puzzle solving. I will say that puzzles were the weakest overall aspect of the game, because many of them were simply "find a symbol/locked door/etc, find the thing that matches that, open/solve/move past the obstacle". There are some pretty fun puzzles that play into the focus on film and movies that the game has going on in the background of its concept, but overall the puzzles mostly exist to give the game something to have you "do" to excuse the back and forth exploration.

Perhaps the surprise here for me is that the combat was the star. A 2D survival horror game with good combat? I have to give that a lot more thought than I have time for at the moment to whether or not I can think of a similar experience, and frankly, survival horror game combat is often not the star of the show. At some level, it is a little funny that Julia can so easily tackle all of the horrible beasts you encounter in the game, but also, it feels pretty good and controls well, allowing even for melee combat to feel less like a last resort and more like a viable option whether or not you are running low on ammo. This especially shines in the boss fights, all of which I frankly found to be a lot of fun and quite imaginative, making great use of the vibe and concepts of the game to present the bosses as fun, challenging, but never frustrating.

Absolute Cinema

I mentioned that film and movies play a big role in Tenebris Somnia, and I wanted to touch on that in a way that won't spoil the game at all; I often find that horror games are best experienced without really knowing the plot beforehand (and perhaps that can be said of just about anything with a plot, but I digress) simply because most of the time the mystery, suspense, and conceits of horror games are wrapped up in their stories. I think perhaps no series has suffered this violence of being spoilered to hell and back than Silent Hill, and Tenebris Somnia certainly has some influence and nods to that series here that makes going in with little information valuable. Primarily, it makes playing the game again, or replaying certain scenes or attempting different endings, more meaningful; you start to notice the concepts and ideas the game was sprinkling in the whole time after your initial experience, rather than having had some loudmouth tell you them all in advance.

In that way, it's pretty clear that the team on Tenebris Somnia love movies, as if the lovingly made and weird practical effects scenes weren't key enough. But there is a lot of focus on what movies are, and what they mean, and how they are made, that permeates the game and its narrative; in a lot of ways, it tends to do so both directly and subconsciously, working in concepts of perspective, storytelling, editing, and the like into the narrative of the game (as in, what Julia is experiencing) and as a story told between the developers and their audience, who are playing and watching Julia's story. While there's always a common discussion going on between the relationship of video game and film and how they influence, react with, or even at times fight for relevance against one another, Tenebris Somnia isn't super interested in that; it seems more interested in exploring the concepts of the viewer and the viewed, and the story it wants to tell resonates with that as well. Perhaps the thing I liked about the game, aside from one of the endings which I found very fun, is that it tells a solid narrative that takes full effect of the mixed media of the game itself. Julia is a solid protagonist, and I liked playing as her as much as I liked watching her, and I found that both the "game" Julia and "movie" Julia felt coherent.

If you're in the mood for a fun new horror game to play as we move closer to Halloween, or just enjoy survival horror and fascinating indie games that take advantage of their gimmick and aesthetic to the max, then Tenebris Somnia is a very solid recommendation; I found it took me about 8 hours to fully see everything the game had to offer, but I actually think I may play through it fully again after I get some sleep and rest on the game; it's a lot of fun to play, and I'm curious to see people talking about it following the initial release wave; if you end up picking the game up, I'd love to hear your thoughts on it!
