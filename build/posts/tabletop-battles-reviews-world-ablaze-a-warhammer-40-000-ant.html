Tabletop Battles Reviews: World Ablaze, a Warhammer 40,000 Anthology

June 19th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Games Workshop

Although they occupy roughly the same volume of pages as a novel, the process for editing an anthology is markedly different in a way that goes beyond just he obvious difference of "working with many writers" versus "working with one."

First there's the subject of theme. Sometimes it can be largely in the background, a gossamer thread that connects the stories like polished gems on a necklace. Other times it's a stout cord wrapping each story in its sequence. The best anthologies have something to offer that informs the selections, though it's not strictly necessary. Indeed, many of the Black Library's offerings have no discernable theme whatsoever, such as No Peace Among Stars (review) which seemed to exist only to collect the contents of recent short story Theme Weeks into a print compendium.

Second is the roster of authors. I've often written about the Black Library has long used its shorts as a talent pipeline, with the most promising writers going on to be offered novels. From the early days of Inferno! to anthologies to digital Short Story Theme Weeks, the Black Library has had a progression-system in place to help maintain bench depth.

In this, World Ablaze is a break from the norm. With only two exceptions (John Sollitto and Richard Swan1), every author here has already had at least one novel published for the Black Library. Authors like Mike Vincent (The Remnant Blade), R. S. Wilt (Final Deployment), and Rhuairidh James (Death Rider) may just be at the start of their novel-writing journeys in the grim, dark future, but they've already made the team with promising debuts (reviewed here, here, and here respectively).

The rest are longstanding veterans, and taken together it infuses World Ablaze with a high level of polish with less variation in quality. That's no disrespect to the relative rookies getting their start- many of whom are hardly new to the writing of speculative fiction, of course, but as with anything long experience brings its own value to the table.

Finally, there's the arrangement of stories. I talked about this at some length in my review of the aforementioned No Peace Among Stars anthology, but putting together a collection of short stories in many ways is like curating a mixtape/playlist of songs. Beyond the theme that's brought these stories together, you can craft an emotional journey through the order you assemble the individual components. How do you want it to open? Any tonal shifts midway through? What's the closing number? These are things the editors get to enjoy considering when working on the book.

With World Ablaze, that theme is quite simply Armageddon, and we're of to the races with an opener by Mike Vincent that sets an interesting tone.

Blood Angels Ancient from the Armageddon box. Credit: Jack Hunter

Pursuit of Redemption, by Mike Vincent

Lieutenant Calinaeus has been forgetting himself. Periods of time that seem to go missing from his memory. For any Astartes this would be concerning, but for a Blood Angel its downright alarming. After all, they carry a genetic curse- the Black Rage- that causes them to relive the last memories of their Primarch, Sanguinius. Once they're under its sway, they're reassigned to the Death Company to seek a glorious death in battle.

The suspicion of this taint hangs over Calinaeus even as he is cleared for duty by the Chaplain. As they arrive on Armageddon to wage war upon the greenskins, will Calinaeus prove the wisdom of that decision... or its folly?

Vincent crafts a surprisingly poignant tale here. This is no simple action story, but rather one laced with a reflection on what it feels like for a Blood Angel on the verge of teetering into the lost mindscape of those unable to resist the curse of their blood. The opening felt a little compressed; I'd have liked to see Vincent fill it out a but more to raise the emotional stakes, but it still works very well through the ending. I'm not sure this would have been my choice for opener, but it certainly let me know that I was going to be in for something a little different with this collection.

Bad Moons Ork Dakkajet/Wazbom Blastajet

Riderz of da Storm, by Denny Flowers

After a well-received run with the Imperium (Outgunned, Above and Beyond), Denny Flowers continues to successfully cement his reputation for strong Ork stories. Hot on the heels of Grotsnik: Da Mad Dok (review) and the terrific Ghazghkull Thraka: Warlord of Warlords (review), I found Riderz of da Storm to be sidesplittingly hilarious.

The tale involves Poko, a grot working in an Ork 'bar' out in the ash wastes who gets caught up in a dispute between a delusional dakkajet pilot and a batch of Kommandos. I'll be the first to admit I'm very picky when it comes to Ork stories, since I find too many writers lean into the stoopid when trying to ladle humor into the writing. Sprinkling in "...and den he punched a grot in da teef" here and there just doesn't move the needle for me, but then nobody's ever accused me of being fun at parties.

Flowers crushes it here. He doesn't go for the gratuitous, but rather builds scenarios for the ridiculous to unfold with a comedy writer's instincts. Without spoiling too much let's just say that Poko gets the trip of a lifetime as he finds himself unwittingly drafted as a tailgunner just as a wing of Imperial Valkyries decide to close the bar down for good.

This one, too, ended on a surprisingly touching note. Not to be missed!

The White Horror, by Jude Reid

From battle-splashed reflection on trauma and madcap hilarity, we come to the third genre in as many stories as Jude Reid (Morvenn Vahl: Spear of Faith, Fulgrim: The Perfect Son) teases a bit of monster-movie horror out of Armageddon.

Here we find a squad of Black Templars, on the frigid wastes of the planet's pole dragging a wounded battle-brother to the hoped-for safety in a nearby Steel Legion outpost. On high alert for any Ork threats, they're unprepared for the real nightmare that finds them: a Frankenstein's Monster of hate and cunning stitched straight out of some insane Painboy's fever dreams. When the hunters become the hunted, can even the Astartes manage to get out alive?

Terrific work from Reid, who pens us an all-killer, no-filler story that puts us right into the action and never lets up. There's a delightful horror vibe to it all, and Reid wastes no space here.

Source: Warhammer Community

Good Enemy, by Guy Haley

Longtime veteran Guy Haley (Shadowsword, Konrad Curze: The Night Haunter) pens a story within a story within a story here, continuing the great variety the anthology has been offering so far.

Orks make terrific villains in the Warhammer 40K universe in large part because of their simplicity. If Necrons, Drukhari, and many of the Traitor Astartes represent the ego (and/or superego) of the setting, the Tyranids and Orks are undeniably its id (with Khornates and Slaaneshi making a fair case as well).

Yet unlike the Tyranids, the Orks have individuality, society, and a loosely-defined set of beliefs that go beyond mass consumption and species propagation. Central to this is the idea of the "good enemy," those that offer adversity that pushes an Ork to better themselves. Haley gives us a novel look at this element of the Ork psyche, one which helps contextualize the entire dynamic between Commissar Yarrick and Ghazghkull Thraka.

To do so, Haley employs the unusual technique of a story that's almost entirely dialogue, one which- framed differently- could be the script for a play. When a Surgeon-Anatomist comes across a spirited debate between colleagues about what kind of men make the best soldiers, he settles the matter by recounting an experience from Armageddon.

I loved the oblique approach Haley took, and the editors placed it perfectly after the book's opening trio of more action-oriented fare.

Credit: Dan "SexCannon" Boyd

Rok Crackers, by John Sollitto

After Haley's thoughtful pause, we're right back into the action with John Sollitto. Sollitto's contributions to the Black Library include Redemption through Blood (included in Paragon of Faith and Other Stories, reviewed here) and The Redoubt (in Death and Duty: An Astra Militarum Anthology).

This is a great editorial choice following Good Enemy, as Sollitto wastes no time putting the reader on the surface of Armageddon right beside a special squad of Steel Legion. A Rok is a small asteroid hollowed out and converted into a base by the Orks, who then have a fully-functioning command center once they crash it through some hapless planet's atmosphere and embed it on the surface.

For the 'Rok Crackers' of the Steel Legion, that means it's time to saddle up, fight their way in, and use strategically-placed melta charges the bring the whole thing down.

Preferably after they've exited it, of course- but that's not always a given.

From regular grunts to special forces, the fighting soldier gets a lot of coverage in the Black Library, but less common are tales about combat engineers. Sollitto lands a great premise here, and if I had any complaint it was that there wasn't quite enough of it. At times I felt like I was listening to a podcast on 1.25 speed, the tale moving just a little too fast. Another page or two would have not been unwelcome, but as we approach the halfway point of the book there's not been a miss thus far!

Credit: Robert "TheChirurgeon" Jones

Steel Mayhem, by Justin Woolley

Haley wasn't the only one to play with structure in World Ablaze. In Steel Mayhem, Justin Woolley- whose previous novel credits feature both Guard (Catachan Devil) and Orks (Da Freebooterz Code)- takes a gamble with a smash-'em-together plot device that pays off terrifically (modest spoiler ahead).

The story introduces us to the Ninth Company of the Steel Legion's 909th, on patrol in the wastes. In what must surely be amongst the worst bit of luck in the whole book, they come across a warp anomaly that turns into a rift, spitting horrors out from the empyrean. The Guardsmen are pushed to their limit as a towering warp daemon steps through, and if that's not enough they suddenly hear the revving of Ork engines behind them on intercept.

Hammer, meet anvil.

But then right there, Woolley completely turns the tale on its axis by switching to an Ork point of view. The story continues through the eyes of mekboy Wazdakka Gutsmek and his Speed Freeks, tearing into the mayhem in search of a proppa scrap- which is exactly what they've just found.

What makes Woolley's switch here particularly ambitious is that it isn't just a perspective change, it's a tonal one. The Steel Legion's lead-in is every bit as gritty as you'd expect, which makes the baton hand-off to the comparatively sillier Orks quite the contrast. But it works perfectly, and when Woolley hands it back to the Imperium the story's denouement is suitably grim.

Crimson Fists Vanguard Veterans - Credit: Keewa

Fighting Blind, by R. S. Wilt

Seven stories in and I'm feeling a bit like a cheerleader for Games Workshop here, almost hoping for a disappointing story so I can demonstrate some objectivity here with a critical review. Unfortunately, it ain't gonna be for R. S. Wilt's Fighting Blind.

Wilt gives us a story of a valiant stand by a group of Crimson Fists against overwhelming odds (Orks, of course). The title has a double meaning, describing both the Astartes cut off from their brothers as well as the disfigurement of the main POV character, Veteran Brother Néstor.

Néstor, blinded in the historic battle of Rynn's World, chooses to wear a metal plate over his eyes rather than have them augmetically replaced as a penance. Still able to see through the helm of his Terminator armor, when the ork warboss strikes the helm from his head Néstor must find a way to battle on if he's to save his brothers from death.

A solid action-focused entry, I did note that this one broke theme by taking place on a planet other than Armageddon. One from the slush pile, perhaps, or a possible precursor short story for an upcoming novel?

Imperial Guard Chimera. Credit: Charlie Brassley

Ash and Steel, by Richard Swan

Another Steel Legion-in-the-wastes tale, this one sees Chimeras trying to chase down and eliminate a gang of Speed Freeks. While the plot and story here are perfectly solid, Richard Swan's strengths are especially evident in a couple of places. First, his dialogue really pops, sounding true-to-life whether it's heated exchanges under fire or a pack of soldiers shit-talking each other standing around their Chimera waiting for orders.

Second, the dynamic he's written in between the squad's Captain and Lieutenant layers in an element of the tragic beyond just being generic officers, helping drive the story towards its end and giving it a conclusion that, like other stories in this anthology, has a bit more emotional depth than I was expecting.

Credit: Alfredo Ramirez

Ark of Salvation, by Robbie MacNiven

The book's sole void-set story, Ark of Salvation sees a tug-of-war boarding action between Orks and Ultramarines over an Imperial transport, the Ark of Salvation. While on first blush it's an Astartes-versus-xenos story, the real conflict at the heart of it is the interpretation of the Codex Astartes.

Twins Kastor and Polixis- Chaplain and Apothecary respectively (and who featured in Robbie MacNiven's 2018 novel Blood of Iax)- face a moral dilemma when ordered to secure the transport from the boarding Orks. Following orders as instructed would see them secure the bridge- and the vessel- leaving the invading xenos to burn through the mortal crew.

Attempting to save them, however, not only means contravening orders, but doing so on their own with no guarantee of success.

While MacNiven is no stranger to writing gripping Astartes action (Oaths of Damnation, Outer Dark), it's again the characters he writes that elevate this entry. Like Fighting Blind, though, this one's a thematic mis-fit given its setting (Ikara IX) is not connected to Armageddon.

Credit: Dan "SexCannon" Boyd

Home Front, by Rhuairidh James

The collection's penultimate story is my pick for the anthology's Best in Show. Again we're treated to a tale of surprising emotional resonance in this story of the life of a young gang member struggling to get by in his war-torn hive on Armageddon. First as leader of a crew of young toughs, then an aspiring solider who doesn't make the cut, and finally as a nearly used-up factory drudge. It's a hell of a journey with no small measure of tragedy, and a reminder of how dreams can be derailed by unexpected turns of fortune.

I'll leave it there as this is one best experienced cold, except to say that Rhuairidh James absolutely stuck the landing. An extraordinary tale, and the one- were I the editor- that would have closed out the anthology the same way I'd have hauntingly closed out a mixtape years ago with something from The Smiths.

Space Wolves - Credit: keewa

Seasons of Woe, by Marc Collins

Instead, last in line is Marc Collins with his newest offering featuring the Space Wolves. While I get a little annoyed at how overexposed his short The Price of Morkai has been, Collins has been a terrific voice for the more poetic side of the 6th. His Krakenblood impressed me with its lush writing (review), and more of that is on offer here for Seasons of Woe.

Here Collins tells the tale of Astartes on Armageddon and the unlikely fast friendship that forms between one of the Space Wolves and a Salamander. Despite their vastly different backgrounds, they come to realize that the essence of what they both believe- that people (as in, regular humans) are to be valued and protected- makes them brothers.

Later on patrol, the Space Wolf hears of his friend coming under attack from Orks, and their bond brings them together one last time.

Final Thoughts

Not since Blood of the Imperium (review) has an anthology so impressed me. Being a softcover-only release priced at USD $18 the bar was admittedly lower than the $30 World Ablaze, but I was also delighted to see this one offered in hardcover. Of course, I want every book offered in hardcover, so there's that.

All the same, this one's filled with quality from cover to cover. With the range of stories on offer mileage will always vary, but World Ablaze hit the trifecta of theme, writer selection, and story lineup.

The downside to being a hardcover offering is availability. This one sold out online quickly on preorder day and hasn't (to my knowledge) been made available since. But if you're able to find one at acceptable cost, this collection is well worth it.

Footnote

  1. In an AMA on Reddit, Swan noted that he had one in the pipeline with an expected release being Summer 2026.
