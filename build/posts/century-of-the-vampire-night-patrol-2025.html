Century of the Vampire: Night Patrol (2025)

May 22nd, 2026 via Goonhammer
Welcome to the Century of the Vampire, an ongoing weekly feature where Tabletop Battles managing editor Jonathan Bernhardt watches some piece of vampire media, probably a movie but maybe eventually television will get a spot in here too, and talks about it at some length in the context of both its own value as a piece of art and as a representation of the weird undead guys that dominate western pop culture who aren’t (usually) zombies.

Last time, Bernhardt reviewed the 1992 Fran Rubel Kuzai movie Buffy the Vampire Slayer. Today, he looks at the 2025 Ryan Prows film, Night Patrol. This article will contain spoilers.



Night Patrol: It’s not Night Watch (2004), and it’s not Night Shift (2023). I’m not sure if it’s a better movie than Night Watch -- I suspect it is, based on what I’ve seen secondhand of that film -- but it is definitely better than Night Shift, which I watched a good half-hour of before going, “You know, CM Punk should have shown up by now,” and checking if I had the right movie. I did not! For the record, Night Shift starts out as a perfectly acceptable spooky motel horror tale that collapses in a poorly-done and lazy third act twist; it would have been much better if Phoebe Tonkin was playing a vampire. She was not, nor was anyone else in the film, so it is beyond our purview here.

Night Patrol is a movie about vampire cops from the LAPD persecuting and trying to kill a bunch of poor Black Angelinos, only to be saved by Black radical politics. It’s both more and less didactic than that sounds; I’m still not quite sure how much I feel like the movie is making fun of its own premise, and especially the Black radical politics involved, but those sure are a bunch of white Nazi on-duty police officers who love killing Black people, and they sure do all die by the time the credits hit. The director Prows has done one well-received short film of note (Narcocorrido, 2011), one less-well received short film of note (he did the Terror short in 2021’s V/H/S 94; some of the people I’ve talked to liked it, some didn’t), and this, which is the only thing I’ve seen by him. This is, aggressively, a short form director’s first attempt at going long, and a bunch of it doesn’t really land. I still had fun with it! You get a lot of runway with me by killing a bunch of racist vampire cops!



 

We’re gonna mostly discuss the characters, because the plot is pretty simple: The cops are gonna attack the block at night and try to kill everyone, and the block is gonna fight back; everyone dies except our hero Wazi (RJ Cyler), who takes the fight back to the vampire boss cop in a pretty hokey fashion involving like, a suit of magical tribal armor. I could do without that last bit; AR platform weapons firing garlic 5.56 rounds are more my speed if you’re gonna do magic modern-day Excaliburs, and that’s the part where maybe the movie’s politics cross the line into self-parody. There’s some stuff in the beginning that moves the characters around into place, but the vast majority of Night Patrol’s runtime takes place during the siege. In this fashion it’s very close to being a true representative of the One Crazy Night genre of film, but no cigar; too much set up in the day time.

Wazi feels clearly like our lead character from moment one; he’s the guy in the framing device bookending the film, he’s the guy in the passenger’s seat in the first scene where Justin Long’s cop character “Ethan Hawkins” shoots a woman in the back of the head during a traffic stop to blood in to the vampire cop gang; the film revolves around him and we’ll be with him pretty much every step of the way. Now, it does try to convince you that the guy named after the actor from Training Day and who spends the next ten-ish minutes doing stuff from the beginning of End of Watch with Wazi’s cop brother, Xavier (Jermaine Fowler), is gonna actually be the lead; both Long and Fowler get top billing in the movie and Justin Long has been a staple of the mid-level horror feature for well over a decade now -- Tusk came out 12 years ago, if you want to feel old.



It has to be said: The only funnier casting choice for a cop in this movie than professional wrestler and aspiring actor for many years, CM Punk (here credited as “Phil Brooks”), is Justin Long. The reason Punk is a funny choice is because despite joke-complaining in press afterwards about being pidgeonholed into cop roles recently (history has shown no complaint from CM Punk should be taken entirely as a joke), he is extremely good at it here; he’s able to handle the smug confidence and grinning self-assurance of the vampire third-in-command quite well, hazing the rookies and being a good soldier to his betters. Long is funny for the opposite reason: It would be absolutely impossible to buy Justin Long as a rookie LAPD officer, or even a young but seasoned LAPD officer, at any point in his career, but Justin Long is forty-seven years old. He’s the same age as Punk, who is at least given the dignity of playing a grizzled department veteran! He’s probably the closest thing this movie has to a genuine draw -- I love Jermaine Fowler, I understand the Punker sold out some buildings in his day, but Long’s a bigger name than both if you’re trying to sell a mid-brow streaming horror feature -- so I understand the choice. It’s just completely ridiculous, is the thing.

Long is a fakeout protagonist, in the end; so is Xavier, Wazi’s cop brother who is also positioned like the lead character for an act of the film as he returns to the block and has it out with former friends and his estranged mother over their criminality and radical politics. These are personified in the drug dealer Bornelius (Freddie Gibbs) and the matriarch of the block, Ayanda (Nicki Micheaux); they’re on different sides of the Blood-Crip divide, but put aside their differences temporarily in the second act to fight the racist vampire cops. Ayanda’s been a radical since the Black Panther days, though her particular brand goes farther beyond those mundane class and race-based analyses into the supernatural -- Bornelius (who gets the vast majority of the film's fun lines) specifically disdains the stuff she’s been pamphleting about for years as “Hotep shit,” which is a whole can of worms we won’t get into here, but the upshot is that white LA cops are literally vampires, and she’s been trying to warn everyone for years. Ayanda, of course, is right.



Justin Long’s Ethan gets turned into a vampire to try to take the vampire cops down from the inside, but in the end succumbs and joins them, because you can’t destroy an institution from within that institution; the whole thing about the master’s house and the master’s tools, right? Well, that’s the story I wish they’d told. They get like two-thirds of the way there, but instead of him just actively choosing to embrace the Night Patrol or passively getting pressured and bullied into compliance by his brethren, Ethan gets…permanently possessed by his vampire elder father and turned into a berserk mindless demon monster after giving some evil speeches? Hard to do much with that, thematically. Poor officer Xavier is almost entirely a red herring, dying just past the hour mark as Ethan finally turns into the third act villain for good. This is the point where the movie stops being interesting on any level above the spectacle itself; we do get a funny bit where possessed vampire Ethan parades CM Punk’s decapitated head aloft to rile up his men following the officer’s death at Wazi and company’s hands earlier in the film, and it’s very clearly a display bust they had done up by a professional artist instead of a prop trying to replicate the head of a decapitated vampire.

The movie did poorly with critics; on the one hand, yes, I can see why. Most of the jokes are only good enough to land once and the plotting is far too concerned with making Xavier and Ethan’s departures into narrative shocks, considering the film opens with a short sequence of Wazi in the interrogation room where he winds up at the end of the film as the framing device, telling you who the actual main character is. I don’t think I’ll be rewatching it. On the other hand, I’ve watched so much worse and less interesting dreck for this column. If I had watched a movie of this quality for Century of the Vampire every week, it would probably have gone a lot shorter, but I’d have a lot less to complain about. This is perfectly serviceable. The vampire cops’ steel mouthpiece fangs are pretty dope, though they also invite one to think about a better movie where the cops still drank blood, but were just normal LAPD officers. There’s a bit too much regeneration and lower-jaw unhinging over the course of the runtime to sustain that reading here.
