Tabletop Battles Reviews: The Cats of Mont Saint-Michel

October 6th, 2026 via Goonhammer

If you've ever said to yourself: "what I really want is a game set in the 17th century where we get to be swashbuckling musketeers, except that we're all cats," then have I got a game for you.

The Cats of Mont Saint-Michel is the latest solo/cooperative campaign game from Raybox Games, creators of Escape from Stalingrad Z and Gates of Niflheim. Like their other titles, this one uses Raybox's Paths Unknown game system to present a multi-chapter branching campaign. An average game takes 30-45 minutes to play, and the result of each game determines which scenario gets played next. Additionally, characters gain loot and experience in the form of new in-game abilities, giving them more options to use in later games.

Image credit: Raybox Games

That's all well and good, but the real draw of The Cats of Mont Saint-Michel is, of course, the cats. When starting a new campaign, players must make the impossible choice from among such utterly charming feline adventurers as Leon, a chartreuse musketeer; Adara, a calico wizard; Sekhmet, an Abyssinian cleric; or Nera, a Siamese ninja. And lest you think it's all cats, we also have Friedrich, a dachshund soldier, and my personal favorite: Tiers-Etat, a stack of three mice who function as one character.

An assortment of playable characters. Image credit: Jefferson Powers

How Does It Play?

The campaign that unfolds in The Cats of Mont Saint-Michel is designed for exactly four heroes, which means the game is great for 1, 2 or 4 players, and okay for 3 if they can decide how to control the leftover hero. At heart it's a map-based tactical movement game that makes use of a spiral notebook to provide a wide variety of different boards to play on. Players move their heroes around the board, fighting (or avoiding) enemies and picking up objectives as determined by the specific scenario.

Movement is grid based, but with an interesting wrinkle: the play area is divided into 2-inch squares, with each of those further divided into quarters, so you have a series of large squares containing four smaller squares. A normal move is either a prowl, which moves your character into any adjacent small square, or a stride, which moves your character anywhere in an adjacent big square, potentially up to 3 squares away. Additionally, a character can pounce: a double-move that uses up more actions but places them anywhere up to 2 big squares away, and gives them a free attack. Limitations such as adjacent enemies and terrain are built into this big square-small-square structure, making for an interesting movement system that (once you get your head around it) is elegant, consistent, versatile, and reflects the way cats tend to move.

One of the game's many maps. Image credit: Jefferson Powers

Combat is resolved by rolling one or two (depending on the weapon) 12-sided dice, adding and subtracting modifiers for things like skills and terrain, and then comparing the result to the weapon's Stun and Wound ratings. If the result of the roll is at least equal to the weapon's stun value, the target is stunned (they miss a turn), if it reaches or exceeds the wound value, the target is also wounded, with most enemies being able to take 2 or 3 wounds before expiring.

New enemies will spawn at various points throughout the game, ranging from weak but plentiful roaches to crossbow wielding rats to fearsome boss villains like the amusingly named Cardinal Ratilieu.

A few of the game's fearsome villains. Image credit: Jefferson Powers

Scenario objectives range from collecting objective markers to neutralizing specific enemies. Some of them involve escaping off a board edge, while others require the heroes to hold out for a set number of turns. The scenarios generally follow a linear path from one to the next, with the players' accomplishments sometimes allowing for the next scenario to be set up differently or even skipped altogether.

In between each scenario, players get to visit la Taverne, an event card deck that can award things like extra healing, additional weapons and equipment, and sometimes even little side-quest scenarios like a tavern brawl or a cockroach race. The tavern is also where the heroes can heal damage, which is otherwise carried over from game to game, and also spend all the treasure they (hopefully) found to buy new gear and more importantly, gain skills.

Each character has their own set of nine skills, one each of levels one, two and three. A character has to gain all three level 1 skills before moving on to level 2, and so on. These tend to add things like extra attacks, modifiers, and movement abilities -- essential when facing the more difficult later scenarios.

Who Is It For?

At the end of the day, The Cats of Mont Saint-Michel is a basic move-and-fight tactical game. The big-square-small-square grid system is interesting, but apart from that the game gets by on its charm. And that's absolutely fine. These types of games are generally only as good as their settings and characters, and honestly, if you're going to enjoy them you really want the rules to get out of the way so you can have fun experiencing the game's world. And this game does that, mostly.

Unfortunately, a lot of the online discussion about The Cats of Mont Saint-Michel has focused on the large amount of errors and errata. Which is fair -- there are a lot of errors and errata, and you will definitely want to have the errata sheet from BoardGameGeek handy while you're playing. Is it enough to spoil enjoyment of the game? I don't think so, as long as you're not going to get too hung up on having every rule flawlessly spelled out. And honestly, this game is too simple, and too silly, to do that anyway. If you're willing to make an occasional common-sense judgement about an unclear rule, The Cats of Mont Saint-Michel is a perfectly entertaining light adventure game.

To Upgrade or Not to Upgrade?

Deluxe vs core box components. Image credit: Jefferson Powers

The Cats of Mont Saint-Michel comes in a basic and a deluxe edition. The deluxe version adds fancier versions of the game's existing components: plastic miniatures instead of cardboard standees, two-layer character boards, and wooden counters to replace the cardboard ones, but no additional game content. None of it is essential, and while I do plan on painting the miniatures at some point, I've been enjoying the game just fine using the standees. In all honesty, I don't think the deluxe version justifies the added cost, so unless you really want the miniatures, the core set is all you should need to enjoy the game.
