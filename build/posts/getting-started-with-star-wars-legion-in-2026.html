Getting Started with Star Wars: Legion in 2026

June 23rd, 2026 via Goonhammer

A long, long time ago in a galaxy far, far away…

Hello there! I’m Matt, and if this article looks familiar, it is! There have been a ton of changes to Star Wars Legion since the last time this article was updated, to the point where it’s practically a new game. There’s never been a better time to jump into one of the most popular sci-fi universes ever created, so let’s talk about how to do that.

Thank you to Asmodee for sponsoring a series of articles on Star Wars: Legion.

What is Star Wars: Legion?

Star Wars: Legion, or Legion for short, is a skirmish war game originally produced by Fantasy Flight games. As of 2021 production has moved to Atomic Mass Games who produces that other miniatures game owned by Disney. Like Crisis Protocol, the main draw to Legion is that the iconic characters you see in the movies are a powerful presence on the battlefield and all have very unique toolkits. Which heroes you bring can drastically change your list’s synergy and what units gel best with it. Luke Skywalker is very much a unit that wants to go in swinging into the opponent’s lines, while someone like Count Dooku wants his opponents to come to him and out duel them.

In Legion you create an army based on one of the several factions we see in the movies and television: The Rebel Alliance, The Galactic Empire, The Galactic Republic, the Confederacy of Independent Systems, the Shadow Collective, and the Mandalorian Clans. Don’t worry if some of those don’t sound familiar, we’ll cover them all in a little bit.

B1 Battle Droids. Roger roger. Credit: Rich Nutter

After picking a faction, you’ll build a list of units consisting of characters, soldiers and vehicles from the Star Wars films and Expanded Universe. These are generally people on foot or in ground vehicles, but AMG has recently announced that they’re adding spaceships to the game so you’ll be able to use things like X-Wings as well. Finally, you must build a deck of 7 “Command Cards” that offer powerful once per turn abilities. These can range from generic cards anyone can use to signature abilities unique to each commander.

Command cards come in three varieties:

  • Generics, which can be used by everyone but lack any benefits other than generating orders.
  • Faction-specific, which offer minor bonuses anyone in a given faction can use, but usually only affect specific units.
  • And finally we have the Character-specific command cards which can only be taken if the given character is in your list. These are typically the most powerful cards and usually ensure that the character they belong to will have an order issued to them, though once the character they belong to has been removed from the game they can no longer be played.

The final step of the list building phase allows you to select which Objective, Secondary Objective, and Advantage cards you’d prefer and place them in your Battle Deck. Picking these is an art unto itself, and we have a whole article talking about the nuances involved in selecting them, but for now but the general idea is that you look at the army you’re playing and pick things that you think would be easy to accomplish, and advantages tailored to your play style and the things you’d like to do with your army.

The Battle Deck ties into the most important part of pregame setup, where both players use their decks in order to construct the mission that will be played in that game. To begin, there’s a roll-off to determine which player is the Blue Player. Starting with the Blue Player, each player selects either their Objective or Secondary Objective deck and flips over the top card. Their opponent does the same with the deck that the first player didn’t choose, then each player draws and places the top card of their Advantage deck on their side of the score tracker.

Legion Score Tracker and Battle Deck. Image courtesy Asmodee and Atomic Mass Games

Once these are placed, each player gets two chances to modify what has been revealed, taking alternating turns. There’s a lot you can do here, but with each turn a player can change any of the revealed cards, replacing it with the top card of your deck of the same type, no matter who revealed them first. Your opponent reveals a mission card you hate? Replace it with something from your deck. If you like the way the setup looks, you can choose to pass and not change anything. You can also decide to become the Blue Player during this phase as one of your choices, and that choice stays valid for the rest of the game.

Each mission comes with its own map card that shows where the deployment zones for each player are and where to place any objective tokens that the mission requires. Each player then deploys any units with the Prepared Positions ability that they have, starting with the Blue Player. All other units start off the battlefield and have to use their first action of the game to deploy into their player’s deployment zone.

From there, each turn consists of both players playing their command cards, issuing orders based on what their command card states. The player who selects the card with fewer “pips” on it goes first, rolling dice as a tiebreaker. Command cards with a larger number of pips generally have more powerful effects, introducing some tension between trying to go first and buffing your units.

Once the turn starts, the game’s “Fog of War” mechanic starts to come into play. To represent the general chaos of the battlefield, certain units near the commander are issued orders, and a token is placed next to them. The rest of the tokens representing your army are placed face down in a stack at random or shuffled in a bag called the Order Pool. On a player’s turn they may select a unit with an order issued to it or draw one randomly from their order pool for the round. That unit activates, then play passes to the other player. A round ends once every unit has activated, and the game continues until either a player has 12 Victory Points and the lead over the other player, or until five rounds have been played. Once turn five has ended the winner is chosen from whoever has the most victory points based on the objectives, with a tie breaker on who destroyed the most of their opponent’s forces.

TX-130 Saber Tank assigned to the 501st Legion. Credit: Contemptor Kevin Stillman

Why should I even play this?

To keep it simple: It’s Star Wars. It’s in the title of the game and it oozes everywhere in the feeling of play. If you were to take a cursory glance at the game you can tell that the people behind this game have a strong passion for the series. You see a model like Obi-Wan in his signature Soresu pose while in the same outfit from the Clone Wars. If you know what those words mean you will identify with its flavor. Many of the franchises’ most quotable lines make it into the game as abilities you can use.

Those that aren’t Star Wars nerds will discover a deep and thoughtful game with loads of strategy. Something as simple as how many points you build your list with can dictate the flow of the game. Do you kit out your units with upgrades to max out their potential? Do you build around a few key characters and use them to control the battlefield? Maybe you just spam the board with loads of cheap units and out activate your opponent to get cheap shots in. The strategy is yours to come up with.

The players building the mission format together is one of the main things about Legion that separates it from other miniature games, which is why we spent so much time explaining it earlier. It’s difficult to overstate how much it makes each game interesting and forces you to think on your feet before a unit even hits the table.

Is it Possible to Learn this Game?

Indeed, and you don’t have to go to any opera houses with shady Chancellors to do so. All the rules can be found for free on the official Legion website.

Choosing a Side

So back to those six factions: we have the Rebels, Empire, Republic, CIS, Shadow Collective, and the Mandalorian Clans.While there is some degree of flexibility, each faction has certain strengths and weaknesses as a whole, so even when different factions have the same list archetype, big heroes backed up with shooting units for example, they’ll still play differently on the table. The balance is pretty solid right now and any faction can win against any other. You will rarely have to worry about coming into a game you absolutely cannot win, even if it might be a bit of an uphill battle.

Galactic Empire Stormtroopers. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

Galactic Empire

With troops clad in armor and having a wide variety of long range weapons, the Empire is a very easy to play gunline faction. Most imperials have access to weapons that allow them to attack their opponents at range four or have the “Suppressive” keyword which allows you to potentially reduce the number of actions a unit can take. With these abilities, Empire troops have ways to limit their opponent’s actions or cause them to panic off the board. Combined with commanders who can give out Aim tokens or extra actions (for a cost), you have a faction that wants to position itself to maximize their offensive potential even if they’re typically using the worst dice for their attacks. To paraphrase a certain famous Grand Moff: “Fear will keep the local systems in line.” 

Play if:

  • You like durable troops who can hold out against heavy fire.
  • You like to dismantle your opponent with fear and suppression
  • You want an army that can excel without too many complicated mechanics
A horde of Rebel Troops and Commandos. Credit: Rich Nutter

Rebel Alliance

While the Empire shoots like Stormtroopers but have strong armor, Rebels are the opposite where their offense is fairly good in exchange for having bad defense. To balance out that poor defense, many Rebel troops have access to Dodge tokens which can negate hits, or abilities that allow them to reroll or gain more defense dice to help block hits. This means they can’t take a blaster to the face like the Empire or Republic but they have sneaky ways to get around it. The real strength in the Rebel faction is their characters and special units. Their characters are impactful to the game with powerful threats like Luke Skywalker, Cassian Andor, and Ahsoka or support characters like Leia Organa or Chewbacca backing them up. You can use fast units like Tauntauns or Snowspeeders to get up the board quickly, or focus hard on objectives with Commandos who can deploy with extra movement to quickly step onto an objective or have the mobility to go after your opponent’s objectives while being extremely resilient. Remember that you have hope. Rebellions are built on hope.

Play if:

  • You like finesse armies with hit and run tactics
  • You want a variety of playstyles and compositions
  • You like Hero-centered armies
Phase Two Clone Troopers Credit: LunarNight

Grand Army of the Republic

With warriors cloned from the most feared bounty hunter in the galaxy and led into battle by members of the Jedi Order, the Republic is seen as the “Elite” Faction in Legion. Using their faction mechanics, their basic troops can easily match up against the other factions’ superior units. The unique mechanic the Republic brings into the game is the ability to share the game’s various beneficial tokens among the clones, letting you use those key buffs where they’re needed at any time. Your force may be fewer, but with your brothers at your side, you’ll always have your opponents outnumbered. Though a Jedi General does help.

Play if:

  • You really like lightsabers.
  • You enjoy a strong gunline
  • You enjoy coordinating and managing your army heavily to victory.
Star Wars Legion Separatist Crab Droids - Credit Bair

Confederacy of Independent Systems

Quantity has its own quality and the droid army of the Separatist is packed with loads of battle droids to swarm your opponents. On paper the basic B1 battle can be seen as the worst unit in the game as they shoot like Stormtroopers and take hits like Rebel Troopers, but they are key to the separatist strategy. While other factions have limited control over their army due the game’s “Fog of War'' mechanic, with proper positioning of your troopers you can issue orders to most if not all of your army. Just be mindful of their faction drawback, AI. If a droid unit is not issued an order, they can only perform prescribed actions as their first action. The CIS is also home to one of the best tanks in the game with the AAT so if you’re an Imperial Guard player both your strats are represented here.

Play if:

  • You enjoy “horde” or “swarm” armies
  • If you want to ignore several rules
  • You aspire to be a tactical genius with full control over their forces
Shadow Collective Maul - Credit Bair

Shadow Collective

If you’re into bounty hunters, scoundrels, and thugs, the Shadow Collective is the faction for you. The game’s first “mercenary” faction, most of the named, recognizable characters of the SC are unique to their force while the troopers can be used in a number of different armies. To support this, none of their units really outshine the other factions’ basic troops in flexibility, but they make up for it by being very powerful in specific roles. The Black Sun Enforcers, for example, are incredible at destroying any unit they encounter in close range, but can’t contribute much if they get caught out at range. If you like the idea of exploring the criminal side of the Star Wars universe, or want to start a second army to supplement your first, check them out.

Play if:

  • You enjoy Darth Maul’s story arc after The Phantom Menace
  • You want the challenge of using specialized units to their full potential
  • You like high-speed, high-aggression lists with lots of vehicles

Mandalorians! Credit: Colin (Legalized Mischief)

Mandalorian Clans

The game’s newest faction, this is where you go if you can’t stop rewatching The Mandalorian over and over again.The Mandalorians have some of the more complex army-building rules in the game.You can choose from four different sub-factions in the army, each representing one of the distinct clans of Mandalorians we’ve seen in the fiction. You can have a force representing Clan Wren from Star Wars Rebels, or one representing The Amorer’s Covert Mandalorians from the Mandalorian show, and your units get special abilities to match the flavor of each clan. If you really want to be unique, you can build a force that mixes and matches two special abilities from a list of five, letting you play Mandalorians exactly how you want them. Many of the units in this faction also have the Mercenary keyword, letting you use them in other armies, so you can use them as a way to expand to those other factions if you want to double dip on a unit and save a little money.

Play if:

  • You enjoy Mandalorians
  • You want to craft an army’s rules to match your playstyle
  • You like synergistic, themed lists

“This is Where the Fun Begins.”

So you’ve taken a look at the models and found the faction that most fits your play style or just looks the coolest. The first way to start is with one of the four faction starter sets, each of which gives you a full 600 point army usable in the smaller Recon version of the game.  In addition to all the units you will also be getting a set of movement tools, range tools, dice and a generous amount of the game’s tokens that you’ll need for the game. All this is at an extremely good price point and with many factions, buying a second starter set is the most cost-effective way to go up to a full 1000 point army.

The second way to go is with a Battleforce box. For the four initial factions, these are a heavily themed box of models that will get you a full 600 point recon army. They’re not the most flexible lists, but you get a lot of models for your dollar. They’re so heavily themed that you probably don’t want to buy two unless you’re really, really into whatever theme the box represents. This is also the only way to start Shadow Collective or Mandalorian Clans, as they don’t have dedicated Starter Sets, and they’re the exception to the rule about buying more than one Battleforce Box.. If you go this way, you’ll need to also pick up the Essentials kit and a set or two of dice to have everything you need to play.

Either way you go, we would suggest picking up a 3×3 gaming mat and downloading the free Recon rule set from the Legion website. Recon can best be described as the “diet” version of the game as it uses smaller armies and a smaller battlefield. It’s a great place to learn the mechanics of the game from activation control to how to best utilize certain units. It is very beginner friendly and there are a set of deployment, mission, and condition cards that are balanced around the smaller point total and table. The nice thing about these battle cards is that they are very similar to their standard format counterparts so you can figure out which cards would be best for your playstyle when you get a chance at larger games.

Heroes of the Republic Credit:LunarNight

“Do or do not, there is no try.”

Legion is still a growing game and Atomic Mass Games has just recently overhauled most of the game, making a de-facto second edition of the game even if they don’t want to officially call it that. The oldest models have been replaced with new, hard plastic versions that look incredible and changes all over the ruleset have left Legion in the best state the game has ever been in. There’s never been a better time to play Legion than right now, and the future is only looking brighter.

So go down to your local game store or use their online store if they have one, pick up some models and get playing.
