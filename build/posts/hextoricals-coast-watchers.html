Hextoricals: Coast Watchers

October 22nd, 2026 via Goonhammer

The Australian coast watchers played a crucial role in the Pacific Theater, providing intel within the campaigns for the Solomons and New Guinea, at great personal peril: Many of these coast watchers were captured and executed by the Japanese as spies, but their sacrifice allowed the Allies to have ample warning of Japanese build up and operations, sighting both airplanes and ships and signaling their approach. Initially they’d been set up as intel operation within the various islands of the Pacific Ocean, but the blistering fast pace of the Japanese advance meant that they quickly found themselves behind enemy lines.

Will you take the role of the coast watchers and provide crucial intel for the Allied fleets, or will you command the Japanese patrols that are hunting them down?

Coast Watchers is the first game in GMT’s new Recon Series and simulates the struggles of the Australian coast watchers during the invasions (and eventual recapture) of the Solomons, Guadalcanal and New Guinea. Designed by Volko Ruhnke, of COIN fame, the game showcases his love for asymmetrical conflicts and hidden information, and there are clear inspirations from both Labyrinth and the COIN games interwoven within this design.

The main conceit of the game is one of bluffs, double bluffs and a cat-and-mouse game of trying to keep your coast watchers hidden on one side, and trying to find them and capture them on the other. Both sides have information that is hidden from the other, so neither has a full picture of what exactly is going on, or what their opponent intends to do. Coast Watchers is also singularly focused on the gathering of intel and information, and a scenario ends as soon as the Japanese run their military operations: There is no capturing terrain in this game, as those are the concerns of other forces and other games.

Unboxing and Materials

As expected from GMT, this is a beautifully produced game: The board especially is notable, as it has recesses for the Allied pieces (which are the green wooden blocks). This isn’t just a way to mark where they should be placed, but also makes them less likely to be knocked away or flipped: Since this is a hidden information game, a moment of clumsiness that reveals a piece might spoil a particular playthrough.

Everything inside the box, apart from the player aids, rulebook and playbook. Credits: Riccardo Fabris

The green wooden blocks are similarly of a high standard, with embossed, illustrated sides that represent empty stations, or ones inhabited by coast watchers, refugees, downed airmen or even guerillas. The Japanese forces are mostly represented by counters: There are patrol markers, with Army patrols on one side and Police patrols on the other (the latter being more effective since the Japanese are working in conjunction with local forces). There are also counters marking Japanese-occupied bases or sites, which, on the reverse either show a quiet location or a build up of some kind, the latter being required in order for the Japanese player to run their operations. The Allies, once they have found these build-ups, have their own report markers to make note of what they found.

Other components include different types of cards: Mission and Asset cards for both sides, as well as two solitaire decks that allow the game to be played alone either on the Allied side or the Japanese side. Along with two player aids, there is a solitaire aid, and various scenario sheets, marked from A to H, which give instructions on how to set up the game and which special rules each scenario has: The scenarios range from the very start of the Japanese invasion to the eventual retaking of Rabaul, the main Japanese base in the area.

Finally, there are two boards for the Delivery and Patrol chits (although you will have to provide your own cups), and a rulebook and playbook, with the latter outlining the intricate operations and missions that could occur in the scenarios of the game.

Gameplay

As befits the theme, Coast Watchers is a game about finding information: The main aim of the Allied players is locating the Japanese build up as a prelude to their operations, and placing their coast watchers in the path of these eventual operations in order to give ample warning to the Allied bases that are being targeted. On the other hand, the Japanese player is on the hunt for the coast watchers, trying to locate and capture them, both to prevent them from spotting the build up as well as clearing a path for their invasion fleets or air raids.

Since both sides have hidden information about their forces, many actions within the game force one side or the other to close their eyes while their opponent changes the state of the board: In many situations, you might not even know the location of where your opponent made their move, which is something quite unique, at least in the world of board wargames. Even the setup of a simple scenario requires the Japanese player to close their eyes, as the Allied player distributes their initial allotment of coast watchers at various locations on the board: Many of their blocks will be palm leaves, signifying an empty station, but some of them will hide coast watchers, and often times, depending on the scenario, either refugees or downed airmen that need to be rescued.

The coast watchers are on the back foot as several of their stations are revealed. Credits: Riccardo Fabris

The different scenarios, along with having different set up instructions, can also have a maximum of three or five turns: The end of a particular game of Coast Watchers is in the hands of the Japanese player, since they get to decide when they run their operations, which means that even a five turn scenario can potentially earlier, even on turn two on occasion.

The flow of a turn starts with the Japanese preparations phase, where they can either gain new assets (which provide abilities that allow them to hunt down the coast watchers more effectively), they can ready patrols for use during the turn, or they can secretly add build ups to their bases and sites: The latter action is dictated by the kind of missions that they drew at the start of the scenario, with the pool of potential missions being set by the scenario instructions.

Once they have finished their preparations, it is the time for the Allied activities phase. They get a total of five actions that they can perform: Delivery, operate, evade and train. Although the Allied player is free to do as many of each type of action as they wish, they have to follow the order above; all deliveries must be completed before any operational action is performed, and so on. Delivery requires the use of assets, representing airplanes, small boats or even submarines, and can be used to either send supplies to your coast watchers, rescue refugees and downed airmen or insert new coast watchers that have been previously trained: Deliveries in Japanese-held areas are risky, however, and run the danger of being spotted or even prevented outright. This is done by placing a specific number of chits within the Delivery cup and then drawing two of them; drawing one or more “spotted” chits will force you to reveal your forces and allow any Japanese patrols to attempt to capture them.

Initial setup for one of the many scenarios. Credits: Riccardo Fabris

Operate actions allow the coast watchers to either observe build ups if they successfully avoid Japanese patrols, or even strike out and ambush Japanese soldiers if they have guerillas and the required asset card. Evade actions allow your revealed coast watchers to become hidden again: If they are revealed, many actions are simply not possible, and being revealed makes it easier for the Japanese player to capture your pieces. Finally, train actions allow you to get more coast watchers ready for deployment in future turns.

Once the Allies have finished their activities phase, the Japanese’s countermeasures phase starts: They also get 5 actions which need to be done in a strict sequence; Recall, Search and Deploy. Recall allows them to return patrols on the map to their ready area (usually done when a station is deactivated due to the capture of a coast watcher). Search is the main way that the Japanese player can strike back at the coast watchers: This is where the Patrol Cup comes into play.

Unlike the Delivery Cup, the Patrol Cup is not reseeded each time that it’s used: Instead, the ratio of chits within it is updated throughout the game. It contains either Japanese patrols, or Allied evasion chits, and when a search is conducted in a location, the Japanese player draws a number of chits depending on what kind of patrol they have in that location (either army or police patrols): The more patrol chits you draw from the cup, the more likely you are to find the coast watchers, or even capture them outright. Crucially, a location can only be searched once per phase, so if the coast watchers are revealed (but not captured), it’s possible for the Allied player to hide them again before the Japanese player is able to capture them..

Another scenario set up and ready to play. Credits: Riccardo Fabris

The final action available to the Japanese player is Deploy: They can either place patrols from their ready area to the Patrol Cup, or place them on the board itself. It’s important to strike a balance between enough patrols on the board versus having patrols in the cup, since the latter makes it more likely that any individual search will be successful.

The countermeasures phase is followed by another Allied activities phase, which is exactly the same as the first. Once the allies have finished their second activities phase, the Japanese player has a choice: They can either immediately run their operations, which signals the end of the game (although this decision is forced on the last turn of a particular scenario), or they can perform an additional countermeasures phase.

If they do elect to run operations, they immediately reveal their build up and mission cards: Some of the missions will require them to set a path from the build ups to their targets, while other missions simply require the build up to be present in a specific location. Once they have set these paths, the Allied player reveals their remaining coast watchers, and then both sides total up the number of points they receive: The Japanese player receiving points for performing their operations and missions and capturing Allied pieces, and the Allied player getting points for accurately reporting the location of Japanese build up, for having coast watchers on the way of a particular operation, or for rescuing refugees and airmen. Once both sides have summed their points, whoever scored the most wins the scenario!

A zoom into the Bismarks and New Britain areas. Credits: Riccardo Fabris

The above might seem quite complex at first glance, but in actual play the game tends to be quite a smooth experience, although the final tallying of points can be somewhat of a chore, since there are many discrete sources of points on both sides, which can often lead to mistakes. The game does feel rather unique and the marriage of ideas and inspiration from COINs and hidden movement games makes the entire package quite novel and unexpected.

The game does feature a way to play both sides solitaire, which is welcome, and there was clearly a lot of effort involved in trying to make both sides playable, although it is clear that the design was intended as a two player experience first and foremost.

Historicity

Although I’ve read quite a few books centered around the Pacific Theater, the role of the coast watchers within the Solomons and New Guinea campaigns has always been somewhat of a footnote: There are plenty of mentions of them helping the war effort, and even outlines of the extreme levels of duress that they and the civilians that helped them went through during the war. But even then there is a distinct lack of detail in most books about the accounts and experiences of these men as they struggled to survive. The game does a lot to outline and explain the history of the coast watchers, especially within the playbook, and I think it should be complimented for this alone: The history of the coast watchers is a history worthy of being told and remembered.

On the other hand, the game doesn’t really reflect the experiences of the individual coast watchers, and puts you in the shoes of the people that either ran the program on the Allied side or coordinated the attempts to hunt them down on the Japanese side. This zoomed out perspective does create somewhat of a detachment from the troops on the ground, since you are looking at a much wider picture than the singular struggle to survive, and the abstraction required for the game to be at this particular scale does diminish, at least for me, the impact of the stories that the game is trying to tell.

Critiques & Quirks

Of all of the GMT games that I’ve reviewed so far, I think this is probably going to be the one I’m most critical of, since I think there are issues with the game that outright prevent me from giving a seal of approval to Coast Watchers.

One of the main issues with the game is that its core gameplay loop feels like you are playing a game of whack-a-mole on repeat, something that is not singular to this game but tended to plague the games in the COIN series as well, although I will argue that the issue is far worse in Coast Watchers than it is in any of the COIN games, even the ones that are strictly for two players only. Much like the insurgent factions in COIN games, Allied pieces in this game can either be revealed or hidden, with the latter making them much harder to remove and also allowing them to perform several actions. This can lead to situations in either series where one side reveals the enemy pieces, is unable to remove them once revealed, and the other side counters by hiding them again. Repeat ad infinitum.

COIN games somewhat alleviate this issue since usually there are more than 2 players at the table, and the turn order can be randomly decided based on the cards drawn. Sides also usually have a limited number of resources they can expend. Finally, special operations or newly acquired capabilities that are unique to each side can swing the fight decisively one way or the other. Finally, there are sometimes other obligations or actions that either side might want to take instead of simply finding or hiding their troops.

New Britain and New Guinea. Credits: Riccardo Fabris

Coast Watchers has none of those release valves. The primary aim of the Japanese player is to simply search and find the coast watchers: The entire countermeasures phase is centered around that. There are ways to stack the deck in your favour, by seeding the patrol cup or finding specific assets, but those options will eat up valuable actions either during the preparation or countermeasures phase: Instant capture is also not possible unless you have police patrols, which require either to be present in the starting set up, or as upgrades thanks to the capture of other coast watchers, or just randomly found as an asset draw.

Now, revealing coast watchers does force the Allied player to waste actions to Evade them, and might prevent them from delivering more supplies and coast watchers, but especially at the end of the game, the Allied player might gather enough points simply by the presence of their coast watchers in crucial areas, and thus won’t care if they are not able to do any actions.

This has led to games as the Japanese where, even though I was pressuring my opponent as much as I could, and I had stacked both the patrol cup and had police patrols in place, I wasn’t able to capture the coast watchers simply because I didn’t get the draws that I needed. The game can reach a state where bluffing is not really required any more, and the two players are mechanically just hoping that luck swings their way and they get the draws that they require to win against their opponent.

This issue is more acute within the more limited 3 turn scenarios, where the Japanese player simply does not have the time to stack the odds against the Allied player enough to pressure them into making risky supply deliveries. Although I did enjoy the 5 turn scenarios more, and there was more scope for double bluffs, the gameplay loop still didn’t really engage me as much as I hoped it would.

This is not to say that the game doesn’t have a deep level of strategy or analysis: The playbook features entire pages that outline the possible combinations of missions and operations that the Japanese can take for each individual scenario, and it’s clear that a lot of thought went into how each scenario is balanced. Despite this, the immediate loop of the game did not enthrall me.

I think the issues with this loop are even more pronounced within the solitaire version of the game. Although the solitaire decks for either side do an admirable job of trying to simulate the decision-making of an actual opponent, the solitaire game felt laborious, and lacked that distinct level of mind-games that made the base game at least somewhat enjoyable. It really brought to the forefront the issues with the loop as presented.

Finally, I do have issues with how the rulebook is written. I have a tendency to prefer games that have a very procedural and technical layout: The Coast Watchers rulebook is meant more as a play guide, and I had difficulties referencing particular rules due to its layout. This is not to say that the rulebook is obtuse or that the rules are unclear, however, and this criticism is more of a personal preference more than anything else.

Final Thoughts

Considering my criticisms listed above, I would be hard pressed to recommend this game. After playing through multiple scenarios, both against other players and solitaire, the core gameplay loop felt very similar between them and it wasn’t one that I really enjoyed going through. On the other hand, the game is very novel and is definitely attempting to push the boundaries of board wargames, and I think for someone that is interested in the Pacific Theater (and, especially, about a relatively little-known part of that conflict) and also likes hidden movement games like Fury of Dracula and other such board games, there is more than enough longevity within this game to whet their appetite. Unfortunately, from my own perspective, I cannot personally recommend such an expensive purchase.
