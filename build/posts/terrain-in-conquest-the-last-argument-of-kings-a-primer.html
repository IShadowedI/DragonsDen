Terrain in Conquest The Last Argument of Kings: A Primer

August 4th, 2026 via Goonhammer

There is one truism that holds across this great hobby of ours, and it’s this: There is no game without terrain. Terrain tells the story of the game, but it also serves an important role in creating friction for all players equally; opportunities to take advantage of as well as challenges to overcome.

But terrain plays an even more important role in rank and flank games, which due to their eponymous setup inherently place an even greater emphasis on movement. So, what shape does terrain come in in Conquest, where do you get it, how should you use it (both inside the game and out), and what does it do? Let’s find out together!

The Role of Terrain in General

If you wanted to boil down this whole article into one sentence, it would be this: Terrain usually serves as a piece of the game board that says, "Don’t be here." That's either because it physically prevents your units from traversing said game space or because it applies some kind of disadvantage, be it blocking line of sight, hindering your charge or lowering your combat capabilities.

The fun comes from the symmetrical nature of the game and the contextual meaning of terrain that arises from it: While it may be true that a piece of terrain blocks your path, it also does so for your opponent. So you can turn that disadvantage into an advantage if you want to protect a unit of yours.

That all sounds easy enough, and it is, but there are some finer nuances left to be explored. So let’s next take a look at the different kinds of terrain that exist in Conquest. 

Types of Terrain in Conquest: The Last Argument of Kings

There are two categories of Terrain to consider in Conquest: The Last Argument of Kings: Zonal and Garrison Terrain. From personal experience, you will be dealing with Zonal Terrain the vast majority of the time, whereas Garrison Terrain is a more specialized bit for certain scenarios.

Zonal Terrain is a designated bit of the game board that confers specific rules, corresponding to a selection of several keywords. These are as follows:
  • Elevated: Hills and such. Regiments can move through Elevated Terrain despite it not having the Traversable keyword (see below). Elevated Terrain adds its size to a Regiments Size, which has implications for Line of Sight. For example: If you have a Size 1 infantry regiment on a piece of Elevated Zonal Terrain (read: a hill) that is Size 2, the regiment would count as being Size 3 for purposes of determining line of sight. Basically, you can shoot over your own guys when you’re on a hill.
  • Cover: Stands that draw Line of Sight through a piece of Cover Zonal Terrain have to halve their Barrage (X) value during Volley Actions. The same is true for Stands that target a Regiment in Cover Zonal Terrain of lesser or equal Size to the terrain piece. This is meant to represent shooting through or into dense forests and such, where your ability to get a good shot is hindered. If a regiment has all of its stands in a piece of Cover Zonal Terrain, it doesn’t halve its Barrage value, since the regiment is in the forest, shooting out.
  • Obstructing: Obstructing Zonal Terrain blocks Line of Sight completely for Regiments of equal or lower Size. This is meant to represent stuff like a big rock and usually, terrain pieces with this rule are also not Traversable (see below). So unless you are on the piece of Obstructing Zonal Terrain, you can not draw Line of Sight through the terrain piece – and if you are, you still can.
  • Traversable: Regiments can move through pieces of Zonal Terrain with the Traversable keyword and they don’t gain a bonus to their size while they are on it. 
  • Hindering: Tangled brambles or uneven ground, Hindering Terrain is meant to evoke difficult areas of the battlefield to maneuver, hindering charges and such. If a Regiment moves half of its Stands (rounding up) through a piece of Hindering Terrain, it does not inflict any Impact Hits during that Charge Action. In addition, Medium and Heavy Regiments do not gain the Inspired bonus from Charging, if they Charge through Hindering Terrain.
  • Dangerous: Dangerous Terrain means actively harmful stuff, like sharp rocks or lava. Any Regiment moving onto or through a piece of Dangerous Zonal Terrain has to roll a dice for each of its Stands that touched it. Each roll of “6” means 1 Wound, while Cavalry and Chariots suffer 2 Wounds per. 
  • Perilous: Is basically Dangerous Terrain but moreso; think sharper rocks or hotter lava. It works the same, but each roll of “4”, “5” or “6” causes a Wound (still 2 for Cavalry and Chariots).
  • Impassable: Exactly what it says on the tin. Meant to represent big houses or natural rock formations to large to traverse, you simply can’t move over or through Impassable Terrain.
  • Water: Standing water hinders your Warriors ability to fight, each Regiment that has at least half of its Stands in a piece of Water Zonal Terrain suffers a penalty of -1 to its Clash characteristic, to a minimum of 1.

Now we come to Garrison Terrain, and I won’t sugarcoat it: The rules of Garrisoning Terrain in Conquest: The Last Argument of Kings are a bit of a doozy. I’ll give you the short version here, since my experience with the game shows that most player or tournament organizers don’t really bother with this aspect of the rules.

In general, Garrison Terrain is recommended to be about 4 to 6” in diameter and should not be overlapping with any other piece of Terrain or any Objective Zones. They have specific Defense and Capacity values that do what you would think they do: They provide a cap for the number of Stands the terrain can hold and a Defense bonus for the Regiment occupying it. Recommended in the rules are Capacity (4) and Defense (1).

Infantry Regiments can occupy Garrison Terrain by marching to within 1” of it and ending their activation. While within the Garrison, the Regiment gains a bonus to their Defense and has a full 360° Line of Sight. From there, enemy regiments can Charge the piece of Garrison Terrain as if it were a Regiment itself, but they will deal no Impact Hits when they do so. To leave a piece of Garrison Terrain, the Regiment Occupying it has to once again use two March Actions. When it does, you set it up within the distance of the lowest March Characteristic in a legal formation. If you can’t, you can set it up within double the distance of your lowest March Characteristic, but the Regiment is then Broken.

This is a lot of book keeping, with a good amount of rules text covering specific edge cases. If you feel like you need this in your games, check out the full rules for it in the Core Rules, pages 87-90. Once you’ve done the brain work to understand Garrison, it reads kind of fun, especially for ranged units due to the added Defense and the 360° Line of Sight. I’d say give it a cursory read and then try it out in your next game.

How Do You, the Player, Use Terrain in Your Games?

Alright, so now that we covered what terrain does for you on the table, let’s go over some general advice on how to use terrain. The rules give good guidance as to the general shape that a 6’x4’ battlefield should take, and they are as follows:

  • At least 6 pieces of Terrain but no more than 8.
  • No Terrain on top of Objective Zones or Markers.
  • No Terrain pieces within 9” of each other.
  • No individual Terrain footprint larger than 9”x6”.
  • No more than two pieces of Garrison Terrain.

What Should You Buy for Terrain?

Conquest terrain. Credit: Mike Bettle-Shaffer

From there, you have a bit of room for personal expression, mainly down to what you actually use for terrain and how you set it up. The age old question in Terrain is this: 3D or 2D? Your answer should boil down to what you value more, verisimilitude or ease of use. 

No matter which you choose though, there are a handful of offerings directly from Para Bellum, if you want to keep things proprietary. For one, they offer STLs for 3D printing for most of the game’s factions. While I have no experience with 3D printing myself, I have experience playing with all of the terrain files printed by someone who printed them and they looked very nice. You can’t overstate how good it looks when the terrain and the minis are designed by the same people with an overarching design philosophy.

Alternatively, Para Bellum offers a more user friendly solution in their newer, foldable cardboard 3D terrain which is catered specifically for First Blood. While I have not yet used them for Last Argument of Kings, they are foldable and thereby easier to transport and carry through some of the design language from the aforementioned STL files. Plus, they come pre-painted, after a fashion, so there’s less work required.

Last but certainly not least are the 2D cardboard terrain pieces found in all of Para Bellum’s Last Argument of Kings starter sets. These often go overlooked by established players but are an entirely satisfactory way for newcomers to start playing the game right off the bat. I would personally recommend starting with these if you're new, since they come bundled with what you’re most likely purchasing to start hobbying and playing. 

From there, you should look to branch out once you have enough experience to know what you’re looking for. But don’t feel pressured by YouTube battle reports to get the full cinematic experience unless you have the time, budget and storage space for it. You can have a blast playing Conquest with 2D design – or even beer cans and styrofoam cups.

How Should You Set Up a Table?

How to set up your table depends entirely on the game setting. Are you playing casually or are you at a tournament? If the latter, any tournament organizer worth their salt will already have set up the Objective Zones and Terrain pieces and all you have to do is play.

If you are playing casually, a nice rule of thumb that I usually see people do (and that is quick and easy to set up) is this: Choose a selection of Terrain Pieces (2 Hindering, 2 Obscuring, a Hill, a large Impassable thing and a wild card like Water or Dangerous or both), put them all aside and roll off to see who gets to place one first. 

Then you alternate until all pieces are placed according to the rules (none on Objectives, 9” apart, etc.). I find that people tend toward symmetry in table setup for fairness. So if there is a Hindering piece near Player A’s deployment zone, we put another near Player B’s as well. This sets a nice vanilla baseline experience that is good for a quick casual game. If you want something more specialized, it’s probably correct to coordinate that beforehand to make sure that everything is fair and balanced.

Final Thoughts

Terrain can be intimidating. It’s sort of like asking an FPS player to be a level designer before they get to play. Which is to say nothing of the hobby time and skill required if you are looking to make a beautiful table, which is like also commissioning yourself as a 3D artist in the analogy above.

But just remember this: The game and the hobby are about fun first and foremost. To have the most amount of fun, you will need terrain, otherwise there is no friction and you will just be mashing army men against each other. But there are also no rules beyond that (apart from the actual game rules, but you know what I mean). If you are more for the crunchy rules aspect of the game, stacks of books, cups and plates will serve you just fine. If you want to make a beautiful table, there are plenty of offerings just for you. Now get out there and get gaming!
