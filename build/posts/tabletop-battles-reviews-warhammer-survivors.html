Tabletop Battles Reviews: Warhammer Survivors

November 10th, 2026 via Goonhammer

By the time you read this, Warhammer Survivors is out; this article is running at 5 AM Eastern, and by the time you’re getting ready to sit through your commute with your Steamdeck or power a console up to play on your morning cardio or picking up your phone at the end of the day to wind down with a couple rounds between doing the dishes and bed, it’ll be there for you. It installs at a little over a gigabyte of hard drive space -- maybe a tad larger than the original Vampire Survivors -- and really, that’s a great way to think about the game: It’s Vampire Survivors, except maybe a little bit bigger in every direction.

Base game Vampire Survivors, of course; poncle’s now-venerable genre-creating hit passed its four year anniversary from release back in October, and over that span it has incorporated free and paid DLC from Contra, Among Us, Balatro, Castlevania -- the IP they were filing the serial numbers off of at launch -- and more. Warhammer Survivors, which Auroch Digital (Warhammer 40,000: Boltgun and Boltgun II) collaborated with poncle on, is a bit larger undertaking; whereas the original base game for Vampire Survivors had five regular levels and then roughly 10 bonus, special, and challenge levels you accessed through various hijinks, Warhammer Survivor has 12 levels split into six in the Warhammer 40K side of the game and six in the Age of Sigmar side of the game. The game has separate progression tracks for each side; the only overlap they have is that when you max out a category on one universe’s progression, the corresponding category on the other side gets a bonus level.

For the sake of formality, we should go over how these games work. You get plopped down in the middle of a sidescrolling infinite floor; you’re a character with some sort of weapon. Your character attacks with that weapon automatically. Enemy units spawn on the edge of the screen and approach you in various patterns, compositions, and at various speeds. When your weapon kills them, they drop experience. When you level up with that experience, you pick one of three rewards, which are either an upgrade to your existing weapon, a new weapon, or a new passive ability that improves one of your character’s basic attributes. You can have six weapons and six passives equipped at the same time. Each weapon is paired with a corresponding passive; when you maximize that weapon’s level while you have the corresponding passive equipped, you can evolve that weapon into a much more powerful final form. You must survive for 25 minutes using health and power pickups in the world, correct leveling decisions, and proper navigation to avoid waves of enemies commanded by bosses who appear at timed checkpoints. Your reward for surviving all 25 minutes is getting to meet the floor’s Extremis Boss, who will automatically murder you to end the run, same as the Reaper did in the original Vampire Survivors. Figuring out how to turn the Extremis Boss encounters into actual fights constitutes the main goal of high-level play in Warhammer Survivors.

The newest mechanic to me, as a player who didn't keep up with a lot of the Vampire Survivors DLC, is the follower mechanic; your Imperial Guard characters, for example, can't use Space Marine weapons like Storm Bolters, and so when you select one of those weapons when leveling up, a Space Marine will appear to wield it for you. There are very slight gameplay impacts here; if you maneuver a boss between your followers and your main character, you can sometimes get in a lot more damage with the right party composition and avoid taking a bunch yourself. More commonly, heroes will have special abilities that key off of how many or how few followers they have. The majority of the time, followers are mainly aesthetic.

At time of writing I’ve put some 40 hours into the game, originally splitting my time between the 40K side of the game and the Age of Sigmar side of the game to experience the enemies and levels in both; I quickly realized that was not the correct strategy for writing this review. From the beginning I should have picked a progression tree and stuck to it, so I could get to the Extremis Bosses and defeat one of them. I’ve reached all three of those guys on the Age of Sigmar branch -- that’s the one I eventually settled on -- but still haven’t fully recorded a win on any of these stages. I haven’t even unlocked the final normal stage for either universe, because reaching level 60 can get pretty gnarly with how the spawns are tuned.

This is good news, in my estimation. You do not buy One of These games to be done with them in ten hours. They’re a form of solitaire; I mainly use them as something to keep my hindbrain and hands busy while I’m listening to a podcast on an exercise bike. You make actual decisions once every minute or so of gameplay, and the rest of the time you play extremely instinct-level games of keep-away from the hordes of monsters rampaging across the screen. Again: You know if this is the kind of game for you. You’ve either put hundreds of hours into these things over the last half-decade or you really don’t understand, at all, what people see in this genre.

Not a lot of story in this game; none, in fact. Good. Don’t need it; not what we’re here for. Great if you know the characters that the game is shouting out; I’m now a huge fan of Armand Callis from Age of Sigmar just because his combination of weapon (Brace of Pistols) and hero ability (+2 Attack, which is +2 Amount from Vampire Survivors, as long as you only have one Follower) is excellent for a lot of the challenges you’ll face in later stages, where sheer damage output becomes a huge problem and you need to have a high alpha strike weapon that can just erase some guys instead of dealing less individual damage to many enemies across the field. But Armand isn’t saying any of the lines that Armand is presumably famous for; we’re here for more important business: Slaughtering tens of thousands of gitz while I get my time in on the bike.

While there’s no writing to speak of, the game is a treat to look at. The UI design is clean, and the motifs for both the 40K and Age of Sigmar sides are well done; the game is in the exact same pixel-art style and perspective as poncle’s original game, and so if you liked that presentation -- and many people did! -- then you’ll like this. In fact the games are so similar that liking Vampire Survivors is just going to be a direct proxy for interest in this game; many of the weapons and power-ups are direct reskins of weapons and power-ups from the original title. The game also runs well; that's not really something that should be a concern with a title like this, but still worth noting that I've run into no high enemy count lag so far when the screen gets real full with guys.

That brings us to the value proposition, then: This standalone game hits the market at $8 on Steam. The original Vampire Survivors launched at two bucks, I think; it currently sits at $5 before DLC. You are, of course, paying some amount of a premium for the Warhammer IP; if you’re a Warhammer fan, you’re used to that by now. Is there eight dollars of game here? I think so. Now, I’m someone who has put about 160 hours into Vampire Survivors itself and was still more than willing to come back for more of this (with some years off, in fairness) so I might be something of a captured market. But if I’m about to eclipse 40 hours in the title and I still haven’t unlocked all the levels -- let alone all the characters or stuff like the Arcana card equivalents -- that says to me I’m probably getting up past 60 and probably towards 80 before I close the door on this version of the Survivors game, and that’s a good return on investment for me. And now I have opinions on an Age of Sigmar character for the first time, to boot.
