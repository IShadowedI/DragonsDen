Commander Focus: Paradigm, Stickers on Face-Downs, & Kirol, Attentive First-Year

May 21st, 2026 via Goonhammer
The feeling of a mythic rare is different now than in Magic's past. Introduced originally in Shards of Alara, almost two decades ago, it was outlined for only the most splashy and 'epic' cards; crucially, rares were meant to be the backbone of competitive play, being more accessible given they were often required at 4 copies in formats like Standard. There's been a trend since around 2020 however, to slot these competitive staples in mythic, despite not being especially splashy (one recent egregious example is Badgermole Cub)...but that's not to say the designers have forgotten what it feels like to cast a mythic Spell. The concurrent trend with the previous is the introduction of a brand-new mechanic to go alongside a mythic cycle, such as Impending from Duskmourn, and now Paradigm from Secrets of Strixhaven. The raw power of a Paradigm spell resounds for the rest of the game, accruing value over and over, with the caveat that only one instance of that spell's name does so at a time. The thing is, though, as we've seen on Commander Focus, names can be changed. While a fair few decks might slot in a Paradigm Spell or two, I want to cover a specific list that combines a few of Magic's oddest mechanics to wring every last drop of mechanical potency from these cards, led by Strixhaven's very own Kirol, Attentive First-Year. Join me as we get a little Delusionary, and manifest a path of play that touches every word of Paradigm's keyword text.



"After You First Resolve a Spell with This Name..."

Let's start by breaking down exactly how Paradigm works. When you resolve a Paradigm Spell, as part of its end of resolution, you exile it and gain a permanent delayed trigger if it's the first time in a game you've resolved a Spell with that name. That trigger allows you to, at the start of each first Main Phase from then on, create a copy of the Paradigm Spell in exile and cast it for free if so inclined. Critically, the original card does not itself need to be in exile, as clarified in release notes, and if copied by an opponent provides them with an ongoing Paradigm copy on resolution. All of the rules surrounding Paradigm can be found in detail under 702.192 of the Comprehensive Rules, but the two most important aspects for us today are that the delayed trigger persists, regardless of where the original game object is, and that it checks for name on resolution. This is all meant to be an allusion to the old Epic mechanic, which locked you entirely out of Spells for the rest of the game, in exchange for dangerous recurring effects, all on massive Sorceries. With Paradigm, while the effects are less game-warping, the Spells...don't lock you out of the game, and therefore are better by default most of the time. The two available Paradigm pieces for our colors, here Red & White, are Improvisation Capstone & Restoration Seminar, each costing a whopping 7 mana. Of course, they're providing massive value once we get them resolved, but what's our gameplan until then?

The answer's a bit of an odd one, because a huge chunk of this deck is dedicated to something you wouldn't expect to see with big Sorceries: Manifest. Our Commander, Kirol, isn't just a source to duplicate the delayed triggers of Paradigm Spells (though that does help), he's also a way to dig through our Library by ripping cards off the top and turning them into 2/2s. The vast majority of Manifest cards in our colors come in the form of triggered abilities, and given they produce untapped bodies, Kirol can both double them and use the newly-generated face-downs to duplicate more triggers on other players' turns! If we play an Unsettling Twins with Kirol onboard, we're able to tap himself and the Twins, Manifesting Dread twice...which in turn creates two more untapped bodies, and digs four cards deep into the deck. Boros as a color pairing doesn't traditionally get a ton of great card selection, so this is a way to sort through cards in our Library to find the right pieces at the right time. Manifest has a secondary benefit though, in being one of the few ways to turn non-permanent cards into permanents; there's not a great number of choices if we're attempting to animate our Restoration Seminar, and the density of Manifest options means we can usually get it face-down at some point during the game. Why on Arcavios would we want to, though? What's the benefit to turning a Paradigm Spell into a permanent? Well...remember how I called attention to the fact that Paradigm checks for the card's name?

Stick 'Em Up

Does the Hayes Code apply to TCGs?

There is a way to change the name of a card in Commander, though folks familiar with this series might already be groaning at the knowledge of what it is. That's right, we're putting Stickers on our face-down Paradigm pieces, to force the mental picture of what a 'Guacamole Improvisation Capstone' looks like into our opponent's heads. Psychic damage aside, Stickers are a unique mechanic that require Tickets to place, coming from three randomly-drawn Sticker Sheets you're made aware of from a pool of ten before the game begins. Name Stickers, the ones we're largely using, cost a cool 1 Ticket, so most of our Sticker placers like Minotaur de Force & Ticketomaton can do so as an enters trigger. Stickers are, crucially, retained on game objects as they move through public zones, meaning so long as a card is on the battlefield, on the stack, in your graveyard, or exiled, they stay sticky—if we refer to CR123.6c (and CR708), Stickers are functionally always placed on the 'face-up' side of cards, meaning even if we choose to slap 'Trapeze' on a Manifested card it's still modifying the front side. This means, if we were to sticker up a Manifested Paradigm piece, have it die, and cast it via Mizzix's Mastery, we're getting our Paradigm trigger for the sticker-modified name of the Spell, not its original name. There is the small hurdle of getting the exiled Paradigm Spell back, but thankfully cards like Pull From Eternity & Mirror of Fate exist, as well as Riftsweeper in colors beyond ours.

As mentioned, removing the resolved Paradigm Spell from exile doesn't stop the delayed trigger, so putting it back in the yard or Library does no harm...beyond, of course, removing the stickers, should it go back to our deck. That's actually a good thing, especially if the first version of the Paradigm piece cast was a stickered version, meaning we don't need to jump through that hoop again with its second copy. There are cards in the list which make us pay full price for the modified Paradigm Spell, such as Soul Partition, and two new options from Secrets of Strixhaven, Suspend Aggression & Practiced Scrollsmith. That's a cool 7 mana, which isn't impossible to generate, but usually takes an entire turn. We'd ideally want to find a way to skirt the full cost, while keeping the relevant Spell in public zones, after having been a face-down permanent. See, we'd much rather pay {2} than the full cost, and luckily one of my favorite new mechanics from the last year is up to the task: Airbending.

Are You an Airbender, Boy?

Still better than the Shyamalan one.

Whenever a permanent is Airbent, it's moved to exile, and can be cast for an alternate cost of {2}; you aren't allowed any other alternate costs, but discounts do apply. Airbend is a White-dominated mechanic, which works out wonderfully for us, and is thankfully found among a number of card types. There's of course the one-off Airbend effects in the form of Instants & Sorceries, of Airbending Lesson & Avatar's Wrath, but also more repeatably by way of permanents, via Monk Gyatso, or Airbender Ascension. Oddly enough though, the single-best Airbend card in this deck is likely the humble Glider Staff, an Equipment with an enters trigger. White is able to tutor one card type more than any other, and that is without a doubt Equipment, and with access to additional copies of the Glider Staff by way of Masterwork of Ingenuity (itself accessible via Tezzeret, Cruel Captain) you can find a way to Airbend your Paradigm Spells at pretty much any point. Due to the sheer density of cards available in Boros which both Manifest & Airbend, it's actually not all that difficult to do for the first time in a game, Stickers or no. Even beyond just Paradigm Spells, this is a shockingly efficient mana cheating engine available to Wx decks, and while part of it was covered in my primer for Monk Gyatso Eldrazi I think it could be spread across far more decks than those dedicated to Airbending.

While finding Glider Staff is a fantastic route to cheating out a Paradigm Spell, it's far from the only Equipment enjoyed by this list. There's of course Skullclamp, which combined with out Manifested 2/2s can result in a ton of value alongside a sacrifice outlet, but also Lightning Greaves—beyond the obvious upside of providing Shroud to something like our Commander, it also represents infinite capacity for Airbending alongside Monk Gyatso. I think the funniest bit of tech with regards to these tutorable armaments though comes through via Conductive Machete & Dissection Tools. These have triggered abilities on enter to Manifest Dread...meaning we can of course flex them with Kirol to peel away cards off the top of our Library. The only thing we can't do as a conducive part of our gameplan via Equipment is sticker up a Manifested card, as unfortunately Pin Collection only stickers itself.

No Mere Mirror

Tying everything together, we have a fairly expensive permanent that puts itself in the graveyard and allows us to make the best-possible use of both Paradigm Spells: Mirror of Fate. This is actually the second time such a grossly obscure card has been showcased on Commander Focus, but here it's essential rather than a nice-to-have. Using Mirror, we get to exchange our Library with up to 7 exiled cards in an order of our choosing, and put it into the graveyard. In the context of our Paradigm options, this means we can get it back every turn if there's at least one Restoration Seminar live, and rip through cards of mana value 4 or less for each instance of Improvisation Capstone. Given this, if we for example make the top of our deck Unwanted Remake, a Paradigm Spell, one other card, Minotaur de Force, and finally Airbending Lesson (Give or take 1-2 cards we want to draw, given Lesson could be any Airbending option), we can build a psuedo-Doomsday pile to add an additional Paradigm Instance with a different name to our cycle each turn once Improv or Restoration gets going. You can construct some wildly intricate lines in this way, as can be expected of a Doomsday pile, but given we're keeping this list in Bracket 2 it's far funnier to make more and more obscurely-named Paradigm resolutions, rather than use this as a means to combo off.

Get into that face-down creature grindset.

That being said, if you're working towards a win in the stricter sense, Glaring Fleshraker is your main option. Every time you Manifest, opponents get pinged, and that can be 20+ damage over the course of a game if they're not careful. Moreover, Robaran Mercenaries & Marvin, Murderous Mimic each represent an additional Kirol-style activation per turn, and that gets wildly out of hand alongside your Manifest triggers, or even something like a Wandering Archaic//Explore the Vastlands. The deck is stuffed full of 2-card soft combos, like the interaction between Guardian of the Forgotten & Veiled Ascension, or Monk Gyatso & Lightning Greaves. These are both reasonable mechanisms to close out a game, once you've had your fun.

Example Decklist: Kirol Gets an English Degree

And in fact, that's quite a bit of fun you'll be having! Kirol is a solid card on his own, and is a weirdly-underrated option for a Manifest/Morph-style Commander as a counterpoint to something like Kadena, Slinking Sorcerer. The deck has few cards that exist purely as neat inclusions in their own right, given how concerted the gameplan is, but there's still a couple worth talking about! Both Bibloplex Guardian & Not Forgotten are ways of getting back relevant Spells (usually of the Paradigm sort) from the graveyard to your Library, and given they get to go on top that means either pairs well with Manifest. Park Bleater is a card that likely deserves to see more generic play, as despite using the Sticker mechanic, acting as a Soul Sisters-style body for some fairly-relevant effects is noteworthy. Even if all you're doing is slapping a better Power/Toughness Sticker onto a body that wants to attack, you're still getting reasonable value. In any deck in which you'd jam something like a Guide of Souls, consider Bleater as well!

The more decks with Red that I play, the more bullish I've been on Glimpse the Impossible, and to a lesser extent, Valakut Exploration. With the former, you get to see 3 cards for 3 mana—a solid baseline rate—but if you don't need them, or can't play them, you still get value. Using it as a way to 'throw' mana forward is awesome, and with the cards still hitting your graveyard, key combo pieces are never truly stranded in exile. It's a similar story for Valakut Exploration, with the same sense of always getting something out of it (though damage is worse than Spawn tokens), and never stranding cards. I want to bring your attention to the manabase though, to highlight another reason why Exploration is seeing success in this list: The cycle of mandatory Capenna fetches. These each trigger on enters to sacrifice themselves, and grab a basic (with only Caberetti Courtyard getting both our colors). The important thing about these is that they enter untapped, and have associated triggers; not only does this mean we get to use Kirol to double up, and ramp efficiently, but these Lands which normally don't tap for mana love seeing Urborg, Tomb of Yawgmoth & Yavimaya, Cradle of Growth. These mean you're getting mana the turn they come down, even if it's off-color, but in a list with so many colorless cards that's hardly a problem. Along a similar vein, the new Petrified Hamlet also serves to allow them to tap before being sacrificed, though you need to 'call your shot' as it has but a single name...unless you double the trigger with Kirol. Unlike so many cards of this sort, it names when it enters, not as it enters, and that means you can knock out two problematic utility Lands at once with Kirol, or keep your options open with Capenna-style fetches. This manabase is something I'd play in every Kirol deck, no matter what you're actually doing with your nonlands. It's just that good.

Decklists are kept updated, and may change with set releases.



This deck has been built as a Bracket 2 affair, and I think that's about right. Our goal is fundamentally to illustrate a deeply weird mechanical interaction, and while winning is nice, that's secondary to the sleight of hand at hand. This list is a party trick (a very cool one), but pushing its power likely means you're switching Commanders entirely, for one with a greater access to colors. Gaining Sultai access gives you oodles of tools for both better Manifest, and better Stickers, and just being able to play 3 additional Paradigm targets is sweet. Still, this list is functional, and has a solid foundation for its gameplan, even if not every aspect is as robust as a 5C option might be. Kirol is there to, at worst, give you two Paradigm triggers each turn, and that alone will win games in the world of upgraded precons at which Bracket 2 resides.

Turning On a Paradigm

Rimas Valeikis bring history to life, with high contrast amid the parchment backdrop.

There's a very reasonable argument that the better version of this list arrives in the form of a five-color pile, and I don't discount those claims. Not only do you have better means of tutoring the Paradigm Spells to the top of your Library, but also a greater number of them, on top of Riftsweeper as an additional shuffle effect. Heck, even Rocco, Cabaretti Caterer could work with that in mind, Blue & Black be damned. My point in having Kirol lead this list was not to say he's the best option for this little experiment, but rather to open the floodgates for others to iterate, to see just how far this immensely cool mechanic could be pushed. Paradigm is a sick way to generate value over time, and Stickers are (begrudgingly) one of the best ways to get the very most out of the keyword; while this also implies Manifest need be a core component, and tangentially Airbend, those work well enough on their own as to not be a problematic component. I'm here to take a bow, to doff my cap, and welcome folks into the world of making 'Breaking Paradigm' an archetype in of itself.

In Bracket 3 & below, it makes a ton of sense for this to be the case, as games tend to go long, and Paradigm Spells beyond Black & Green have sufficient impact as to be worth copying. If your goal is to resolve three differently-named variants of Improvisation Capstone, that can and will push you towards a win. Kirol helps to ensure you get extra value off of even one, given the trigger copying, but that's small potatoes compared to what you could really get up to, given the chance. All this boils down to doing things you really aren't meant to, though, and that gets at the crux of this coverage: Magic is deep. There's ways to take non-permanents, make them Creatures, change their names, and cheat their costs—all while remaining in public zones—and even retrieve them while exiled, a place from which cards aren't really meant to return. If you tilt your head and check the fine print, you can do almost anything in Magic, so while sure you'll see a UGx Landfall deck or Boros aggro at nearly every FNM, opportunities abound to get weird with the game in terms of the system itself. Be the story you want to tell your friends. Show people things they've never seen.

Until next time, I've got to clock in for my Paradigm shift.
