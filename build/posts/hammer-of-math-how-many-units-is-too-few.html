Hammer of Math: How Many Units Is Too Few?

November 9th, 2026 via Goonhammer

In Hammer of Math, we look at various statistical concepts in Warhammer 40k, looking at how to evaluate different options mathematically and understanding how those probabilities and statistics affect your games. In this article we're looking at a question posed by a Patron last week on our Discord.

A few weeks ago I did an article on Hammer of Math around the number of units by points value in winning vs. losing lists, trying to validate the idea that there was an ideal structure to winning lists. That ultimately didn't prove to be true, but it did prompt this question on our Discord the following week from Blackbird Logan:

Q. With the rise in elite armies, how models is too few or too many for the different dispositions, and how do you feel about certain armies being solidly geared towards certain objectives? Would you like or dislike to see certain armies lose access to specific dispos if it made the army more thematic or cohesive?

I'm mostly going to focus on the first part here, because it's a question we can answer with math, using the dataset I had already pulled a couple of weeks ago for that last analysis.

The Sample

I used the same sample as last time - I pulled all of the 40k lists from GT-sized events (28+ players, 5+ rounds) run over the eight weeks before October 26, 2026 and split them into two groups:

  • TiWP (Tournament in Winning Position) lists, i.e. lists which started their event with a 4-0 or better record. These are lists which win their first three games and actually make it to the upper rounds of an event. At a Games Workshop event or a 3-day event with a top cut, a 4-0 start usually guarantees you'll be in the top cut or playing to be in the top cut. It also speaks to a sustained ability to win against tougher opponents: Most tournaments do record or win-path pairings, so if you start 4-0 it means you beat opponents who started 1-0, 2-0, and 3-0 to achieve that record. I like this more than an "X-1" list because it means you can have lists that started 6-0 and finished 6-2 while excluding people who lost their first game and submarined up to a 4-1 finish in the loser's bracket.
  • Non-TiWP lists. Everyone else.

Note that I treated Attached characters as one large unit for the purposes of this analysis - if a unit cost 150 points and had an attached 100-point character, they were treated as a single 250-point unit.

The total sample was 530 TiWP lists and 7,545 non-TiWP lists.

I ran this analysis twice, once with Imperial Knights and once without, as my notion was that they'd bias the sample toward having a smaller number of units (Chaos Knights were less likely to do this, owing to war dog-heavy lists frequently being 10+ units). I looked at the results by Disposition to understand if there were thresholds worth looking at by mission type.

The Results

This generated more interesting results than I was expecting. There were clear differences between the Dispositions, and ones that made intuitive sense. What was also interesting was that the results were more or less identical regardless of whether I left Imperial Knights in the sample. For every disposition, 10-14 units was the most common range, making up around 70% of the lists Per Disposition.

  • Priority Assets: TiWP lists for Priority Assets had an average of 13.2 units and a median of 13, compared to 12.7/13 for non-TiWP lists. TiWP lists tended to avoid having 9 or fewer units.
  • Take and Hold: TiWP lists for Take & Hold had an average of 11.3 units and a median of 11, which was more or less the same for non-TiWP lists. Having 9 or fewer units was common for Take and Hold lists.
  • Purge the Foe: TiWP lists had an average of 11.5 units and a median of 12, versus 11 for non-TiWP and a median of 11. TiWP lists tended to be tightly clustered in the 10-14 range here compared to other dispositions.
  • Reconnaissance: TiWP lists averaged 14.3 units and a median of 14, which was about the same as for non-TiWP lists. Recon had the highest unit counts, and having 15 or more units was normal for Recon lists.
  • Disruption: The smallest sample, TiWP lists here averaged 12.9 units and had a median of 12, versus 13.6 / 13 for non-TiWP lists.

As a general guideline, the answer to "what's too few units" is "anything less than 9," though generally speaking you're going to want something more like 10-14 depending on your army and disposition. That's a fine general rule for building around, and if you aren't on Take and Hold or Purge, you're often going to need to do actions as part of your primary mission scoring, which makes having more units a better deal. The difference in average unit counts - 12.6 units for TiWP lists vs. 12.3 for non-TiWP lists - is real, and driven by TiWP lists being less likely to drop below 10 units rather than from a shift towards higher unit counts. TiWP lists tended to be much more tightly clustered around that average. But generally Disposition mattered more than a unit being a TiWP list when it came to the number of units.

Essentially Take and Hold and Purge the Foe tended to run the fewest units, focusing more on big threats which can stay on the table for multiple activations and can kill major opposition if needed. Take and Hold lists were by far the most likely to make use of 300+ point units in our sample after we removed knights, with an average of two 300+ point units per list. With Take and Hold you want big, meaty units which can throw a lot of OC on the table and bully threats off objectives .

Reconnaissance lists had the most units by far in its TiWP cohort and the lowest general point values for the lists it was taking. Recon lists tend to want 14+ units to be able to spread out and occasionally score action-based secondary objectives. Recon lists were also the list likely to make use of 300+point units, running an average of 0.91 units.

While I was doing this I figured I'd also look at some unit costs and the number of sub-100-point units included in armies for each disposition. Generally speaking, TiWP lists tend to pack more cheap units, but Disposition was a bigger factor:

  • Priority Assets - Median of 5 sub-100 point units in TiWP/non-TiWP lists, 5.3 average vs. 5.1.
  • Take and Hold - Median of 4 vs. 3 in TiWP vs. non-TiWP lists, average of 3.9 vs. 3.5.
  • Purge the Foe - Median of 3 sub-100 units, 3.3 in TiWP lists vs. 3.1 in non-TiWP
  • Reconnaissance - Median of 5 vs. 6 sub-100 units, 5.6 vs. 6.1 in TiWP vs. non-TiWP lists
  • Disruption - Median of 4 vs. 5 sub-100 units, average of 5.4 in both

This suggests that TiWP lists tend to take slightly more cheap action and chaff units in Priority Assets, Take and Hold, and Purge the Foe than their less successful counterparts, while Recon and Disruption lists may be more likely to overdo it trying to play more cheap units to do primary instead of focusing on killing the opponent first and scoring for being in table quarters/doing actions second. Of course, this is also all wrapped up in faction dynamics but hopefully Ork unit counts here help offset counts for more elite armies on the TiWP side, while knights help balance things out on the non-TiWP side of things.

Losing Dispositions

As far as losing Dispositions goes, I think this is kind of a non-starter - you want every faction to be viable in a teams environment, so even if it isn't the most thematic thing for an army, you do want every army to have access to every disposition. I'm amenable to the idea that an army like World Eaters can play Disruption - and that it might look very different for them than other armies, where they dispatch forces to sow carnage and wreak havoc in the enemy lines and disrupt their operations rather than explicitly just trying to kill as a primary goal. But the missions themselves still don't always reflect that - in no world are Bloodletters doing actions, but that hardly matters since the best way to play Disruption is often to just play it like purge.

Final Thoughts

This was a fun follow-up analysis to do. There hasn't been enough broad list analysis in 40k using the data available and digging deep into units, their costs, and list structures has yielded some interesting results, if not necessarily strong rules for building successful lists. I think the idea that you don't want to run fewer than 10 units unless you have a compelling reason to do so is a good rule of thumb to follow, and gives players lots of wiggle room, especially when you consider the counts by disposition.
