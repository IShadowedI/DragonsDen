Tabletop Battles Reviews: Voice of Command, by Richard Swan

October 9th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Games Workshop

If it wasn't for Australian Rules Football, it's entirely possible that I would never have gotten into Warhammer.

Believe it or not, Louisville was the modern birthplace of the sport in the US with the first match against a team from Cincinnati taking place in 1996. That was a decade in the rearview when I was playing for the Louisville Kings, of course, but one day I got to chatting with a teammate about our shared interest in science fiction and he gushed about Warhammer 40,000. Seeing he'd caught my interest, the very next practice he brought a few of his omnibuses for me to borrow- and that's how I learned about Eisenhorn, Gaunt's Ghosts, and Ciaphas Cain.

One of my enduring memories of that time saw me sitting on the steps of the family Summer cottage on Cape Cod next to my future wife Jimi, hunched over that loaner copy of Eisenhorn until it grew too dark outside to read. Dan Abnetts' depiction of the world of the Chaos-tainted Saruthi in Xenos, with its strange coloration and unnatural angles, spoke to a familiar kind of eldritch horror that had me turning page after page to see what would come next.

Similarly, Abnett's descriptions of plummeting temperatures and appearance of ice during warp manifestations echoed back to my lifelong love of ghost stories, and to this day remains some of the most evocative and unsettling depictions of Chaos in the Black Library.

I was, as they say, hooked.

Now in the present, having read many more books in the Black Library, I find this "horror-story sensibility" of Chaos to be the exception rather than the norm. Oh, we get plenty of descriptions of strange mutations and plague zombies, pink and blue unearthly horrors and uncomfortably alluring beings with lobster claw hands, but I always sit up and take notice when a writer brings something new and distinctive to the table when it comes to describing Chaos. Something that recaptures that early sense of discomfited wonder at the unspeakable malignancy of the warp and its Ruinous Powers.

And with his Black Library novel debut, Voice of Command, author Richard Swan did precisely that.

When Voice of Command was revealed on the mothership, the response in the community was somewhat muted. I mean, c'mon, the deck is probably stacked against any book not named Pandaemonium that's teased alongside a new offering from Peter Fehervari, right? But the rather uninspiring cover art (illustrated by Alex Gor, whose last name makes drawing for Warhammer almost seem predestined) alongside a rather generic title and a general sense of 'Cadian fatigue' wasn't winning Swan any nods for Most Anticipated Novel of 2026.

But as it turns out, Voice of Command is one of the year's more interesting surprises.

The Story

Voice of Command puts us alongside the Cadian 2002nd Mechanized Regiment on the shrine world of Nymea. They're two years into a grueling campaign to push back a traitorous Ecclesiarchy and Guard that have succumbed to the taint of corruption. A malefic blue star sometimes appears in the night sky, its toxic light uncomfortable to look at and heralding misfortune. The Imperial vox is taken over each night with broadcasts of unlistenable heresy, and strange crystals have begun to sprout from the earth.

On the eve of their push to retake the capital city of Saint Stanislava's Adoration, the Cadians must not only navigate the schemes of the enemy, but tensions between Cadians and Nymeans, Inquisitorial meddling, creeping corruption in the ranks, and the misfortunes of war.

The action focuses on Colonel "Venom" Velterson- whose nickname is almost an afterthought, adroitly avoiding coming off as cheesy- and a number of the officers and troopers of the Cadian regiment. Swan plays with a relatively large cast here, helpfully including a nicely detailed Dramatis Personae so you don't need to backtrack if someone slips your mind. The POV frequently shifts, but always in focused service to the greater narrative flow (think Robert Rath's excellent Fall of Cadia here).

As the Cadian troops slog it out in the streets and alleys of the city, an airborne Kasrkin unit attempts insertion for a decapitation strike. All the while, a monstrous- and monstrously familiar- threat from within threatens to destroy the Cadians before they can reclaim the city.

Image credit: Games Workshop

The Crystal Method

I was blown away by Swan's ability to generate genuine dread and suspense almost from the jump. It helps that the story's protagonists are Imperial Guard, and in being "regular" humans they're in many ways the most relatable of all Warhammer 40K's factions. That makes them a perfect foil for the cosmic and body horror elements that increase in volume as the story races along. When someone goes to move a corpse only to find it crunches like broken glass, you tend to sit up and take notice.

As a work of military science fiction, this had the same clarity of writing and terrific pacing that made Abnett's Gaunt's Ghosts stories so riveting. There's little slowdown to the story; once things kick off (and they do almost immediately), the rest of the book is essentially one scene as the battle grinds towards its inexorable conclusion. This may be Swan's debut Black Library novel, but hardly his first. He's penned two fantasy trilogies (Empire of the Wolf, and the ongoing Great Silence trilogy), as well as a science-fiction one (the Art of War trilogy), and is a Sunday Times bestselling author.

That deep experience was clearly evident in Voice of Command, not only in the narrative elements he brought with him, but the ones he declined to use. One of the most refreshing parts of this story is that while the main characters are Cadians, that's only modestly relevant to the story beyond just providing a source of friction between them and the native Nymean regiment. If you give this one a miss due to that aforementioned Cadian fatigue, it's worth having a rethink.

Finally, the writing is also terrifically descriptive without bogging down the pace. Here's one example, a moment of battlefield fatigue that's almost poetic in its expression:

"Was this it? Could this truly be it forever? Did they just move from one battlefield to the next, fighting and dying? Uncountable staging areas? Bivouacs, tents, trenches - trench foot - lung rot, brain rot, dysentery, venereal disease? The false, desperate love of a camp follower, the illicit drag on a narcotic cigarette, shots of life-shortening spirits distilled in an ammunition can? Was it nothing but mud and blood, the sight of yet another trooper being cut in half by artillery, listening to the resigned sighs of the old guard, the disbelieving weeping of the fresh recruits? Was it sitting in the cramped holds of mass troop conveyances in the cold depths of space, the mind-altering - mind-ending, life-ending - horrors of the warp? Was it just battle after battle, round after round, bullets and lasgun beams and chainsword swipes, until her life was ended by the enemy, or her own hand - or worse, by completely random bad luck? Would she one day be killed in a vehicle collision or a Geller field failure? Would they be subjected to the capricious whims of the Inquisition, or the bottomless incompetence of the Administratum? Would she be carpet-bombed by her own side, stand on a land mine, get shot by a sniper?

Just one more mile - one more planet, one more system, one more campaign, one more sector. But it never ended. That was the truth of it. It was infinity, and the worst sort, too. In the grim darkness of her future, of all their futures, there was only war.

Sublime.

Image credit: Games Workshop

Final Thoughts

Of all the criteria I use to assess a book, one of the simplest and most informal is, how long did it take me to read it? Did I grind through the book, reading only during my usual pre-bedtime ritual? Did I carry the book around me, happy to squeeze in a few pages at every opportunity? Something in the middle? It's been a minute since I tore through a book as quickly as I did this one.

Overall, Voice of Command is a cracking read and a real statement of what Swan is able to do in the Black Library if given the opportunity. I feel a bit foolish for having done so, but I did indeed judge a book by its cover- though in fairness, a look at the book's product page on the webstore shows that the marketing team's own copy was itself a bit lackluster (a "heart-rending Warhammer 40,000 novel," really?)

But that aside, Swan blends a veteran writer's skill and craft with a fresh take on the horrors of Chaos, and nestling all that within an Astra Militarum novel gave it a lot of room to breathe. This is certainly one of the year's standout books, and I recommend it without hesitation.
