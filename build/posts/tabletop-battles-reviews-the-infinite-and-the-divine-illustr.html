Tabletop Battles Reviews: The Infinite and the Divine (Illustrated and Annotated Edition), by Robert Rath

August 7th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Games Workshop

To hear Robert Rath tell it, he went into writing his story of two bickering Necrons with the thought firmly in mind that this could well be the only Black Library book he'd ever get the chance to write.

He had arrived in the stable only just the year before, with the story The Garden of Mortal Delights. Interestingly enough, this remains his only work for the Age of Sigmar setting (though he'd get close the following year with a short for Blood Bowl, The Freelancer). The editors of the Black Library then approached him to write a Warhammer 40,000 story for the 2019 Advent Calendar, which became the first of his series of Officio Assassinorum stories that would culminate in 2022's Assassinorum: Kingmaker. With his foot in the door for a grim, dark future where there is only war, Rath never looked back.

On the strength of his early work, Rath was greenlit for a novel- and he leaned in to a tale of an intense rivalry.

The Story

As indicated by the book's terrific title, this is a story of a timeless rivalry between two Necron lords. On the one side you've got Trazyn the Infinite, historian and archivist renowned for his collection of historical artifacts, curiosities, and stasis-locked living creatures comprising a museum of the universe.

On the other, Orikan the Diviner, a time-bending chronomancer obsessed with evolving towards his vision of becoming a being of pure energy, unconstrained by his primitive mortal shell.

The two are set on a collision course when Orikan breaks into Trazyn's museum in order to steal one of the countless items on display, a strange puzzle box called the Astrarium Mysterios. The Mysterios is both the map and the key to the tomb of Phaeron Nephreth the Untouched, a legendary member of their race who died before the flesh-based Necrontyr were transformed into the metallic and undying Necrons.

The theft leads to a tit-for-tat war between the two, one that- depending upon your generation- is reminiscent of Tom & Jerry, Spy vs. Spy, or Itchy and Scratchy. Any upper hand gained is momentary as the other strikes back to seize the initiative, in a millennia-spanning, ever-escalating feud between the two as they wrest the Mysterium back and forth.

As the terrible secrets of the Mysterios begin to reveal themselves, the two must begrudgingly work together to save the Necrons from a doom their own incessant warring has threatened to awaken.

Image credit: Games Workshop

The Three Ingredients

Rath harnesses three distinct elements that are tightly woven through the book, elevating it to the classic of the Black Library that it's come to be seen as.

First is the comedy. Typically when we think of humor in 40K we look at the Orks, but Rath pulls off a great trick here in writing a comedy that never actually feels like a comedy. Instead, it's a serious story that just happens to have moments that are genuinely hilarious, not least in the frequent banter between the two. They often sound like two clever schoolboys, each trying to make sure it's the other who gets in trouble with the principal.

The effectiveness of the humor is heavily contingent upon the second element- Rath's terrific writing and character work. You get a deep sense of both Trazyn and Orikan here as fully-dimensional characters. Their strengths and weaknesses, virtues and foibles, all richly rendered and threaded through the tale. Rath himself homed in on this idea, and how it reinforces the humor found in the book. "A lot of people have asked how this book manages to make one of the darkest universes imaginable into a comedy," he noted in the afterword of the 2024 Special Edition of the book, "and the answer is really very simple. Comedy flows from character, and while the galaxy isn't funny, Trazyn's perspective on it is. Like an entomologist watching two anthills at war, he's fascinated by the process, but not emotionally invested in the winner."

I'm being deliberately vague here to avoid any spoilers for the new reader, but perhaps my favorite example here was when Trazyn- who is able to shift his consciousness into other, lesser Necron- masquerades as one of Orikan's soldiers, affecting a deliberate slowness and stupidity sure to trigger the intellectual snobbishness of his rival. It's side-splittingly funny watching Orikan grow increasingly exasperated and on-tilt, but only because Rath has put in the work throughout the book of showing Orikan's smartest-guy-in-the-room arrogance.

The third element here I think is perhaps the most underrated, and that's Rath's sense of history. "From drafting legal memos, to scouring the archives of London, to hiking the Amazon, to searching for buried Confederate cannons in New Mexico," he once wrote of himself, "I’ll go anywhere and do anything to get the story." Rath is the Head Writer for Extra History, a YouTube history series with four-and-a-half million subscribers, and he brings this passion for the sweep and flow of history to his fiction.

"One of the greatest things about Warhammer is that it's a historical setting," he observed (again in the afterword). "I can think of no other worlds - with the possible exception of Tolkien's Middle-Earth or Herbert's Dune - with a timeline stretching back millions of years. Being able to explore [that] through the oculars of immortal beings was probably the thing I found most attractive about The Infinite and the Divine as a project. That we could take a single world as a microcosm of this twisted galaxy and highlight the frankly bonkers timescale of this universe, as well as playing with real historical concepts."

By centering his story on the timeless Necrons, Rath is in his element. Most of the tale unfolds on the world of Serenade, and as the years pass over the course of the book we see history in motion there. The presence of eels in one passage, for instance, presages the appearance of their fossils later. Remnants of the Aeldari Exodite civilization featured early on turn up in the Human one that eventually takes their place. "Civilisations, after all," he writes, "always build on the dead."

Ultimately, Rath pulls off an impressive double here, with incredible characters contained within a vibrantly-painted setting. This is a masterpiece of a book, well deserving of the esteem its garnered in the years following its release.

That also makes it the perfect book to select for the new Annotated and Illustrated Editions.

Special Edition Extras

Image credit: Games Workshop

One of the elements that I think gets overlooked in these editions is the additional art. They are after all, Illustrated Editions, though as readers perhaps it's natural for us to linger a bit more on the annotations that highlight the work itself rather than its presentation.

And to be sure, they're a subtle touch, gracing only the occasional page so as not to overcrowd the story, but rather to accentuate it.

But the illustrations for this edition of The Infinite and the Divine were supplied by Erin H. Rea, a freelance illustrator out of Edinburgh, Scotland, and she knocked it out of the park. Her dark imagery is suffused throughout with that emerald hue, an almost sinister shade that's very evocative of the Necrons themselves (as well as warpstone, interestingly enough). There's a certain haunting pensiveness here, a neat contrast from some of the more kinetic imagery we saw in the Eisenhorn trilogy previously released in this format. And given the longer lens of the story's timeline, Rea clearly understood the assignment.

In addition to her work here, Rea is no stranger to the worlds of Warhammer either. She's been an illustrator for Cubicle 7 Games since 2018, lending her talents to bringing their Warhammer role-playing games to visual life.

Then, of course, we've got the annotations. I wonder if there's something of a historian's drive for a complete record at work here, as Rath's 242 annotations far exceeds what we've seen in others of the series. Dan Abnett offered 89 of them in Hereticus1, Chris Wraight dotted his Lords of Silence with 81 of them, and Sandy Mitchell's For the Emperor contained 130. We shouldn't read too much into that- some of Abnett's were rather lengthy, for instance- but it nevertheless suggests that Rath was very keen to add some context, nuance, and understanding to his text.

And add he does! Some of his insights show admirable candor, particularly when it comes to the process of working with an editor. Indeed, I was very surprised to learn that Rath's initial concept for the book wasn't an adventure across the timeline so much as the galaxy itself. "A good editor can be an excellent creative partner who opens doors you didn't even see," writes Rath in annotation 97. "Great example: my original pitch for The Infinite and the Divine had it taking place on multiple worlds, a sort of scavenger hunt across the cosmos. My editor, Will Moss, suggested that instead, maybe we could return to the same world over a long period of time. And BOOM, with one line i an email the entire book snapped into focus."

Other annotations include writing advice and thoughts on the craft ("One of the most important steps in writing an action scene is getting the reader acquainted with the geography of the space"), historical insight ("Transformative figures are often controversial in their time, and only universally acknowledged as heroes after they're dead"), reader feedback "As many have pointed out..."), and fascinating tidbits like how a factory defect in a particular miniature had broad implications for Rath's conception of the character (#174).

Final Thoughts

I've been a huge fan of the Illustrated and Annotated Edition format, and thrilled to see them continue. The Infinite and the Divine is a worthy inclusion, not just a terrific story in its own right but also with a lot of effort on behalf of Rath to point at the many things in the text readers might not have caught on their first read-through and understand the fullness of the story.

It's also fascinating when you stop to consider just how unlikely all this is. This was Rath's first novel for the Black Library, one where he swung for the fences- and connected. Not only that, but the Illustrated and Annotated format itself was never a guarantee. After the first one, Dan Abnett's Xenos, it took nearly three years for them to try for a second one. Yet now, after the one in late 2024, we had two in 2025 and are on course for three this year.

The Black Library took a chance on Rath, took a chance on Illustrated and Annotated Editions, and with both paying off wonderfully it's fitting them come together here.

Footnote

  1. Xenos and Malleus did not have running annotations; the annotation numbers would reset in each chapter. Obtaining those would have required a manual calculation.
