The Paint Review: Squidmar Color

November 24th, 2026 via Goonhammer

By now, we’re all familiar with prominent miniature artists having their own signature sets, but Squidmar takes things to another level entirely with a full range of paints bearing their name, in collaboration with Acrylicos Vallejo. I know what you’re thinking: Is this just a collection of already-existing Vallejo paints, bundled into a set with a new label? According to Squidmar themselves, the colours are all brand new formulations, rather than rebadged Game Color paints. Happily, since I already have all the Game Color paints, I can test the validity of that assertion.

It’s always a huge risk to launch a new paint line; the path of success is littered with the bodies of paint lines that burst onto the scene, launched ambitious Kickstarters and failed; or those that made big promises, failed to deliver, and faded into obscurity. Partnering with an experienced industry staple like Vallejo is a very astute move, allowing Squidmar to sidestep the headache of dealing with a paint manufacturer that isn’t already well-versed in the miniatures world.

Thank you to Squidmar and Acrylicos Vallejo for providing a sample copy of the Mega Set for review.

The entire range comprises 72 bottles: 48 regular acrylic paints, 7 metallics (not on the swatch card), 5 fluorescent paints, and 12 inks.

A quick glance at the swatches shows this to be a pretty feature-complete paint set, rather than an expansion set or otherwise - pretty much everything you need to paint the kind of colourful dark fantasy on display in the box art is here. The palette is broad and features plenty of saturated spectral colours - anyone who knows me knows that I cannot resist a good turquoise and blue palette, and Deep Blue Sea, Ghastly Turquoise, and Ocean Eyes are wonderful, especially when combined with Ghostly Ink.

The teal trio of Corrupted Emerald, Gemstone, and Spearmint also have a lot to recommend them - although they’re quite similar to the Scurvy Green BSL quartet from the main Game Color line, I actually prefer these, since they’re a little further over to the blue end of the scale than the green. If there’s one area in which the colour selection is somewhat lacking, it’s probably the greens, with two olive-y browny- greens, two straightforward warmish greens, and one cold green - since green is such a complex colour, it would have been nice to include a few more, but it’s understandable in that the regular Game Color line already has most of those bases well covered.

The skin tones also have some pretty nice options amid them as well; I don’t know what a Dunskar (presumably some kind of dwarf) is, but Dunskar Skin is a lovely, luminous pink flesh tone, while Northern Tan makes for a great, pale highlight colour - when combined with Warm Grey from the main Game Color line, it’d make an excellent Drukhari skin tone recipe.  Steve Flesh makes a good all-rounder base for skin, while Sun Kissed Skin occupies the warm and punchy Sunny Skin Tone vibe with which we’re all familiar.

Other standouts in the spectral colours include: Tulip Fever – an exceptionally punchy and bright light violet colour, and Tentacle Pink - an Emperor’s Children-esque bold pink with lots of saturation and smoothness.

Thirteen of the acrylics are either brown or grey, with some great standouts: Snow Storm functions great as a white ice-blue pastel, ideal for mixing cold highlights or just painting a cold white highlight. Ancient Tome works very well as a drab brown for basecoating and shading old, musty parchment and bone. Blackwood does exactly what it says on the tin, providing a nice shadowy forest wooden tone.

Excitingly, alongside the Game Color base of the ordinary colours, the metallic included in the range boast of possessing the same technology as the much-lauded Vallejo True Metallic Metal paints. Metallic Metal is a stupid name, granted, but it functions very nicely as a quasi-Leadbelcher and goes on very smoothly, the perfect base for any steely/irony metal.

Godly Steel is exceptionally bright and shiny, sitting in the Stormhost Silver area of the graph, but suffers a little from being splotchy and watery if you’re not very careful. Ungodly Copper, meanwhile, is a very fitting option for your Decayed Metal style dark bronzes and coppers, fitting very well alongside the muted brown tones of some of my favourite dark bronzes. That said, the metallics aren’t massively different from pre-existing True Metallic Metals - this was somewhat inevitable, since the parent range is already so expansive. This probably won’t be a problem to anyone, but it’s worth noting anyway.

The inks are excellent; the colours available add a ton of punch with their high saturation and move across surfaces easily with great flow characteristics - since they’re fully transparent, I’ve made use of them as airbrush filters and glazes and, although I’m not overly fond of the kind of glossiness you have to expect from an ink, they perform very nicely. It’s important to emphasise how saturated they are, though; if you’re not careful with your dilution, they can easily overwhelm an underpainting and squash all the contrast out of it. Deep Sea Ink is a particular standout for me; I’m an absolute sucker for an oceanic blue with the right amount of green; I used it to airbrush volumetric shadows on my Valayan Gholems. Ghostly Ink is similar, but sits in a brighter turquoise mode; I love them both, and they’ll absolutely become a mainstay of my painting.

Airbrushed shadows with Squidmar Deep Sea Ink

Fluos are always a bit of a crapshoot in my experience; some manufacturers just “get” them and some really don’t, and while these aren't the best fluos I've ever used, they do the job just fine. When I was painting High Kyng Valankrom and his war throne, I used the yellow, turquoise, orange, and green Squidmar Fluorescents to paint the glow on the respective god-face statues - by and large, they pack a good amount of punch. The turquoise is the only one that's lacking, and that's largely a result of physics. All blue fluorescent paints struggle to glow as a function of the part of the spectrum they reflect, so I can't hold the relatively weak performance of Fluorescent Ghost against it too much.

The mega set also contains five miniatures from the Squidmar fictional universe of Sona; these are scaled-down soft-plastic versions of the large-scale resin display pieces they made some years ago. I’ve seen a bit of buzz online about these lacking sharpness and detail - I think this might be the result of people seeing photos of the large-scale miniatures and being confused, certainly the miniatures in my box are detailed enough, it’s more akin to Reaper Bones than GW plastic but it’s fine for all that. The characters are well defined and make a great platform for trying out the paints. I doubt anyone is buying this set just for the miniatures, but they are nice to have anyway.

So the answer to the big question about this collaboration: Are Squidmar paints just the same as existing Game Color paints with the labels swapped? The answer to that one is easy: No, they aren't already existing Game Color paints - while there are some very close cousins among the selection, nothing is “the same” as an existing Game Color paint. It would perhaps be more reasonable and accurate to consider them more of a parallel extension of Game Color with a specific theme (somewhat dark, comic fantasy style), and a very significant one at that; with 48 new regular acrylics, the Squidmar range increases the existing Game Color palette by over 50% when taken together.

I am already an enormous fan (and frequent user) of the newly reformulated Game Color paints, and although some of the earlier ones had issues with bubbling, I’m happy to report that the Squidmar paints don’t suffer from the same foaming-up in the recesses - if you give your miniature a tap on your desk or pop the bubbles with your brush, you shouldn’t have too many problems. The formula thins down very nicely for glazing and airbrushing with a little medium, and yields a nice, smooth finish and soft colour transitions.

The Squidmar series doesn’t have any triad or BSL system associated with it, which is slightly lacking compared to the regular Game Color series and might make things somewhat harder for beginners, since so much of miniature painting education is built around the use of a more systematic approach. It also contains a selection of inks instead of washes, which I’m absolutely fine with, but might frustrate beginner painters if they try to use them in the same way.

The Squidmar Mega Set retails for around 300 euros, which, for 72 paints and five miniatures (including one big one), isn’t bad. The Squidmar paints are straightforwardly more expensive than the regular Game Color paints (each bottle costs about 80 cents more than ordinary Game Color), but they still come in well under the cost of price-leading, smaller-bottle brands like Warhammer (and are less than half the price of Tone Pro) and Two Thin Coats.

In addition to the Mega Set, Vallejo offers an “Essentials” set, which, as you might expect, is a stripped-down version of the main offering. The essentials set excises all of the inks and fluos, 2 of the metallics, and 23 of the regular acrylics, leaving a selection of 30 paints, plus four of the miniatures (the Essentials set doesn’t include the big owl), with an RRP of 163 euros - this price is essentially the same as the BSL Color set, but with four miniatures instead of 18 paints. In addition, some (but not all) of the Squidmar paints are available in quartets, similar to how they sell TMM and Game Color paints - at the time of writing, these sets consist of: NMM Gold, NMM Steel, Leather, Demon Skin, Orc Skin, Fairy Skin, Elf Skin, and Dark Skin, retailing for 16 euros each, about 4 euros per set more than regular Game Color, which is consistent with the price of the individual bottles compared to Game Color.

In terms of cost, if you were to buy all 72 of the Squidmar Color paints individually, it’d come to the same price as the Mega Set, so the set-related savings all hinge on the value you place on the five miniatures and a free brush, which certainly will vary from person to person - to me, the miniatures are a nice little extra. Would I buy them on their own? With the exception of the owl, probably not. The main event is obviously the paints, and those are above reproach. I love Game Color already, and the Squidmar paints offer the same high-quality, deep, rich saturation, and ease of use as their Game Color cousins - the Squidmar Colors are absolutely worth your money, even at the slightly increased price.

Pros:

  • Deeply saturated colours
  • Diverse and feature-complete palette that can also be an extension to regular Game Color
  • Same excellent Game Color formula

Cons:

  • Slightly increased price over ordinary Game Color
  • Metallics not massively different from already-existing TMMs.
  • No triad system or washes.
