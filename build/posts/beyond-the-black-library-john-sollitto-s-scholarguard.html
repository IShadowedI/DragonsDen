Beyond the Black Library: John Sollitto's Scholarguard

August 21st, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: John Sollitto

The Warhammer universe is forged by some of the sharpest minds in speculative fiction. But what do they create when they step outside Games Workshop’s sandbox? In Beyond the Black Library, we cast a keen eye on the different fictional sandboxes and original worlds our favorite creators build when they aren't writing for the Imperium or the Mortal Realms.

While my earliest exposure to Warhammer fiction was many years ago now (Dan Abnett's Eisenhorn Omnibus), for the past two years I have been absolutely marinading my brain in the grimdark. While I've taken the occasional detour, one of the fun side-effects is that when you come across something light-hearted and whimsical, it can shine like a beacon on a moonless night. Scholarguard proved to be a terrific palate-cleanser that I didn't know I'd needed, but came as a breath of fresh air.

Getting Cozy

Like any other consumer market, speculative fiction isn't immune to trends. The one we're currently weathering (for better or for worse) at the moment is the LitRPG phenomenon, with a deluge of stories ranging from inspiration to imitation riding the wave of success currently enjoyed by Matt Dinniman's Dungeon Crawler Carl. While Scholarguard has nothing in common with the adventures of Princess Donut's Royal Court, it does have some of the genetics of a contemporary trend that still has a bit of life in it: the cozy fantasy.

Exemplified by books like The House in the Cerulean Sea by T. J. Klune and Travis Baldree's Legends & Lattes, these stories tend to tack in the complete opposite direction of your more traditional epic fantasy and grimdark tales. They tend to have low-stakes plots that focus on the magically mundane, everyday life in a fantasy world, and often spend more time on interpersonal relationships over combat or the intrigues of politics. Baldree's story, for example, involves a grizzled orc adventurer hanging up her sword to open up a coffee shop that becomes a haven for the community. Low stakes for sure, but for many that's part of the charm. 

And while 'escort quests' tend to be the bane of MMO's like World of Warcraft, it's exactly the kind of low-stakes, character-driven story John Sollitto aimed for with Scholarguard.

The Story

The story centers on Hunter Harcutt, a young aspiring adventurer in Autumngate looking for his first real contract to get his foot in the door at the Adventurer's Guild. When an elven scholar named Ianthoris Embergleam turns up at the inn that Hunter is working at looking for an escort back to his home city of Riverhall a hundred kilometers way, Hunter thinks he's found just the job.

What seems a simple and straightforward journey turns out to be anything but, as the scholar has in his possession a very rare scroll. A very rare necromantic scroll, the kind that less savory and scrupulous types would kill to get their hands on. Embergleam came across it in an old bookstore and, recognizing it for what it was, vowed to see it safely back to the academy for safekeeping.

Naturally, his acquisition draws exactly that kind of less savory and scrupulous attention, and with thugs, assassins, and a criminal necromancer hot on their heels Hunter and Embergleam must race to Riverhall before their pursuers relieve them of their treasure- and quite possibly their lives.

Image credit: Veritas

About John

While this is naturally a fantasy tale, Black Library readers will likely be more familiar with Sollitto's work in the grim, dark future. To date he's penned a trio of Warhammer 40K stories that have been featured in various anthologies.

His debut story, Redemption Through Blood, was originally released as a digital short as part of 2024's Daughters of the Emperor Week. It then saw its print debut the following year in the Paragon of Faith and Other Stories anthology (review). That was followed by The Redoubt, a Guardsmen-verses-tau tale which was one of the three brand-new stories contained in Death and Duty: An Astra Militarum Anthology (review).

Finally, the terrific World Ablaze anthology (review) from earlier this year gave us Rok Crackers, putting a different group of Guardsmen- these being combat engineers- up against the greenskin menace inside one of their asteroid bases.

Beyond Warhammer, he's also written an anthology of his short stories, The Ones Who Remain, and has done extensive work in comics including writing for Veritas, Certifiable Investigations, and Circuit Champ.

Scholarguard, notes John, was inspired by old-school pulp fantasy as well as countless hours spent playing Dungeons & Dragons.

So given the pedigree, how does it hold up?

All The Small Things

As I joined Hunter and Embergleam on their trek, what stood out to me the most was the contrast I've seen in his Warhammer stories. I don't want to call it "growth" because I don't know the timeline of his work, but the contrast is certainly suggestive of an author much more comfortable in his powers. Don't get me wrong, I've liked Sollitto's small (but growing) body of Black Library tales, but in each review there's been something I pointed to in that story that I felt it might have used- and here in Scholarguard it was delivered in spades.

For example, take his most recent story, Rok Crackers. I had some pacing concerns here, noting in my review that while the tale had a terrific premise, "if I had any complaint, it was that there wasn't quite enough of it. At times I felt like I was listening to a podcast on 1.25 speed, the tale moving just a little too fast."

Scholarguard never drags; its sequences don't overstay their welcome. But there's a deliberate slowness to some of them that lets the reader enjoy the scene the same way you might linger at a painting in a museum that's caught your eye, enjoying all its fine details. Sollitto's novel plays these to great effect, and that's where some of the 'cozy' feeling can come from.

In one of the book's best moments, the pair of heroes find shelter in a cottage in the woods that's been overtaken by nature, its original inhabitant long deceased. As Hunter explores this house that was once a home, Sollitto pulls the reader into the scene's fine details, drawing an almost affectionate portrait of the original owner. He reflects over the burial plot in the yard1, discovers the original owner's tranche of charcoal and sketchbooks filled with Audubon-like depictions of nature, and stares at the stars in the sky through the holes in the slowly-collapsing roof as he goes to sleep. Upon waking, he even attempts a sketch of a nearby bird himself. It's a wonderful respite from the story's action, as by now the intrepid duo have found they're being pursued.

It never feels overindulgent, but these details make the story shine and breathe with a richness of life.

Then reviewing The Redoubt, I noted that "a little more depth on our characters would not have been unwelcome." While it's undoubtedly true that the longer word count of a novel certainly affords greater opportunity for deep characterization than a short story, Sollitto uses the space well in particular with his main protagonist, Hunter. The story's premise- a an aspiring adventurer on his first quest- has plenty of opportunity to fall into the easy cliches of the genre.

Sollitto avoids the easy tropes, and does the actual work of building with depth. While Hunter does get into some swordfights, for instance, he's no young master swordsman with full metal plate-worthy plot armor. These skirmishes tack towards the realistic side- short, bloody affairs that carry only slightly less significant threat of injury to the victor as to the vanquished. For much of the story, Hunter has to balance the duty to push on with his quest with the physical toll his injuries and dwindling supplies are taking on him.

Indeed, there's just enough grit here to push the book outside the confines of YA fiction, but not so much that I couldn't easily recommend it to my older kids. The Hunter that ends the journey is a different young man than the one that started it, and by doing the work along the way it all feels very consistent.

Final Thoughts

If there's a place I did find the book a bit light, it was in its worldbuilding. Putting it simply, there really isn't much. With the exception of a few deities which have a light presence in the story, there's not a lot that made Scholarguard's world feel as alive and detailed as its inhabitants. It's all very standard-issue, generic fantasy-land with the usual D&D array of orcs, half-orcs, elves, half-elves, dwarves, and halflings. These distinctions are also largely cosmetic, with little impact on character or story, and if you rewrote it changing everyone to humans you wouldn't lose (or gain) a thing.

By the same token, it also means that we are spared the risk of what many over-eager aspiring worldbuilders do, liberally dolloping in the lore dumps on the reader like there's going to be a test at the end of the book. In the absence of a rich world or high-stakes plot, Sollitto leans on his characters and eye for painting the fine details to get the reader invested in the story, and in the main it he gets it right.

Ultimately, the main test for me of books like these comes down to, "would I read the next one?" In this case, I certainly would. If Sollitto continue to show the growth as a writer we're already seeing, I'll be delighted to see what he comes up with for Hunter's next adventure.

As for the Black Library, Scholarguard shows that Sollitto's can carry a novel, and while I'd never turn down seeing more of the grim, dark future it would be fun to see what he could do with the Age of Sigmar side of things. But if you're looking for a fun, pulpy fantasy novel with a delightful amount of depth, this one's worth a look!
