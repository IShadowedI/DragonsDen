Hobby Build Academy: How to Handle a Backlog

November 2nd, 2026 via Goonhammer

Welcome back to Hobby Build Academy, where your professors Marcy and Elly explore the world of hobby model kit building and all of the ups and downs that come with it. We've been focusing on building out tools, useful things to have, and reviewing kits as we build them, but perhaps keen readers have noticed something: We haven't had many model reviews lately! Well, we'll talk a bit about why that is, and that also gave us the time to talk about something that seems to always circle hobby activities: the dreaded backlog. But is a backlog actually a bad thing, and how best to think about it regardless of the answer?

Faculty Lounge

This guy WILL be built.

Marcy: So, I think it isn't such a bad idea to open up this week's talk with some of the reasons as to why we've had a little lull in actual model building as of late; if anything, I think it's a great way to explore the fact that backlogs can build up very quickly, and also that a lot of things that we can't control often play a big role in them. I see people talk about them from the perspective of laziness or personal failure, but I think the actual reality is that our hobbies tend to always play a secondary role that, unfortunately, tends to get forced to the sideline.

Elly: Yeah... so, I haven't talked about it much here, but I suffer from a few chronic illnesses, which tend to greatly impact my available energy and focus. That tends to mean I have to prioritize my work first, and my other activities second, otherwise there's a chance I won't get things done that absolutely need to get done. And, well, I have a lot of interests, but also what energy I have left can sometimes be pretty variable too. On days when I have a lot of energy, I might prefer to go out or be more active, and in others, I might prefer to relax and read or play a game. Models have been... kind of an interesting challenge.

Marcy: For myself, I tend to be juggling a lot of things and spinning a lot of plates, so my downtime is generally pretty restricted. Actually, one of the reasons we started this column and this hobby together was to have something we specifically do together that isn't just an overlap of our interests, but something we do together. So, this means that when your energy isn't where it needs to be when my time is free, or the opposite, it can make working on things together difficult.

Elly: Then there's the attention and focus, which are... another problem, hah.

Marcy: Yeah. And I don't think there's much wrong with talking about that either; while you can have a larger struggle reigning in both of those, my focus can by laser pointed, but that doesn't mean I have the time to engage with things.

Elly: Which is to say, there's been some other things going on too.

Marcy: Yeahhhhhhhh. After September ended, we had picked up quite a few new models, as the end of the month is my birthday, so we'd gone on a bit of a trip to get some new kits, and also attended the model show. Unfortunately, that got tied with an increase in my work schedule and a decrease in my downtime, but also my car suffered a pretty huge need for repair, which took a lot of my time, energy, and focus (as well as money) to circumvent.

Elly: So, on the bright side, we have a lot of models to build, but also, on the down side, we have... a lot of unbuilt models. And actually, I have one that's incomplete, because we were working on a new set of kits before things kind of snowballed and I hadn't had the time or energy to get back to finishing it.

Marcy: Right! So, now that our personal preamble is out of the way, let's talk about backlogs themselves; if anything, we mostly thought talking about ours and how it got created so fast so openly would be relatable, since there's often a problem with recognizing that it isn't very hard to amass one, thinking that they just sneak up or appear due to other factors.

Back to Backlogs

This guy, also, will be built. Eventually.

Marcy: So, a backlog, for anyone blissfully unaware of them, is a term used for a "pile" of figurative, or literal, projects or items that generally have built up over a period of time and inactivity. You'll sometimes hear about people joking about their Steam backlogs, for example, so it isn't that they have to huge physical piles of unbuilt models or minis, but in reality, it just has to be a set of things that keep getting forgotten and possibly added to over time.

Elly: Yeah... which can be hell on one's mental health when it comes to hobbying. The sense of guilt about a backlog can really create a pretty negative cycle in your head: "I didn't finish that, I guess I won't finish it, I feel bad for not finishing it," which can just keep taking those projects to further and further lengths of being unfinished. I know I've certainly had this problem myself so far, where I keep feeling like because I didn't finish a kit, I've become guilty of some sort of thing and it makes it harder to stay motivated to get back in. It sounds irrational as I write it out, but I think it might be relatable...

Marcy: No, I think it absolutely is; there's a lot of times in which something that hasn't been completed, just out of eye sight or concentration, starts building up some level of weight on its own that somehow being unfinished is a reminder. Something you didn't do, or finish, or complete.

Elly: The other thing with backlogs is that there's no real size to them; if anything, a single item that hasn't been interacted with can be a backlog! They don't all have to be mountains of grey plastic and shrink wrapped boxes. For us, we've got about ten models between us now that need to be built before we could consider our backlog emptied.

Marcy: Yeah, and... honestly, I could add a few more things to that. I have a Transformers LEGO kit that I haven't gotten around to working on that's been about a year now, for example, so... sometimes a backlog goes from 1, to 5, to 10! But at least ours is shared, so 10 isn't really as bad as it sounds in terms of gunpla.

Elly: I'd say that as we talked about backlogs, something that seemed to come to mind is that it doesn't really matter how big or small it is, or how it came to be, just that it exists, and that it existing can create further problems when it goes too long without being completed.

Managing a Backlog

And THIS one! We actually did track one down!

Marcy: So, in considering how to deal with a backlog, there isn't really a single magical answer that we can provide. Obviously, these are things that we know work for us, and are mostly just sharing them as ways to consider or think about them that might help turn something that seems insurmountable into something that can actually be conquered.

Elly: So, obviously, "just do it" is a simple answer, but it rarely is actually as easy as it seems. If "just do it" was that effective a mindset, people wouldn't have backlogs or other things stumbling their way otherwise. That said, there is some merit to actually setting yourself up to succeed when you fall behind on projects. As we were talking, the idea that "guilt" can keep you from finishing things really does become something that can get in the way of making progress. "I can do it later, I've already failed to do it" is a mindset, and so setting an actual time, making a promise or other sort of commitment, is probably the first, best step to getting real progress to happen.

Marcy: For me, that's generally how I approach my writing. I always try to write a few hundred words a day at least, if not a few thousand, and I developed that primarily because I stalled out on my PhD. years ago and realized if I didn't set something into a routine, I'd never finish it; ever since, I just keep at it like I would anything else. Making it into a routine, a thing that I keep up with, helped make my writing easy but also consistent. For hobbying, frankly, I actually still have the same problem; hobbying isn't something I have to do for my daily life to function, so it becomes really, really easy to forget about things.

Elly: Another thing we discussed is tackling projects in orders of magnitude. We have a few new kits, but of them, there are some RG and MG kits, but also a few SD kits, and so far our "return" to working on kits will likely be the SD kits. A smaller project that works with your backlog might just be an easy first step; I definitely know from other projects I've fallen behind on that trying to tackle the absolute biggest thing on the list often ends up as a mistake.

Marcy: Yeah, back when I was painting miniatures, I certainly fell into that trap a lot. Figuring that I'd become burnt out on smaller models and boring details, I kept trying to work off of larger, detailed projects, which only served to deepen my sense of guilt over not finishing things, as bigger models were harder to stay engaged with on my schedule. Sadly, I have a half painted zombie dragon to attest for that...

Elly:I think the hardest part of a backlog to wrestle with is... sometimes, you just have to admit that you've moved on. Thankfully we didn't reach that point with gunpla, or this column would stop existing, but I know both of us have had that in the past with other hobbies. When you realize that you are just actually never going to tackle those projects, it may not be because of not finding the time, it may just be that a loss of passion or interest has turned that "pile of shame" into a "pile of disinterest".

Marcy: Yep. And when that happens, it really does become time to be honest with yourself: sometimes, the best thing you can do is get rid of your backlog, literally. Selling, trading, or otherwise offloading your backlog to other people is a great way to actually remove a sense of guilt over the things you haven't finished. It can also help remove the monetary sense of guilt that a backlog creates too; by selling or trading the things you've really lost interest in, it can also alleviate a sense that you've wasted money too.

Elly: I do think it's the last resort, obviously. You don't want to rush to sell something, only to then realize you actually were just going to get to it eventually and have to re-buy it again!

Marcy: Overall, I do think there's no real answer to backlogs; we certainly aren't going to sit here and pretend we know how to prevent them. But, maybe some of this will help mange them. I don't think they're something to be ashamed of, just that letting them grow and grow is irresponsible. By actually taking steps to mitigate or approach the backlog, it becomes something that you can actually get rid of, rather than just "tolerate" as being out of sight, out of mind. We do hope to be back with builds soon, because we've got a few really cool kits we're excited to show off!
