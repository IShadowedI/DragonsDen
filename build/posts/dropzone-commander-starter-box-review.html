Dropzone Commander - Starter Box Review

September 21st, 2026 via Goonhammer

Hello and welcome to a review of the Dropzone introductory box, many thanks to TTC for sending a preview copy our way!

This is a rather substantial collection of sprues and the assorted accoutrement of rules and tools for playing the game once you’ve gotten enough models together to give it a spin. We’ll start into the box by diving into the models, as you’ll need to work through at least a few of those before you get to give the game itself a go and a fresh set of plastic is always exciting!

The Models

As this box is intended to be an introduction to a transport heavy game, this starter comes quite reasonably with sprues split between ground forces and the eponymous dropships that sling those forces into combat. Each side gets a pair of duplicate drop ship sprues that let you build a number of different ships classes, each with a handful of configurations, while the armies share a common ground forces sprue duplicated in sufficient number to fill out a small army of each side with some spares of a few units.

The plastic itself is about what you’d expect if you are coming from a GW game, good quality and with crisp castings that I didn’t spot any obvious miscasts on, though one bioficer model had a transparent layer of something on it that was a bit odd. The sprues are reasonably laid out, with parts that are numbered and easy enough to find, though models aren’t tightly grouped as one set of constituent parts instead a more common style of distributed across the sprue layout. Models aren’t enough pieces for this to be an overly large concern though, with each model usually only having a handful of individual pieces. The instructions are mostly straightforward, with a couple of cases of obscured steps that are slightly confusing, though the limited number of pieces and well matching part cuts mean that you can figure your way out of anything without much difficulty.

The Armies

Bioficers

A sampler of all the distinct hulls present for the Bioficers. Credit: Rockfish

Bioficers kick off strong with a ton of unique designs that admittedly can be a slightly confusing build, the ground stuff is few enough parts that it’s not too bad but you’ll definitely be flipping between the instructions and what you've got in your hands for the first few models. The aircraft might give you a bit of extra trouble as they are both large and look like an explosion in a girder factory, so you might find it tricky to give them time for the glue to set without something falling off. The end results are cool and worth the initial confusion you might encounter; every vehicle is distinct and would look great in a filled-out force.

One thing worth bringing up here is that the infantry for both sides of the box do feel a bit out of scale compared to the vehicles. This is clearly to let them have enough detail to enable painting and allow some degree of mix and match on each base but I think I’d probably have preferred lower detail smaller ones. Otherwise, the Bioficer infantry are solid, having relatively simple silhouettes on the bodies with easily read weapons, they aren’t the standout designs of the vehicles but nothing to complain about.

You will want to pick your battles as to how much effort you want to put into painting this side of the box as those distinctive designs do result in very greeble dense surfaces, this does mean that they should be good candidates for dry brushing! Though I recommend that with the caveat that you will want to phase out the sections you paint as the large panels in the designs do look very nice when clean and layered/blended.

UCM

The UCM side has the same number of distinct hulls and infantry types as the Bioficers. Credit: Rockfish

The UCM have some classic and fantastic looking staples of human forces in fiction, including dropships that I think may have won my love over the Bioficer equivelents, although admittedly the ground forces do have a few things that I found to be snaggles. Where Bioficers were able to hide the planer nature of injection molded sprues by being geometric constructs bursting to the seams with details and structure to obscure the initial state of the models, it’s much more obvious on the UCM units for better and worse because they are using more recognizable designs.

The lighter buggies and infantry both come out really great; having two similar body count infantry units at this scale is always going to be difficult to differentiate but the Legionaries and Pretorians have distinct enough garb and gear to be hard to mistake and shout out to the MA5 reference on the Legionaries being visibly different to space P90 of the Pretorians. The buggies come out pretty well too, you can only just tell their bodies are cast in one part by looking excessively closely at the wheels and with the turret you’ll never notice at all.

Things are still fine with the MBT as its hull is relatively simple in shape thanks to clearly having been inspired by modern vehicles, but there’s nothing inherently wrong with that and the turret is distinct enough you’ll get that nice bit of dimensionality to the model. The heavy tank unfortunately really suffers from the choice to make it one piece; it’s got the full combo of being a pancake with the main gun very clearly having no under cuts to split it from the hull at all. It’s disconcerting to compare it to the other units and I very much wish they’d given it a separate turret or even just split the upper hull off to give the model more depth, you can see the casting limitations far too much for being the centerpiece of the UCM ground forces.

Getting back to things I do like, the UCM drop ships are great. They use all the perfected classic design elements that were created all the classics you’ve ever loved, Aliens, Halo and every generic sci-fi movie, show or game you’ve seen the previous iterations of this concept but there’s nothing inherently wrong with that. A classic chubby little drop ship with a belly full of tank or infantry is such a fixture of the genre for a good reason! A minor complaint I’ll have on these and all the UCM vehicles is they did go a little hard on the greebling, they could have pulled back just a touch without losing any of the flavor the designs are already rich with.

The Wider Picture

Unfortunately, it’s time that an elephant is going to smash its way into the room, the flight stands the box come with are extremely poor. While the plastic rods and the rubbery piece that attaches to the ship are just as good as they were back when Dropfleet popped up, sadly here they are paired with an awful acrylic base. It's a simple laser cut square that the stick doesn’t even fit nicely into. I wasn’t impressed when I last painted a ship for my Bioficer fleet and they are no better here. I’d suggest looking to see if you can find the old bases on the secondary market or maybe using GW bases that you bore the center out a bit, as these do not glue together well and can’t be used without glue at all.

All complaints aside, I think you’ll be happy to paint either side out of this box, but I lean a little more towards the more distinctive vehicles on the Bioficer side. There’s plenty to enjoy in the plentiful plastic and most of the issues I had with the models themselves can be overlooked, plus you’ll not really have any issues with the physical act of painting as there aren’t any pockets hiding details away from you. I would have loved to get a few more things painted up in time for this article, but I'm afraid I spent rather a lot of the last month and a half sick such is life.

Supporting Infrastructure

Credit: TTCombat

They very much tried to lean into the idea of this being a starter box and for TTC that means packing in everything you need to get your first few games going, including a mat, cardboard buildings, and some tokens alongside the rules and models. How useful you find these extras will vary a bit as I found the paper mats a bit too unruly to place such light models on and the cardboard buildings are basic, they aren’t going to sit quite right or hold up to much abuse but are good enough to get started. Given you can skip the use of a mat at all or use one you have for another 6’ x 4’ game, looking at you 40k long timers, that’s not too big an issue and having enough terrain to get started is great even if you might want to upgrade before long.

The only thing missing here, at least in our review copy, is the command deck. While I think this is probably the right choice for your first handful of games, it still feels like a bit of a miss given how the box is otherwise set up to get a reasonably accurate representation of the elements in a normal game. The other thing that could have been nice to have is some simple zone templates, but the buildings meant I didn’t feel like I was missing too much here given they could be made by cutting out cardboard or paper shapes.

The Rules

Credit: TTCombat

Where mass market games like 40k have gotten increasingly word heavy and leaning ever harder on specificity to ensure the minimum of possible variants of interpretation, Dropzone is still in the design space where suggestions to be reasonable about the arcs of fire of a model and physical interactions with the board is the norm. This is honestly a good place to be for playing with your buddies, as you don’t need to engage rules lawyer brain when trying to get a fun game in with your friends but does risk some excessively argument heavy experiences with a certain sort of player.

The core rules are straightforward and common sense in general with moving and shooting that will feel familiar to anyone coming from 40k. When you get to the features more distinct to this game you will also notice how Dropzone shares a lot of DNA with Dropfleet, which is a game I’ve always enjoyed. Similar to Dropfleet, the core game loop takes place over six turns in which players alternate activating groups of units, each of which is made up of one to four squads that, hopefully given the game name, ride in a common transport. When activated, that group of units gets to move and attack, though not necessarily in that order, after their transport helpfully delivers them onto the board.

As you can expect, there’s a number of ancillary rules to help bring in more texture to the game:

  • Commanders grant your army buffs through a deck building mechanic we couldn’t explore with our review copy but look to give a variety of tools themed to each army.
  • Infantry can nesting doll their way into dropships or APC within other transports before hopping out into zones for objective play, skirmish with their counterparts in their own sub phase of an activation and can plink at armored targets when they have spare time.
  • Zones which represent various buildings or areas that have significance to the scenario and may contain scoring objectives or features that might be weapons or offer utilities to the army in control of them.
  • Ground and air units are almost different ‘levels’ with dropships only threatened by AA weapons or each other, which helps to encourage them to scoop and their charges up and across the board.

While I prefer the choices introduced by Dropfleets restrictions on movement and shooting, the Dropzone style makes for games where your forces will be in a scuffle fast. Transports are fast enough to toss their contents across a good chunk of the board which when combined with a unit fresh out of dropship being unable to attack leads to games where it’s reasonable to expect players to spend their first turn chucking out units around the board before turning on the firehose turn two. Although I found that there weren’t quite enough limitations beyond the factions in the box having weapons that are mostly short range to prevent things from turning into brawls near objectives, with the alternating nature of combat it’s easy to fall into a trap of players taking turns smashing units that emerge from cover to engage or hop on objectives.

To mitigate this a bit, the box comes with both a card of starter scenarios alongside a more substantial set in the book that have more complex scoring and table set ups that encourage more thoughtful gameplay. I don’t think I’d be too concerned with running out of distinct missions to play once you have an army and terrain collection built up a bit more, but the starter layouts are rather basic and unbalanced. Usually, these sorts of limited terrain boxes do lead to that situation, but by limiting themselves to using the included mats printed road layouts it makes for rather uneven building distribution or clear sight lines between objectives.

Tusk Tank. Credit: Rockfish

From my admittedly limited time with the game, I’d suggest avoiding scenarios with primarily end game or vehicle-based objectives, they can result in most of the game feeling a little mindless with forces beating each other up as scoring is directly linked to how much of your force remains alive. This can leave infantry occupied with rather unimportant fights inside buildings or plinking tanks, which maybe feels a little awkward given how infantry is a bit more of a rules heavy component of the game than their equivalent in Dropfleet. With some more experience I expect games can come a series of skirmishes with forces that strike before being pulled out by dropships in a back-and-forth exchange where players attempt to cut down each other’s forces while trying to hold enough in reserve for a late game push. Personally, I prefer the progressive scoring scenarios with a minimum of unit restrictive scoring, as end game scoring with only certain units able to be involved tends to result in skewed player incentives that distract from the otherwise fast pace of play.

I would suggest being a bit strategic when introducing the game to new players as the UCM side runs with conventional rules out of the book exactly as you’d expect, the Bioficiers on the other hand get an entire extra sheet of rules you need to learn to play. Lean into your inner 13-year-old and be the evil villain doing weird stuff and give your friend the UCM for the first round or two so they can focus on just learning the game. Nothing on the Bioficers is particularly complex to wrap your head around, it’s mostly mechanics to support a theme of drones puppeteered and/or spawned by support units along with being able to respawn infantry after killing enemies but it adds another layer of complexity.

While I do think you need to go into this game being aware that you’re probably not going to be seeing any serious competitive tournaments popping up near you, and you might need to be a game ambassador in your region, you will have a game that is a good excuse to spend a couple of hours pushing pretty models around a board with a friend. Speaking of, shout out to my friend Connor for taking time to help me try out the game in the busy end of summer time!
