[40k] 11 Biggest Changes in 11th Edition

June 1st, 2026 via Goonhammer
We’re at the cusp of a whole new edition for Warhammer 40k, and that’s gonna mean learning a bunch of new rules, forgetting some old ones, and figuring out what’s changed. There’s nothing the Internet loves more than an arbitrarily ordered numbered list, so today we’re gonna go over the 11 biggest changes in 11th edition. We've got plenty of coverage of the rules as-is, but stay tuned, you just might have missed something. 

The Video Version

11 Biggest Changes in 11th Edition

1. Objectives


One change that GW’s been very happy to talk about is the shift from capturing objective markers to capturing terrain, with the terrain footprints becoming the objectives themselves. That means you’re not going to have units standing on circles behind ruins, but actually occupying those pieces of terrain and holding them. Rules that incentivize targeting units on objectives are going to have a field day at launch, since the places to hide are also where you’re most likely to get blasted, so enjoy that, Votann players. The Objective Control characteristic might also matter a bit more here, since more models are going to be able to fit onto a larger terrain footprint than on a standard neoprene marker. Objective Secured is now in the core rules too, so expect to see that become a more standard rule across the game instead of just living on a few specific datasheets and stratagems. As for your old neoprene objective markers? Well, they’re pretty disappointing as frisbees, but you can always use them as coasters or mousepads. 

2. Terrain and Cover

As for terrain, it’s familiar but changed in a few ways. Terrain is broken up into Exposed, Light, and Dense terrain. Exposed doesn’t come up much in the rules, but covers stuff like craters or more aesthetic scatter terrain. Light is stuff like barricades, pipes, and other small pieces of terrain that can provide cover without blocking line of sight. Dense is the kind with the most impact on the gameplay, and that’s stuff like ruins, cargo containers, intact buildings, and woods. You can even hang out on top of these pieces of terrain so long as the model can stand on it, their base doesn’t overhang, and they’re infantry, swarm, beast, flying, or monster models. These pieces of terrain are going to be on footprints, by and large, which define their terrain area, and provide The Benefit of Cover to some of the units hanging out inside of them. The Benefit of Cover provides a flat -1 Ballistic Skill now, as opposed to improving the armor save of the models standing in it, making re-rolls that much more important, and AP-1 weapons that much more dangerous.

Only infantry, beasts, and swarms can actually get the benefit of cover from hanging out in terrain, and they only get cover if every model in the unit is in the terrain footprint, or barring that, is otherwise not completely visible to the attacking unit. The only way vehicles and monsters are getting cover is by being obscured behind a footprint, a piece of terrain, or through special rules and abilities like Smoke. In some ways that’ll make finding cover for a vehicle easier, since you just need to be partially obscured, and you won’t be trying to finagle your vehicles to fit onto terrain footprints just so. Those dense pieces of terrain also are treated as Solid, so you’re not going to be able to see through windows in your ruins or whatever. Most tournaments had this rule in place already, but now it’s codified in the core rules. Swarms can now move through Dense terrain, same as infantry, and vehicles can go through terrain if they have the movement to completely go up, over, and clear of the terrain model. 

People have already been murmuring that this would be a melee edition, and we’re not so sure about that. You see, if any part of a model’s base is on a terrain footprint, it can see and be seen through it. If you’ll remember 10th edition, a model had to wholly be within a terrain feature to see out of it. That might sound like splitting hairs, but that means a model or unit can just toe into a piece of terrain and shoot out of it, which means slower or larger units can bring their guns to bear more easily, and nobody needs to try and jam a Repulsor Executioner onto a footprint the size of a postcard. 


Credit: Games Workshop

For the entirety of 10th edition, I’ve been convinced that Plunging Fire was not real and could not hurt me. It only applied when a unit shot from a piece of terrain that was 6” or taller, and that just didn’t happen much on Matched Play layouts - when it did, it gave the shooting unit a bonus to their AP. Now it applies to any terrain 3” or taller, and while that sort of stretches the meaning of the word “plunging”, it means it’ll actually happen pretty much any time you’ve got a unit standing on a piece of terrain. When they do, they gain a point of Ballistic Skill. Towering units have this whenever targeting a unit within 12”, but aircraft never get plunging fire, even though I’d imagine their fire plunges more than anyone else’s. 

3. Detachments and Army Construction

Force Dispositions

The single biggest factor affecting what you put in your army is the Detachment system. In 10th edition, you’d ideally find a Detachment good at doing a particular job, and build your army around that. Now, each Detachment has a Detachment Point cost, and you have 3 Detachment Points to spend at army construction. That means you can mix and match with your Detachments, maybe going all in on a 3-pointer for something similar to what you used to do, or opting for 1 and 2 point options to build out for a different mix of abilities. Instead of having a full suite of stratagems and enhancements, those cheaper Detachments will generally have a less impactful rule and just a handful of stratagems and enhancements to dole out, some of which can even be applied to units that aren’t characters. Each of those Detachments also has an associated Disposition associated with it that will inform what missions you play, which we’ll get to in a minute - missions changed a lot too. This system opens up loads of options for players, though I personally fear it might lead to some balance issues, where certain combos might interact in unexpected ways and cause some problems. 

Also, characters are attached to units at army construction, as opposed to once you hit the table. People kinda did this anyway, since you usually took a character with the intent of attaching them to a specific unit most of the time, but now it’s a hard rule. When those characters do attach to units, they’re generally broken down into Leader and Support units. If you remember how a Space Marine Captain and Lieutenant could join a bunch of units in 10th, it’s like that, but more universal, with characters gaining one of the two key words depending on their role. One wrinkle is that the character and bodyguard units count as a single unit at army construction, so in situations where the bodyguard unit is wiped out and your character is still left alive, they still count as the same unit, meaning any abilities that can bring dead models back will still work. That’s right, those Wraiths and Technomancers will be harder to kill than ever. Your characters’ abilities also still work when they’re alone, so your Chaplains will remember how to hit harder when they’re not surrounded by their running crew. 


 

4. Missions

Credit: Games Workshop

Gone are the days of just picking Mission A, Layout 1 in the Tabletop Battles App - I’ve seen the data, I know how you live - and now your missions are picked based on your army’s disposition. There’s five of these - Take and Hold, Disruption, Purge the Foe, Priority Assets, and Reconnaissance - and your missions are going to be decided based on what Disposition both you and your opponent are running. You secretly pick those Dispositions, reveal them before the game, and that’ll determine what you and your opponent are trying to do for the rest of the battle. Most importantly, those missions are going to be different for each player, so you’re not trying to achieve the same goals. There’s 15 possible combinations, and thoughtful players will need to plan out how their army will score any given mission while building their list. Those Primary Missions also max at 45 points instead of 10th edition’s 50. Naturally, you still get 10 points for Battle Ready - paint your models, people - and the other 45 points are reserved for Secondary Objectives.

Secondaries should look familiar but have also changed in a meaningful way. The specific missions on them aren’t all too different from their 10th edition counterparts, but you don’t have a two card hand limit anymore. That means you could fail to score a card in one turn and cash in big on the next, scoring three. Note that if you meet the conditions of the card, you can score it, but don’t have to, should you want to hold on for later or something. That might not always be the best choice since you can only score a maximum of 15 victory points each for secondary and primary missions in a turn, but you do you. You can still spend a Command Point in your Command Phase to ditch one and draw again, but you can only do this once per game. Alternatively, you can still pitch one at the end of your turn for a Command Point, or at the end of your opponent’s turn, but there’s not too much reason to do that since you don’t have a hand limit anymore. As for fixed Secondary Objectives, they’re still here, letting you pick two cards and score a max of 20 points for each. Oh, and Twists are still here. You still probably won’t use them.

5. Charging

When you wanted to Charge before, you’d pick a target, roll 2D6 for charge distance, and if you didn’t make it, your dudes just had to hang around twiddling their power-armored thumbs, even if something else was closer. Now, you declare that a unit is going to Charge, roll those 2D6, and choose any enemy units within that distance as charge targets. This is similar to how Age of Sigmar does it, and should lead to fewer situations where your jacked up melee unit is just stuck sitting around because it couldn’t make a risky long bomb Charge. When you make that Charge, each model has to end up closer to one of your Charge Targets, within 1” of one or more of said targets if they can, and every model must end in Engagement Range if they have the movement to do so. That Engagement Range is 2” instead of 1”, so more models are likely getting to fight. It might not mean you always end up in combat with the thing you initially wanted to Charge, but at least your unit is still getting to fight something. 

6. The Fight Phase



The Fight Phase saw a complete overhaul, affecting who can fight, where they fight, and when they fight. As we just mentioned, Engagement Range is 2” now, so all that chicanery about deploying "on or off the wall" in 10th is largely gone. There’s also no more Supporting Attacks, where models in base contact with friendly models in base contact with enemy models got to fight; just a flat 2” Fight range, which definitely streamlines things.

First, all units that can Pile-in - that is, anyone engaged in combat or who made a Charge Move that turn - Pile-in first, it’s not like other editions where you do that when a unit is selected to Fight. Players still alternate Fighting with their units, but now the Active Player gets to select who fights first. So let’s say the Defending Player has a unit with Fights First. Well, all Charging units also gain Fights First for the turn, so the Active Player still gets to pick one of their Charging units before the Defender can activate their own Fights First unit. That means it still pays to have a bunch of units Charging at once, but also means you won’t end up in situations where you straight up don’t want to Charge a Fights First unit because your opponent will get to fight before you regardless, which was always a real feelsbad moment. 

After Fights First units go, units without Fights First get to attack. The alternating order continues, so if the Active Player has two units that Charge, they both get to swing, then the Defending player gets to pick a unit, then the Active Player, back and forth until everybody’s fought. That can lead to some edge cases where maybe you don’t want to Charge with any units at all because you want to pick an already engaged unit to Fight in an Ongoing Combat before your opponent can attack.

There’s also a new kind of Fighting called an Overrun Fight. This lets a unit Pile-in again and Fight if they’re no longer in Engagement Range with an enemy unit. That could be something like a unit causing Mortal Wounds on the Charge, killing its target with those, then Overrun Fighting into something else, or if two units Charge something, one Fights and kills the target, that second unit can Overrun into something else, though that does open you up to getting hit back by whatever you Overrun into.

At the end of the Fight Phase, all units that could have fought can Consolidate 3”, and there are a few explicit requirements there. If they’re already engaged, they have to Consolidate into the unit they’re engaged with, as well as every enemy unit they can touch. If they’re not engaged, they have to Consolidate into an enemy unit. If there are no enemy units but there is an Objective Marker, they have to Consolidate onto it. That’s more or less how it worked before, it’s just ordered more logically in the rules now, and like with Pile-ins, it happens all at once.

7. Hidden

Credit: Games Workshop

There’s a new rule this edition called Hidden. Infantry, beast, and swarm units that didn’t shoot this turn or last turn and are in a piece of terrain with at least one Dense terrain feature - like, say, a ruin - are Hidden, meaning they’re not visible to enemy units. So long as they’re not in an enemy unit’s Detection Range - usually 15” - they’ll stay Hidden. This seems like a way of letting your cheap objective holding units stay alive in your backfield, and mitigating any sort of first turn alpha strike your opponent might try - though not being visible doesn’t prevent them from getting shot by Indirect fire weapons, inaccurate as those are now. There are some matchups where it might not matter at all - World Eaters certainly won’t care most of the time - and it’ll definitely be less impactful as armies close in together and start blasting, but there could also be some late game scenarios where the last desperate models in a unit are able to hunker down and hide from the big scary shooting units across the table. It also opens up new design space for units that are harder to detect or able to detect units from further away, which we’ve already seen with some of the T’au rules previews. All units can also reduce their Detection Range with the Gone to Ground rule - if you are eligible for Hidden, and are only partially visible thanks to a Solid terrain feature like a ruin, then your Detection Range is reduced by 3”. This means that positioning models behind bits of ruins can give you that extra bit of protection from distant foes.

8. Wound Allocation

Black Templars Primaris Crusaders. Credit: SRM

While it didn’t come up very often, there were times in 10th edition where you could get clever with wound allocation, maybe having a sergeant with a better armor save or multiple wounds tanking damage for their squishier comrades. Now, when a bunch of melee or shooting attacks are allocated to a unit, the target breaks their unit up into groups based on their wound and save characteristics, and then characters. So if someone is shooting my unit of Black Templar Crusaders with an attached Captain and Lieutenant, I’d have the Initiates with their 3+ saves, the Neophytes with their 4+ saves, the Lieutenant, who is a character with a 3+ save, then the Captain who is another character with a 3+ save and a 4+ Invulnerable save, making for a total of four groups. Characters always take damage last, so I’d get to pick if my Initiates or Neophytes took the incoming damage and had to make saves first. Those saves are then applied in lowest to highest order, so if I had four saves and rolled a 1, 2, 3, and 4, I’d then apply those in that order, starting with the lowest number. This means you might not want to pick those Initiates with the better saves to take that damage first, since they’ll be eating 1s and 2s just as hard as the Neophytes will. That might not sound that different, but it will lead to some faster play, where a player won’t generally be rolling one save at a time on their sergeant with an invulnerable save before getting on to the squad or whatever. Mortal Wounds also get doled out at the end of each batch instead of all happening at the end of the entire unit’s attack sequence, so they don’t all just get piled on to your attached character at the end.

9. Overwatch

Rainbow Warriors Land Raider Redeemer by Craig "MasterSlowPoke" Sniffen

While we normally wouldn’t focus too much on a single stratagem, this is one that already had a huge impact on the game. As before, the defending player gets to pick a non-Titanic unit to shoot in their opponent’s movement phase, and it can shoot an enemy unit that’s visible and within 24”, only hitting on 6+. That hasn’t really changed. What’s different is that it now only happens at the end of the movement phase, not in the Charge phase, and not right after a unit moves or gets set up, and it doesn’t even have to target a unit that moved. Overwatch also doesn’t get re-rolls to hit anymore, making it a bit less reliable while making Torrent weapons an even better pick for it. This is going to speed up the movement phase, as the Active Player won’t be looking over their shoulder the whole time waiting for that Overwatch to happen, or specifically trying to bait out that Land Raider Redeemer’s flamers. This should lead to fewer gotcha moments, since the Overwatching player will have a clear picture of where everything is on the board before triggering it. It also provides a bit more counterplay - if your opponent has pushed something with a bunch of flamers right in front of your precious unit, you can run away back behind a wall rather than being frozen in place.

10. Flying Units



Flying has always been a little janky, either not quite working with terrain or giving units a functional noclip mode to just phase through everything. 11th reaches a new middleground. You can still jump over units and terrain and the like, but to do so you Take to the Skies. That’s just a flat 2” penalty to their maximum move distance, and they can move over anything and everything. It always felt really dumb to measure up and down a given wall or try and figure out how to measure vertical distance on something that wasn’t all flat 90 degree angles. You still can’t land on top of a piece of terrain with a flying vehicle though, so unless you want to get whimsical with house rules, we’re not gonna see Riptides sniping dudes from on top of a building. 

11. Rules Organization



Last but certainly not least is something that I think should be uncontroversially seen as a good thing, and that’s a clearer rules layout. 10th edition was great at cleaning up the language of the game, but 11th lays out nearly every rule in an orderly sequential chart, which makes figuring out what rules work in what order much easier. It’s also all organized in a numerical system like Age of Sigmar 4th edition, which makes referencing rules way easier. For instance, a more complex rule like Charging will first say what kind of unit is eligible to do it - so, a unit that declared a charge, then move on to what the effect is - moving, as described in section 3, what you do before moving - which is picking targets, what to do while moving - dictating who can go where, and what happens after moving, which in this case, is where your models need to go, and what benefits they get. In this case, that’s Fights First, which is in section 24.13. That might sound exhaustive at first, but having that sort of flow chart effect for basically every rule is going to cut down on ambiguity and confusion, making the game more understandable whether you’re duking it out at the top table of a tournament, or three beers in at your garagehammer slamathon. 

Honorable Mentions

There were a few things we had to drop from the video (the listicle format is a harsh mistress) but are including here.

11.1. Battle-shock

One thing that Warhammer games of all flavors have always struggled with is morale mechanics, and that extended to Battle-shock in 10th edition. While it could shut down objective control and turn off Stratagems, it didn’t come up entirely too often, was swingy, and was easily negated. You’d have to test if a unit was below half strength, attempting to rally again in your Command phase, and if something caused a test on a unit above half strength - like the Tyranids’ Shadow in the Warp or much of the Chaos Knights’ arsenal, they’d automatically rally in their own Command phase. In 11th edition, Battle-shock rolls trigger when a unit is at half strength, meaning those two-model strong units of Spawn are finally going to be taking some tests. Additionally, and more universally important, once a unit is Battle-shocked, they need to test again in every one of their own Command Phases, and don’t automatically come out of it. You still can’t use the Insane Bravery Stratagem on a Battle-shocked unit, so units like Chaplains who can automatically remove Battle-shock from a unit are more important than ever. 

11.2. Indirect Fire

Death Korps of Krieg Artillery Team. Credit: SRM

Indirect fire also saw an overhaul. It got loads of errata in early 10th edition to keep it from being oppressive, and that still very much seems to be the case here. The target gets the benefit of cover - meaning you’ve got -1 to hit - and the best you can ever hit on is a 4+. That only applies if you have a friendly unit who can see the target and your indirect shooting unit remained stationary, and on top of that, you can’t get re-rolls to hit while shooting indirect. In a moment of unexpected verisimilitude, that makes a case for artillery spotters, as the best you’re hitting otherwise is on a 6+. 

11.3. Hazardous

Aside from just being a word I struggle to say when I’m not consciously enunciating it, Hazardous got both more and less punishing, as well as applying in more situations. It used to just happen to weapons with the Hazardous keyword, generally causing a model to roll a Hazardous test, and on a 1, kill a model, or do 3 Mortal Wounds if it was a vehicle or character. Now, it just causes a single Mortal Wound on a roll of 1 or 2, meaning it’s going to happen twice as often, but not usually with as harsh consequences. It now also applies when disembarking from a destroyed transport or when making a Desperate Escape test. It basically standardizes all those rules for something bad happening to your dudes. 

Conclusion

When I first started seeing the drip feed of rules changes, I really got the notion that this was going to be 10.5 - some things cleared up, some new missions, and that was kind of it. Now I’m not so sure; the sweeping changes to fundamentals of the game like Detachments and the Fight Phase make this seem like it’s going to be a fairly different edition, even if it’s not as comprehensive as a 7th to 8th edition-style reboot. Everyone on the team is pretty excited about what’s coming next, even if the next few months are probably gonna be the wild west while people get to grips with the new edition.
