BattleTechnical: When to Use Vehicles

August 7th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Vehicles are often a fun addition to your forces – having these shorter vehicles running around really emphasizes the scale of mechs. While you can just toss some main battle tanks in to replace mechs, that’s not always going to lead to a particularly effective force. In this article I’m going to look at some of the key differences between mechs and vehicles, and what situations reward vehicle use, or when you should just stick with using a mech.

Manticore. Credit: Rockfish

Key Differences

On the table there are a couple key differences between vehicles and mechs: their ability to navigate terrain, and how they take damage. In general, vehicles (or at least ones on the ground) are less maneuverable than mechs: depending on their motive type they’re limited on what types of terrain they can enter (only tracked vehicles can enter light woods, nothing can enter heavy woods, wheeled can’t enter rough or rubble, and only hovers can cross water), they can only change elevation by one level per hex (at 2 MP per level), and being only one level tall means its often easier to block line of sight. Vehicles also have mobility degraded by damage far far more easily than a mech – about 15% of hits from the front will at minimum reduce MP by 1, about 21% of hits from the rear will, and 26% of hits from the side will. That’s on tracked vehicles – wheeled and hovercraft vehicles take motive damage at even higher rates. Essentially, this means that if a vehicle starts getting hit by cluster weapons it’s going to stop moving very quickly. Combine that with the limited visibility caused by being only one level tall and there’s a real solid risk that a vehicle can get reduced to 0 MP (or immobilized) in only one or two turns despite still having armor and weapons; every time you move a vehicle you need to be thinking about whether that location is going to keep the vehicle relevant for the rest of the game.

The other significant difference to a mech is how a vehicle takes damage. At a glance they can have a lot of armor – a 1,149 BV Manticore Heavy Tank (hppc) has 43 armor on the front, 34 on each side, and 42 on the turret. That’s comparable to an atlas on any individual location. The raw pips of armor don’t tell the full story – different hit location tables and attack directions mitigate the extra armor significantly. From any given direction, 61% of attacks will hit the same side (on vehicles with turrets, without a turret that increases to 77%). Compare that to a mech, where you’re looking at 20% to hit the center torso from the front (assuming no floating crits, a little less with them). Tanks just don’t have three times as much armor in any location compared to mechs, even at similar BV, so they’re going to lose locations much more quickly – and losing a single location destroys the entire vehicle. The wider side arcs (incoming attacks through either side hex hit the side table, rather than just the rear side hex like a mech) double down on this issue – it’s really not hard to get a couple attackers into the side of a tank, at which point you’re concentrating damage extremely effectively and also probably immobilizing it.

When taking damage another significant difference compared to mechs is that they take through-armor crits on 2/12 on the front and rear, and additionally on 8s on the side – so getting hit on the side is a ~20% chance to roll on the crit table (at which point you just need a 6+). Crits also apply differently, as you’ll keep moving down the list of possible effects until getting one that can apply. For a tank that doesn’t have weapons in the side, rolling a 6-8 results in a vehicle that can’t shoot the next turn, and a 9+ immobilizes it and disables all energy weapons from taking an engine hit. From the rear without weapons it takes that engine hit on a 6+ (though the chance of actually getting crit is much lower).

Lyran Commonwealth Vedette. Credit: SRM

What to Avoid

If vehicles are less mobile than mechs, and die from damage more quickly than mechs, then we don’t want to replace a mech with a vehicle. If you can do the same thing with a mech, do it with the mech. Typically this hits main battle tank type units – your manticore, patton, drillson, condor, or similar. The hovers do get more mobility, which helps counteract the damage concentration because they just won’t get hit as frequently, but it’s not enough to counteract how easily they get immobilized.

All of these are units that do a bit of everything – just like a mech. There’s always going to be a mech option that does about the same thing at a similar cost, but the mech is a lot more mobile and more survivable. If you do for whatever reason need a MBT-like vehicle, make sure to look for one that has a turret and has at least a small weapon in every location, which helps spread damage out and minimizes how quickly you get destroyed by crits.

SRM Carrier. Credit: Rockfish

What to Use

The most effective vehicles are those that do things that mechs struggle with. A highlight is an LRM or SRM carrier – these are units that aren’t designed to get shot at, and care less about mobility (at least if the SRM carrier is able to deploy as a hidden unit, if it’s driving in from your board edge it’s unlikely to accomplish anything). They shove a ton of weapon on a chassis, and either expect to die immediately (SRM carrier) or never get shot because they’re sitting behind a hill (LRM carrier). Mechs pay for too much armor, structure, and mobility to generally be able to do this effectively. An LRM carrier costs 833 BV for 60 LRM tubes, you’re paying twice as much to get those same tubes out of an Archer 5CS (admittedly, the archer does other things, but that’s exactly my point).

Similar to the LRM/SRM carrier is a saladin or SM1/SM5 – these are extremely fast hovertanks that exist to transport an AC/20 or ultra AC/20 into your rear. Their armor is described more as hopes and dreams than anything that’ll stop incoming damage, but there’s no other way to get those guns with that speed – the fastest mech with an ultra ac/20 is the blitzkrieg, running 7/11 at 1,273 BV. An 8/12 saladin is 806 BV. This is where the vehicle shines – it brings a capability to your force that can’t be otherwise replicated. The saladin in particular is effective, because it leans into the issues with getting hit by being cheap and fast. If getting hit kills you, don’t get hit, or at least be cheap enough that you’ve absorbed more fire than you’re worth.

Wolf's Dragoons Hawkmoth VTOL. Credit: Jack Hunter

But What About VTOLs?

I’m mostly talking about ground vehicles above, because VTOLs are about as different from ground vehicles as ground vehicles are from mechs. They almost never have meaningful armor, and like hovercraft rely on movement speed to avoid damage, but are harder to hide because they have to remain in the air – flying at level 1 gives them similar visibility to mechs, but because it’s possible for them to sideslip when turning at speed being that low runs the risk of slamming into terrain, which never ends well. In general this means that VTOLs are generally best served by being extremely cheap and used for spotting, where you don’t really care if your 300 BV helicopter got blown up. There are a couple of effective combat VTOLs, which follow the same rules as hovers – make use of their high speed to bring short range burst damage, and don’t try for long engagements where attackers can concentrate damage.
