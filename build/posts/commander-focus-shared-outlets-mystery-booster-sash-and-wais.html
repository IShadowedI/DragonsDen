Commander Focus: Shared Outlets, Mystery Booster, & Sash and Waistcoat, Unmen

September 10th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Prior to the debut of Mystery Booster: Commander Edition, I like so many considered myself reasonably well-versed in Magic’s lore. I began my journey with the game back in Aether Revolt, during the rising action of the Bolas Saga, watched videos on older stories in the setting, even played a ripped copy of Shandalar—none of this prepared me for my favorite Commander of the set to be two obscure henchmen from the Onslaught block novels. Sash and Waistcoat, Unmen inspired the same reaction in me as was so eloquently described by one Brian David Gilbert in his treatise on E3 presentations: “What? Hell yeah! What?”. I’m far from alone in saying so, but seeing this set spoiled image by horribly pixelated image was some of the most fun I’ve had when it comes to immediate card reactions. Once the dust settled though, there was one Commander standing above it all, and indeed that was Sash and Waistcoat. Between a modest cost, interesting ability for the table to share, and a shockingly available combo, there’s power hidden in these roaches, waiting to be turned face-up! So much so that we’re actually planning for Bracket 4, something I don’t do lightly here on Commander Focus. What about these Unmen makes me unafraid to start them at high power? Let’s try to understand, together.

"Manifest the top card of your library. Any player may activate this ability."

The first card everyone thinks about when it comes to this pair is Training Grounds, and rightly so—reducing the cost to Manifest all the way down to 1 isn’t just good for you, but makes their first ability all the more appealing for your opponents. That’s not exactly a Bracket 4 mindset, and naturally we have more sinister purposes for a reduced cost ability, but at all levels of power it should be obvious that Training Grounds is the first card to build around for Sash and Waistcoat. The second card, however, is where I differ from most, because there’s but one single other card which reduces an activated ability’s cost by {2}: Power Artifact. S&W isn’t inherently an Artifact, this is true, but there are more than a few ways to get them there, if we even need to. This brings us to the true reason S&W are exciting: If you squint, they’re probably the best mana outlet for Monoblue since Urza, Lord High Artificer. If you make infinite mana, it doesn’t matter what the cost of your Commander’s ability is, and Power Artifact readily combines with Basalt Monolith & Grim Monolith to do just that. On that line of thinking, Rings of Brighthearth doesn’t just give us the ability to basically pay {5} for 2 Manifests by way of S&W, but also make infinite mana with Basalt. Its worst-case scenario is ultimately saving one mana per two activations, which is fine enough. Where things get a bit funny is crossroads at which Sash and Waistcoat's hunt for {2} mana reductions meet the same pioneering spirit on Grim & Basalt. In much the same vein as us turning S&W into an Artifact for Power Artifact, animating a Basalt with Training Grounds around—get this—also makes infinite mana, with it being a Creature and thereby having a reduced cost to untap. All our core combo pieces want the same thing, they just need to swap types around a bit to get there. For Sash and Waistcoat, you're intending to couple that with Ashnod’s Altar to cycle through Manifests (and our entire Library) with a S&W ability that costs {2} or less, meaning that the scariest Artifact to ever hit the board propels another combo machine with a fairly innocuous, cheap outlet in the CZ. You’ve basically got a cheaper, weirder, less stax-reliant build of cEDH’s Power Urza that can go wide in a simplified gamestate. I don’t think S&W are necessarily cEDH material themselves, but borrow those play patterns to do something way, way cooler.

This bridges on the incomprehensible, but trust me this is basically our gameplan.

There’s another wrinkle to this Commander, and that’s in terms psychological. No, I’m not referring to the Kafkaesque art and the effect it may have on those squeamish about pests, but rather the fact that everyone shares in the spoils of your ability’s reduced cost…and position as a mana outlet. S&W get very interesting in B4 because the fact that you can be an opponent’s combo piece means games won’t just end faster, and more explosively, but also involve you far more often as a player. The higher in power level you creep, the more dangerous it is to run S&W out when an opponent could combo off, because as it turns out letting someone Manifest their entire Library is perhaps a bad idea. Moreover, it gives players something to do if stalled, reducing hopelessness and funneling mana into beating down the biggest bad—not you, of course—with unblockable 2/2s. There’s really nothing Faustian about activating S&W as an opponent, especially if you have the mana for it, and you’re far more likely to let things like a Training Grounds live if it appears they could benefit. They shouldn't, of course, but The Selfish Gene is alive and well at the pods in which this Commander sees play. There's an added element of 'Vulture Theory' as well, where in Sash and Waistcoat allowing an opponent to pop off early, and ideally get stuffed, we can try to be the second player to pop off once everyone's burned their interaction. S&W is one of those rare Commanders that gets a ton more interesting at higher Brackets, even if it’s still cool to do things like Manifest Eldrazi and blink them at Bracket ~2.

Statler and Waldorf, Unmuppets

At a baseline, Sash and Waistcoat are indeed just a worse Urza of greater nuance, but the devil’s in the details. I would contend that, in comparing their abilities which serve as mana outlets, their option is far more impactful in the midgame than Urza’s—which often sees use only in the endgame—and that it pulls upon some powerful synergies that have lacked a strong enough home. Foremost among them is a pair of Duskmourn Commander cards, Glitch Interpreter & They Came From the Pipes. Glitch Interpreter was tailor-made for S&W, or perhaps the other way around, turning your unblockable Manifests into a potential 3 cards each turn, on top of having some Acerak-esque combo potential with Ashnod’s Altar. Pipes, meanwhile, just tacks on a draw to each S&W activation, on top of being a phenomenal option to blink, netting two bodies, and therefore two draws, each time it enters. There are other worthy face-down synergy pieces to consider, even at Bracket 4, but the biggest issue is grappling with how bad they are to have in hand, versus hit off of S&W’s ability. Fathom Seer, Kadena’s Silencer, Willbender, Mischevious Quanar, etc are all fantastic to flip at the right opportunity, especially during your combo turn, but they lose a ton of luster if you ever need to pay {3} to play them face down. The agony here is that our buckets of ability cost reduction don't help, as flipping up face-down permanents is a "Special Action", and trades the need of using the stack for not being activated abilities. The issue with Morphs has always laid with that upfront price of {3} for a 2/2, something you’re far happier to pay via Sash and Waistcoat’s ability, rather than a card in hand.

The bug eyes are a design choice.

For that reason, this is far more a deck that cares about putting together its combo than it is a morph/face downs-matter list. We’re playing the best ones, but far more important is flexing S&W’s ability to rip through our Library and blink the best pieces, turning mysterious 2/2s into powerful value engines or pieces of our endgame. We can flex cards like Willbender in much the same way as a deck which draws its Library does, with included free countermagic. It's insulation when you're popping off, with the occasional happy accident of having hidden tricks onboard in the midgame. These also help solve our problem of being a meek little combo deck, with blockers for threats like Tymna and big beaters, encouraging opponents to fight amongst themselves. If we leaned too heavily into incentivizing foes to activate our Commander, such as by playing mass control changing effects or boardwipe buffs, paradoxically it would likely make foes suspicious and less likely to do so. The furthest I’ve gone is in considering Ward of Bones, to lock portions of the game away like playing Creatures & Lands, making S&W’s ability the only way to develop their board, yet unable to attack us. In practice, this led to opponents agreeing not to attack, until we were on the table as a target.

Rosencrantz and Guildenstern, Unalive

There’s a host of ways to make infinite mana in this list, between a Sash and Waistcoat whose ability costs {1} or less and Ashnod’s Altar, Rings+Basalt, or Power Artifact on either Monolith. Navigating those lines isn’t especially difficult, and we can even use a Land released in Murders at Karlov Manner to filter into colored mana throughout the combo, flipping a Branch of Vitu-Ghazi to add {U}{U}, which then allows us to turn a Laboratory Maniac effect face-up and follow that with all manner of draw effects. The slow way is to flex a card like a blinked/bounced and recast Primordial Mists & Nexus of Fate, and kill with unblockable damage (a far sicker path to victory, which requires you play a thematic-but-expensive Enchantment). Absent these paths of play, however, there lies the murky inbetween of Ashnod’s Altar and a S&W ability which costs {2}, discounted by something like Heartstone or Forensic Gadgeteer. Oftentimes we will look to clone these, to acquire our preferred discount of {2}, but you can still steer through the combo with Ashnod's and a 2-cost S&W. This does cycle through your entire Library, sure, but to what end? You can only flip Creatures, inherently, so how are you accessing some other path to victory?

See also: Arthur clenching his fist.

So long as S&W is an Artifact, either works. Let's assume a starting board of S&W, Heartstone (for ease of explanation), Ashnod’s, and three bodies. Keep sacrificing one body to manifest, until you hit Branch of Vitu Ghazi, which we'll flip up by sacrificing another body, floating 1 Colorless mana and turning {3} into {U}{U}. Sac the third body to Ashnods (and recall Branch is now a face-up Land that taps for {C}), retaining the {U}{U}, until you hit a face down Gerrard’s Hourglass Pendant, then spend your Branch's tap and the floating {C} to continue cycling towards a face down Exit Specialist. Spend {1}{U} to flip the Specialist, bouncing the face-down Pendant, then sac your board, cast the Pendant, and bring everything sacrificed previously that turn back to the board tapped. That should provide enough fodder to dig until you hit whatever else you need, so long as you're deep enough; this combo is dependent on the order of your Library, yes, but by virtue of (not kidding) Jack O’Lantern we have an alternate means of finding the {U} necessary for Specialist, even if we’ve played Branch earlier. We need the extra Ashnod fuel to pay for the {1}{U} cost on Specialist’s Disguise. Lines like these are as barebones as possible, as an illustration of your worst-case-scenario of a minimal discount on the Commander. Given any other synergy piece sees “Manifest most of your Library, then bring it back tapped” as a patently absurd path of play alongside any trigger which provides value, it still gets you there. As mentioned though, it is far easier to just go infinite, by way of a {1}-cost S&W ability and Ashnods and/or a Monolith that can untap for {2} or less, and win via Nexus—I’m just showing how savvy pilots can steer the bugs to victory under rationed mana.

Example Decklist: Sash and Waistcoat with Garterbelt

There’s a lot of very cool things going on here, but let’s start by mentioning Relic’s Roar & Behind the Mask. Each of these takes one of the two disparate types for our discounts, being Creatures for Training Grounds/Heartstone, and Artifacts for Power Artifact/Forensic Gadgeteer, and makes the relevant permanent both for the turn. While this can make a card like Power Artifact potentially fall off after that turn, to some extent this works to our benefit, meaning our opponents won’t have the capacity to use a cheap-to-activate S&W on their turns. These are also 1 mana apiece, which is a cost we’re very happy to search, with Dizzy Spell being one of the best means of finding Training Grounds in Monoblue. The very same Micromancer or Spellseeker which finds that Transmute card also has the capacity to grab our ‘wild cards’, and we’re very happy to abuse that. Along the same lines as our Hourglass Pendant, discussed in the above section, is Reenact the Crime; as we cycle through Manifests, this can allow us the opportunity to cast cards Blue normally cannot access from the yard, by virtue of them being Manifested, sacrificed, and then copied.

Decklists are kept updated, and may change with set releases.

This is built as a Bracket 4 list, complete with as many 2-card combos as our funky Commander can muster. To continue that thought, I’ll affirm what I said earlier on in this article: Sash and Waistcoat get more interesting, as relative power level climbs, because forcing the table to play around everyone having a mana outlet is a wildly cool situation to present. S&W are like a Blue take on Heartwood Storyteller, a bane of modern cEDH that's led to ample discussion on the stack as players bicker over a forgotten group hug card, now back with a vengeance. You’re putting a gun on the table, on the bet that you’ll be able to pick it up the quickest, yet it’s arguably cooler to see an opponent try their luck and get stuffed when they attempt to combo off early. As mentioned, ‘Vulture theory’ is in full effect here—it can be to your benefit to be the second person to attempt to combo off, and S&W gives foolhardy foes the chance to be first in line to be interacted-with. If you do drop the Bracket down, suddenly the gun you’re placing on the table isn’t a mana outlet, but instead the ability to go wide with unblockable 2/2s, and that damage can likewise stack up; play cards like Curse of Verbosity, give opponents mana with Victory Chimes, and root for some fun face down gameplay. Heck, you can even try to make it a Timmy list, and flicker some Eldrazi you Manifested.

Un-Axe-cessible

Doug Shuler's original art for Power Artifact is iconic, which is good, given it's stuck on the Reserved List.

More than just being a good Commander, Sash and Waistcoat, Unmen is a fun Commander. There’s elements of some of the best cEDH options borrowed in its play patterns, between Urza, Kinnan, & Thrasios, but I would contend that S&W’s endgames are more interesting problems to solve, and their midgame keeps players engaged who may have otherwise stalled out. While it's not a 1:1 comparison, the feeling of Heartwood Storyteller is present, but instead of being a communal value engine for the table, it's an outlet for decks that might be a card down while assembling an infinite mana combo. What’s crazy about Mystery Booster: Commander is that S&W aren’t even the only 2-mana option with the description of 'communal engine that gets better at high power', as Yume, Chronicler of Valor is also suspect for playability in the 99 of cEDH lists (collusion notwithstanding). On the topic of the set from which Sash and Waistcoat originate, we do need to address the elephant in the room: The way these are being distributed, at time of writing, is disappointing. I don’t think S&W will be one of the truly sought-after Commanders from the set (unless this article blows up, I suppose), but for smash hits like Whtz, the Bibliophile or Homer, the Hermit players who don’t proxy are looking at three-figure price tags for mechanically-unique cardboard. If you’ve gotten this far, you likely do proxy, with Power Artifact being a key card in this list sitting at around ~$120 for a heavily-played copy, and I would contend no set to date makes a better case for getting acquainted with proxies than MB:C. Real-world quibbles aside, I do genuinely urge you to give S&W a try, especially if you enjoy Blue as a color, and high-power Magic as a social experience. You won’t be sandbagging or throwing by playing this list, or this Commander, but that yearning for everyone to ‘do the thing’ is met by providing this communal mana outlet, something no other Commander can do while still being a reasonable pick for true Bracket 4. A measure of altruism can indeed be adaptive, even in the harshest environments.

Until next time, you've activated my trap card, Urza-boy.
