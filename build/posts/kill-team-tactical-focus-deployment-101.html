[Kill Team] Tactical Focus: Deployment 101

November 12th, 2026 via Goonhammer

It’s easy to overlook deployment in almost any tabletop game. I doubt that anyone is eagerly bounding over to a table because they simply cannot wait to take their dudes out of a case and place them on a board in the just right formation. Folks play tabletop wargames for the battles, the pretend war, the tiny plastic glory to be earned by rolling dice and pushing models around a board. For many players, especially those just starting out, deployment is just the act of setting up for the good stuff to follow.

Deployment isn’t the sexiest part of a game in any system, but an early lesson that most people learn early on is that no matter the system, deployment is one of the most important parts of any game. While good deployment rarely can win you a game all on its own, bad deployment can absolutely lose you a game before the very first turn. The key to winning any game is making sure your models are where they need to be on the board, when they need to be there, and poor deployment makes it all that much harder for you to do that. Poor deployment means you’ll waste time getting your forces to where they actually need to be on the board, all while key moments that you could’ve taken advantage of slip through your fingers.

Credit: Games Workshop

Kill Team is no different then any of these systems, and in some cases good deployment versus bad deployment can be even more pivotal than in many larger games since you have a much smaller number of models on the board to cover space. The last thing you want to realize in the middle of a match is that your operative with the nasty gun ended up somewhere on the board where they’ll never get the chance to shoot at a good target because they were deployed badly, and you didn’t figure it out until it’s too late.

So what are the best practices for good deployment in Kill Team? In this article, we’ll take a dive into a four step thought process behind building a strong and consistent plan for deploying your team on the table to help you better understand and improve your gameplan, and set yourself up to win in the Killzone before the first round even starts.

Necron Canoptek Circle Kill Team. Credit: Magos Sockbert

Step One: Know Your Team

In theory, Kill Team is a game with a limited number of variables for building a deployment plan. Especially if using the Approved Ops ruleset, there’s only so many maps to play on, and only so many teams you could expect to play against. You could, given enough time, patience, and practice, learn each and every matchup and map and build a tailor made plan for each combination. In practice, that’s a huge number of potential matchups, which doubles in size when you realize you factor in that your plan might change depending on which side of the killzone you’re deploying on, and then multiply that further when you take into consideration the different crit ops and tac ops. While some players can plan for each and every possibility, for most it’s impractical to plan for each individually. While there are a few important things to think about for the opponents team, such as considering their most dangerous operatives and their team’s threat ranges, the most important part of deployment is understanding your own team's rules, and having a general game plan before deployment even starts.

Knowing your own rules is important to winning sounds like I’m stating the obvious, but just because it’s low hanging fruit doesn’t make it any less true. There’s a pretty substantial number of teams in the game, even with declassified rotating out older teams from competitive play, and it’s almost a guarantee that most players won’t remember every single rule for teams they don’t regularly play as or against. You, on the other hand, are a fixed variable: you know exactly what team you’re going to be playing, you control that. So, learn your team’s rules, look it over and learn what the team is good at and bad at. If you know your teams rules like the back of your hand, then thats quite literally half the battle of knowing what the teams on the killzone do.

Plus, once you know your rules, you can understand what your team is good at, and from there you can start to build a plan on how to win. Is your team tough and good at bullying enemy operatives off objectives? Do you play your Tac Ops extremely well? Are you an all gas no brakes Kill Op team? How's your mobility? What offensive tricks do you have? Can you swarm your opponent with bodies, or are you an elite team who needs to be more careful with each operative? While it is true that no plan survives first contact fully intact, it’s important to have a general idea of what your path to victory in a game will look like, as well as maybe a handful of alternate plans in case you end up with a matchup that makes plan A difficult to do.

If you feel comfortable and confident in your own knowledge, and have a solid basic gameplan, it hugely reduces the mental load during both deployment and the game in general. You can worry more about how your plan is changing as the game goes on, rather than trying to decide what the plan is on the fly, which is a huge relief from the mental load during a game. Plus, generally these kinds of plans that are prepared for pre-game are much more difficult for an opponent to stop than something improvised haphazardly, even if that pregame plan is fairly loose.

Credit: Dan "Swiftblade" Richardson

Let’s take Murderwing, a team I’ve been playing extensively recently, as an example. I know that while this team has operatives that hit like a runaway freight train, it also has very limited defensive options and can lose operatives quickly if they’re hung out to dry, so generally I want to avoid just full sending the team unless I’m certain I can just overwhelm my opponent in a wall of Chaos Space Marine muscle. Because Murderwing is so mobile though, and I have great access to stuns and modifying APL, Murderwing can play the Crit Op and Tac Op fairly well, and thanks to Boost the team is super mobile and I can afford to hang back from objectives until I can zoom up and contest it safely. With this in mind, my general gameplan is to focus on Tac Op as my primary with Retrieval from Approved Ops 2025, and then focus on two objectives that I can stage safely far away from until I can fly up and take them near the end of a turning point. As a kicker, I can even send one operative on a long bomb run to the opponent home objective if I can get the right angle, which makes leaning on Retrieval even better. If I don’t think retrieval is going to work, I can pivot to Flank and use boost to reach safe spots in my opponents territory while picking off anyone sent my way, or Dominate if I can just get away with raking up kills in the back half of a game.

So without even seeing what map I’m playing on, or what team I’m up against, I’ve got the foundations of a gameplan and a path to how I will win. I’ll need to adjust for the unexpected of course, and there will always be the unexpected, but now when it's time to deploy I have a much better idea of where I need to place operatives so that they can be where they need to to execute the plan.

Step Two: Understand the Killzone

The next step in leveling up your deployment game is by understanding the various killzone maps in Kill Team, particularly in the Approved Ops mission pack.

While every Killzone will present a different set of problems and opportunities for your team, such as whether it’s open or close quarters and what sort of terrain and cover is nearby your drop zone, the main thing we’re going to focus on is what makes them all the same, and that’s how each Killzone can functionally be divided into three lanes based on where the objectives are placed on the map. Generally, if you take a look at any given Killzone and then split it into thirds based on where the objectives are placed, extending from drop zone to drop zone, these lanes become pretty easy to parse visually, even if they aren’t officially marked off from one another in any way.

While moving in between these three lanes can be very fluid in most games of Kill Team, during deployment it's important to decide how much you want to or need to control each of these lanes and deploy accordingly. Deciding to focus your efforts on controlling certain lanes tends to be a much more effective strategy than trying to spread your team out to be everywhere on the board at once, as it prevents you from being spread too thin and feeds back into your general game plan that you make prior to the start of the game.

As an example, if you know that you’re playing a team that wants to push aggressively into enemy territory, such as Vespid, it might be best to focus your deployment on the lane containing your opponents objective, where you’re likely to deliver the most pressure, while a smaller contingent of your forces hold the center and home objective lanes. For a team that wants to lie in wait, like Nemesis Claw, it may be best to ignore your opponent’s objective lane altogether and split your team between your home and center. While how your allocate your forces between each map may change based on the layout, understanding how to best split your team during deployment based on these three lanes will almost always be better than haphazardly trying to cover the whole board.

Step Three: Deploy Effective Equipment

Kill Team Volkus Terrain painted by Crab-stuffed Mushrooms

One of the most unique aspects of Kill Team is equipment selection and how it can shape your team by providing a wide toolbox of options for every team to draw upon that can change from game to game. While there's a good bit of equipment that doesn’t affect deployment choices at all, many pieces of equipment, such as barricades, mines, and razorwire, can be deployed on the killzone prior to the start of the game. In many cases, effective deployment of equipment can be just as important as effective deployment of your operatives.

When boiled down to its most basic functions, deployable equipment generally has one of two purposes (though some pieces can be used for either option): The equipment either protects or aids your operatives, or the equipment denies or debuffs your opponent. Based on what you need each equipment piece to do, you’ll need to consider the placement of each piece differently, but either way you’ll once again want to think about killzone lanes.

Buffing or aiding your team can come in several different forms, such as a barricade to protect your models from shooting, a ladder to help get your model traverse vantage quickly, a comms device to boost support abilities, or an ammo cache to buff your shooting reliability. Deciding where these equipment choices need to go is usually pretty easy, as it largely depends on where you want your operatives to be. While that does again seem like a very silly and obvious statement to make, considering which lanes you want your team to focus on controlling during a game and deploying buff equipment in these areas means you’ll make the most out of where you place items like a comms device, or get the most protection out of a barricade. It's also important to think about how you want to stage up the board for later turning points with these equipment options, making it easier to project threat in parts of the killzone you want to control while making it difficult for the opponent to threaten you.

As an example, a barricade placed right on the center objective is strong, especially if it’s out in the open against shooting, but it might be so far forward that making the best use of it puts you in easy threat range of enemy operatives. Placing a barricade further back from the objective while keeping it in your operatives effective threat range, whether it’s shooting or charging, means that if your opponent wants to control the center objective, they have to walk within your threat range, and now you’re the one who can effectively start a trade from a safe staging position.  Consider what lanes and objectives you want to control, and how these choices will help improve your control over that part of the board.

The other function of deployable equipment can be trickier, but just as effective, and that's using deployable terrain as area denial. Deployable equipment used as denial pieces strengthens your control over a given lane by making it more difficult for your opponent to access it freely, slowing them down or corralling them into specific areas of the map. Examples of universal equipment choices that can do this include mines, razorwire, and even barricades placed at choke points that your enemy has to spend movement trying to climb. As far as where this denial equipment is usually most effective, they tend to be most effective in spots that either deny access to your home objective, or at choke points between lanes that make it difficult for your opponent to move freely from one lane to another should they have to redirect their forces. If there’s a part of the map you know you don’t want your opponent to be, and think your opponent will be able to easily get there if left unchecked, consider using deployable equipment options at those spots to slow down or deny your opponent access altogether.

Brood Brothers Kill Team. Credit: John from Can You Roll A Crit?

Step Four: Deploy Effective Operatives

A little over two thousand words later, and we’re finally on how we can best deploy our operatives.

After all of the considerations we’ve made so far, actually getting down to deploying our operatives might just be the easiest part here, since every decision we’ve made so far has set us up for success. We know what our path to winning the game looks like, we know what areas of the killzone we need to control to do it are, and we’ve set up our equipment to either improve our strength in a lane or deny the opponent free access. Every choice here has brought us to a razor sharp point that shows exactly where we need to be, which means when we’re deploying our team, we’re not just improvising or putting down operatives wherever. We can be purposeful in our decision, and maximize the effectiveness of our team.

The most important part of deployment, plan or no plan though, is to place your operatives in safe parts of your drop zone. This takes priority over all else, even if it means splitting your forces up more than you may like. An operative is no good to you if your opponent can grab an easy shot on it. If your safe spots are truly dire, consider using equipment to buy you more safe real estate in your drop zone.

Feral Mechanicum Kill Team; Credit: Colin Ward

Being purposeful is the key aspect here, and even if we’ve set up for success already, we still need to think on a per operative basis on where they will be most effective. For example, if you have an operative with a key support ability that improves your team’s offensive or defensive capabilities, they need to be deployed where you expect the bulk of your team to be, which means that this operative probably isn’t one you want babysitting your home objective. Or if you have a very aggressive operative, they need to be placed somewhere where you plan on the bulk of the engagement to be, as the last thing you want is to waste precious time trying to redeploy them because they were deployed too far away from the action.

Lastly, a good rule of thumb for deployment is think about where you want your models to be at the end of turning point one, and then set your model up in a spot where they can reach that part of the killzone when they activate. In the current edition of Kill team, turning point one doesn’t present any ability for your team to score outside of kill op, which almost always requires you to overextend your model, so that first turning point move is almost like a secondary deployment for the following three turning points. Just like with deployment, rather than just pushing your operatives directly up the board absentmindedly on the first turn, consider where you need to be to score points according to your game plan, and reposition your operatives in a spot to do so, even if it means potentially having a model not leave the drop zone so that it can be more effective later in the game. No matter where the operative needs to go that first turn though, the only way they can reach it is if they’re deployed in the right spot.

Credit: Dan "Swiftblade" Richardson

Final Thoughts

Deployment is the least interactive part of Kill Team, and while your opponents placement may influence your own placement of operatives and equipment, there's no randomness from the dice involved in deployment and no conflict to consider. It is entirely an operation that you, the player, control, and because of that it's one of the most important parts of the game to have a plan for. As stated earlier in the article, while deployment is something that very rarely wins a game all on its own, you can absolutely lose a game by deploying poorly before any dice are ever rolled. Deployment is the foundation for the rest of your game to follow, and with a carefully considered plan to build from and by deciding what lanes of the Killzone you need to focus your efforts on, it's much easier to set your equipment and operatives in positions to execute that strategy effectively and set yourself up to be in the strongest position possible for the rest of the match.
