Hope and Horror: Reviewing Two Essential Tales from Trench Crusade

November 9th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Factory Fortress

If you're a veteran Warhammer author looking to expand the portfolio a bit, stepping into the Trench Crusade property right now must feel a bit like a homecoming. It's not just that the lore's grimdark tone should be reasonably familiar to anyone working in 40K, but also a reflection on the very deep roster of talent it's managed to attract.

Three of the Black Library authors who have contributed to Trench Crusade's expanding body of story are Black Library Readers' Hall of Famers, with two-time winner Gav Thorpe and the other two (Graham McNeill and Josh Reynolds) in the top five. Marc Gascoigne, one of the three people responsible for the creation of the Black Library itself, is the Head of Special Ops in charge of licensing and product development. Black Library novelist Andy Chambers is on the development team, while former White Dwarf editor-in-chief Lyle Lowery is putting those skills to good use helming The Trench Wire, the online digital magazine hosted on the official website.

As the writer for the Beyond the Black Library occasional series here on Tabletop Battles, it's always fun to see what our favorite Black Library authors are creating when they're not hard at work on Warhammer, and seeing them help expand Trench Crusade's body of work has been like chocolate meeting peanut butter.

Today we'll be looking at two of these early tales, Gav Thorpe's The Tale of the Weaver and Blood & Iron by Graham McNeill. While both were distributed as part of the digital rewards for the Trench Crusade Kickstarter campaign, Graham McNeill's Blood & Iron has also been made publicly available for free.

Image credit: Factory Fortress

Tale of the Weaver, by Gav Thorpe

Our first Trench Crusade story comes to use by way of Thorpe, and was included in the Carcass Front release. The Tale of the Weaver introduces us to Sister Letitia, a battle-hardened Spanish nun on a pilgrimage to the Holy Land by way of Rome. This brings her to the Buco di Viso, a tunnel that passes through the highest peak of the Cottian Alps and into Italy. In a bureaucratic touch that will feel right at home to fans of the Adeptus Administratum, the pass is controlled by the Church- and only a certain number of pilgrims are allowed through each day.

After heretical partisans trigger an avalanche to sabotage the crossing, Sister Letitia joins up with Italian Alpini soldiers to hunt down those responsible and prevent another attack. As the patrol searches the catacombs for evidence of the attackers, she makes the acquaintance of a fellow volunteer, an Englishman amidst a pilgrimage of his own.

The man- once a weaver of Church vestments and other sacred cloth- had a divine manifestation appear in his work. Words in ancient Aramaic appeared in the threading of a humeral veil, which his Archbishop translated as meaning go forth and fight for God. Commanded to take the relic to the War Cardinal in Rome, the English weaver set off on his pilgrimage.

Sister Letitia is moved to share her own tale with him, one that involves a sculpted stone head of the Virgin Mary rescued from a fire in Zaragoza. The head has been speaking to her, exhorting her to undertake her pilgrimage to New Antioch. When the head of the Virgin is placed close to veil, it tells Letitia that the words had been mistranslated. When she learns what they actually say, only one of them will make it through the pass alive.

This was a solid story from a veteran, one that works well because it assumes little in the way of reader knowledge of the setting. Not only that, but it also helps establish Trench Crusade's tone for those who might be new to it. Thorpe makes much of ambiguity here, leaving a great deal to assumption and imagination. For instance, Sister Letitia is the only one able to hear the statue speak, and given the events that follow it's only natural to wonder if it was ever speaking at all.

The only thing I got stuck on here was the reference to Sister Letitia's gun as a Browning. Given that in the world of Trench Crusade Europe never colonized the New World, there's no America, no Utah, and, presumably, no John Moses Browning. But quibble aside, this was a great introduction to a character it's hinted we may well be seeing more of.

Image credit: Factory Fortress

Blood & Iron, by Graham McNeill

Imperial Guardsmen holed up in a Shrine to the Emperor are under siege by Death Guard forces. Things look grim until the Inquisition shows up, then.... err, wait. Sorry, wrong game. But McNeill's story is a good look at how closely Trench Crusade and Warhammer 40,000 track, and I imagine this must have been very familiar territory for McNeill to write from.

Blood & Iron puts us alongside an embattled lieutenant of a Varangian Oathbound warband, who, coming under artillery bombardment, are forced to seek shelter in a church. But it's no ordinary bombardment; where the bombs fall, the dead begin to walk.

Now let's be clear, calling Trench Crusade derivative is unfairly reductive, not least given just how much 'inspiration' from other properties has been baked in to Warhammer's firmament. Sure the Lord of Tumours featured in this story shares a fair amount of DNA with a Heretic Astartes of the 14th, but then I'm just as likely when seeing a swarm of flies used as an element of the daemonically profane to think of some of the entities Hellblazer's John Constantine has had to contend with in the pages of the comic book. Humanity has been using flies to depict supernatural rot and decay since at least as long as the ancient Zoroastrians, for whom the daemon Nasu was depicted as a corpse-infecting fly.

Or consider everybody's buddy Beelzebub, depicted here in Collin de Plancy's Dictionnaire Infernal from 1818.

Given the kind of setting Trench Crusade is and the central role religious lore and mythology plays in it, you'd be more inclined to raise an eyebrow if it didn't play in this space.

So while sure, there's a lot here that feels familiar, the strength of the Trench Crusade setting also means that it feels fresh at the same time. With few modifications this could be a perfectly serviceable Black Library story, but it works just as well as a terrific, introductory Trench Crusade one. An upside of the thematic parallels of the two properties, too, is that the 'barrier to entry' for existing Warhammer writers is fairly low. Yes, they'll need to be familiar with the Trench Crusade story and setting, but this isn't something so fundamentally different that their experience writing for the grim, dark future offers them little advantage.

McNeill brings the same gritty tension to the story that made his Black Library Readers' Hall of Fame novel Storm of Iron so unforgettable, both being stories of besiegers and the besieged but this one on a far more intimate scale. The result is a story that feels familiar in all the right ways, but in a refreshingly different setting. More of this, please!

Overall, Factory Fortress has released about enough Trench Crusade fiction to fill an issue or two of Inferno!, that monthly Warhammer short-story magazine that laid the foundation for the Black Library as we know it today. That's a solid start, and the author roster is nothing if not ambitious.

With Carcass Front freshly released and the game's first global campaign, Operation Leviathan, only recently concluded, this is a property that is still in its infancy. I'm very excited to see where it goes from here, and whether eventually we'll see the "Black Library playbook" at work, bringing to life the world of the Faithful and the Fallen in novel and anthology alike.
