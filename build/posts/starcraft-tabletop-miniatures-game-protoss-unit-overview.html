StarCraft Tabletop Miniatures Game: Protoss Unit Overview

November 17th, 2026 via Goonhammer

StarCraft Tabletop Miniatures Game brings the war for the Koprulu sector to your tabletop, letting you build, paint, and play armies of Terran, Zerg, and Protoss units as they fight toward victory. And while the game initially launched with a very limited roster, continued new releases offer new units that fill diverse roles on the tabletop, opening up novel strategies and unique playstyles to suit any aspiring general’s tastes.

Today, we’re here to look at the various non-Hero units available to every Protoss faction and how to use them on the table. If you’re looking for guidance on how to get the most out of your Protoss units, read on.

Core

Every Protoss Faction card gives you 3 Core slots, except Nerazim which only gives you 2. And given that several of the stronger faction cards (including the Nexus and Warp Prism) tack on a few more, you’re likely to find yourself spending at least 5 supply on Core choices.

Thankfully, both of the Core options currently available to Protoss are pretty good: Zealots are a workhorse close range damage-dealer that provides a reasonably durable 2-supply unit suitable for scrapping over objectives, while Adepts combine respectable shooting output with best-in-class mobility and utility tricks.

Zealots

Zealots can only attack in melee, but that’s fine. If they can make contact, Zealots hit pretty damn hard: out of the box, they’ll roll 9 Impact dice on the charge, then follow that up with 12 more attacks in the combat phase. And they’re reasonably tough, too, having 3 shields and 4 HP per unit. That means that killing even a single Zealot requires 7 damage, which is enough damage to rip a big, bloody chunk out of nearly any other Core option.

Zealots are the Core unit in the game that keeps efficiency uninhibited for the most amount of damage, only dropping their first model after taking the 7th point of damage. Protoss players should take advantage of this by positioning Zealots within the control range of mission markers they expect to be contested, or at least able to declare a charge onto any enemy unit that comes within control range. It takes a real damage commitment to threaten Zealots, so we want to use Zealots to force the enemy damage dealers into positions where we can counter-attack and remove them. Sometimes a few Zealots must be sacrificed to win the battle at hand. Their lives are for Aiur after all!

Zealots need Leg Enhancements. The extra damage provided by My Life For Aiur! or the extra durability provided by Zealous Round don’t matter if the unit is not in the correct position, and while at first glance this ability appears to be a 2" move, that actually becomes a 5" stretch thanks to StarCraft TMG’s coherency rules. Don’t bother with We Stand As One, it is the worst of their upgrades as it incentivizes you to group your Zealots up in melee. We have too few units for that. If you have twenty points buy My Life and Zealous Round instead.

Zealots have Speed 4/7, which leaves them 13" away from the Marines. But with Leg Enhancements, the extra 2" move works out to another 5", leaving them within 8" and not only able to charge, but likely to make it.

Would you pay 1 Psionic Energy out of the 7 to 8 you have access to on a turn to have Zealots go from unable to charge anything to 66% of the time they charge their target? I know I would.

Adepts

Adepts are a ranged combat unit with powerful utility. Their shooting isn’t bad: 8 dice for the unit with a d3+1 surge against light is a decent statline. And while their shorter 8” range puts them at risk of being charged, at 3HP with 2 shields, they’re reasonably tough and their Glaive Cannons are better than you’d expect in engagement thanks to Anti-Evade (1).

Psionic Transfer is their standout ability, and it offers the unit absolutely incredible flexibility. Some of the uses are obvious: you can use it to leapfrog a unit up the board to get into position to stand on an objective, or stick it out to give your entire army Precision (1) against a key enemy unit. But the extreme mobility combined with the fact that you don’t need line of sight to place it can open up some really useful options, giving you a decent skirmisher unit that’s near impossible to nail down.

Adepts are your errand-runners. One good combo is to Deploy the Adepts their full speed plus coherency toward an objective within 19” of your entry edge, and then send their Psionic Transfer Shade toward a different objective. In the Assault Phase you can Run with the Adepts and get close enough to Sticky Control of that Mission Marker before teleporting to the Shade.

Protoss Adepts use their Shade to outplay a Terran Siege Tank.

There's a lot going on here, so let's break it down: The Adepts aim for an objective within 19" of their Entry Edge.  In this case they would like to sticky the blue objective and not get shot by the perfidious enemy Siege Tank, then end on the red objective also not able to be shot at next round unless somebody moves. For most units, this is impossible. For Adepts? It's just another day at the office.

They first move 5" plus a 3" stretch, ending outside of 18" of the enemy Tank (move A). Here they spend 1 PE to throw a Psionic Shade to the red objective on their left, which they will teleport to at the end of the round. After the Siege Tank has activated in the Assault Phase, the Adepts can Run for another 8" total which puts them within the 3" control radius of the blue objective (move B). The End of Round Scoring check happens first, and the blue objective becomes "sticky" controlled by our Adepts. Then End of Round Effects occur and the Adepts make their final move to safety (move C). They end within coherency of the shade but not overlapping its base and all within 1" of a wall that will provide them Direct Cover of incoming Terran fire next turn, allowing them to avoid enemy attacks even if the wall isn't tall enough to provide Full Cover.

On top of their outstanding utility, Adepts are also deceptively solid against melee enemies. Unwary foes may try to charge them early and often, but they’re better at standing their ground than you might expect. First, at 2 HP per model with 3 points of shields, your opponent will need to deal at least 5 points of damage to remove a model, giving them a decent chance to absorb any Impact hits on the charge without losing effectiveness. And once they weather that damage, their Glaive Cannons’ Anti-Evade (1) and anti-light surge lets them clap back that much harder.

Upgrade-wise, if you’ve got the points there’s a case to be made for any of them. Resonating Glaives is probably the standout, allowing 4 of these to shoot with slightly more effectiveness than 6 unstimmed Marines, though it does cost 1 PE. Guidance is cheaper and pushes their ranged weapon to Anti-Evade (2), making them more reliable ranged threats both in engagement and against any enemy that can get an on-demand Evade. Glaive Strike is the most situational, upgrading their melee attack to gain both d3 surge and Pierce (2) against Light targets. But if you’re expecting them to eat a lot of charges, both of those rules are great ways to help make sure they acquit themselves well once they get touched.

Elite

Elite slots are, somewhat unsurprisingly, a bit harder to come by than Core, though you’ll have no trouble finding room for plenty of them if you really want a walker-heavy force. Both Protoss Elite options sport the “Armoured” tag and pack weaponry designed to demonstrate their superiority over anything else with it. You’ll generally want at least a couple of these units to deal with any enemy armor, but the specific choice will depend on whether you’d rather combine that anti-armor firepower with impressive mobility or best-in-class durability.

Stalkers

These spindly walkers were Protoss’s only unit with a surge against armoured until the release of the Immortal, and they still find use in that role thanks their Particle Disruptors’ decent shotcount, d3 surge, and flat 2 damage characteristic. And between their 4+ save, 6 HP, and 3 shields, they’re tough enough that taking them down will usually require your opponent to actually try. They’re worth considering including in your list even if only as a reasonably-priced option to deal with armored enemies your other units can struggle to handle.

But the biggest thing Stalkers have going for them is their speed: these guys are fast. If you’re willing to burn 2 PE for Blink and the Warp Prism’s Phase, a pair of Stalkers can move a staggering 25” in a single phase, including 2 Place effects that will allow you to move clear through blocking terrain if needed. Between that and their 12” range, a pair of Stalkers is almost never going to find themselves out of range of whatever unit you want to point them at, allowing them to serve as a potent rapid-reaction force that can exert supply over an objective in one corner of the board one round, then leap clear across to the other side to make an enemy armoured unit regret its life decisions the next.

The Stalker upgrade Path of Shadows is the stand-out of the two upgrades, as gaining Hidden until the next action is performed allows them to force the enemy to either commit a Tactical Card to reveal them or otherwise move units within 4” where the Stalkers still get an Evade for their enemy trouble. Because it can be used in any phase, it also forces enemy shooting to contend with the possibility that the Stalkers will shoot first and then hide after. The other upgrade, Fury of the Nerazim, is for Stalkers that activate after their enemies in the Assault phase, and gives them Instant to thwart enemy defensive Reactions. This upgrade synergizes with Path of Shadows as being successfully Hidden in the Movement Phase means Stalkers can wait to shoot until their key enemy target has acted in the Assault Phase. Fury of the Nerazim is still really good when you can use it, but you'll usually look to Path of Shadows first, if only because it's a lot easier to get mileage out of Fury when your unit can benefit from hidden to wait out enemy attacks.

One final thing to keep in mind is that you don’t have to take a pair of Stalkers. It’s not always going to be an optimal play since one model costs nearly 2/3 of the pair rather than half and adding an activation can mean ceding the initiative in the Assault phase, but there are some upsides worth considering here. First, the unit gets 3 shields regardless of its size, so that single model has 9 effective HP. And if you take 2 of them rather than 1, each of them gets a surge die when they attack. Finally, if you’ve got a single Elite slot you want to fill, a singleton Stalker is currently the only way to do it. At current points costs, you’ll frequently find that it’s not worth it, but it is the cheapest non-Hero way to add some anti-armored punch to your list.

Immortals

An Immortal is not only likely the single toughest unit in the game right now, it also packs some serious heat on offense. Its stock weapons, Photon Disruptors, hit like trucks, and will absolutely tear through armored targets since their dice are split between two profiles, each of which gets a surge die. And if they’re not powerful enough for you, each can be upgraded to a Phase Disruptor, which trade away the Long Range (16”) rule to deal 3 damage against armoured units and get rid of the base weapons’ Concentrated Fire (1). This makes the unit even deadlier into nearly all targets, and it’s often worth swapping out at least one.

The use-case here is very straightforward: put one in the middle of the table and force your opponent to deal with it. Between Improved Barrier and Shield Overcharge, you’ve got two very powerful defensive abilities right there on the sheet, and it’s a great target for defensive buffs from Tactical cards.

Speaking of Tactical cards, Warp Prism is an excellent card to help you get every drop of utility out of the Immortal. Use it in the early game to get the Immortal onto the table in a forward position so it can step out and immediately start contesting objectives and ruining enemy armor’s day. But it really comes into its own later in the game, when Phase allows you to blink the thing out of engagement, then move 7”. Used late in the movement phase, it can let you avoid the engagement penalty for falling back and prevent enemies from taking Evade rolls against it. Using it like this might not be as obnoxious as solid Warp Prism/Immortal micro in the PC game can, but it’s a useful trick to keep in mind when it counts.

Protoss Immortal for StarCraft: the Miniatures Game | Credit: Blizzard Entertainment/Archon Studios

Most of the upgrades on Immortals are worth considering. For the Ancients is the standout, giving you Precision (1) against targets more than 8” away. Thanks to the many movement tricks available to Protoss, maintaining that minimum range against whatever you’re trying to kill often won’t be difficult, and the rule triggers on both the unit’s guns, making it more potent than you might expect. And as we mentioned before, swapping out at least one of the guns for Phase Disruptors is often worth a look, as it’ll make your Immortal better at dealing with basically any unit that isn’t a Hero. Finally, if you’ve got the points, Shield Overcharge is worth a look, especially if you’re worried about being overwhelmed with lots of low-damage attacks more than taking a few high-damage ones. The final upgrade, Fury Unyielding, isn’t bad when it triggers, but it’s often too situational to be worthwhile: both the Immortal and its target have to be in range of the same objective, and there’s no lack of 20-point upgrades for Protoss units that don’t come with that sort of condition on their use.

Support

Finally, we’ve got the Support units. Sentries are the sort of defensive unit you’re probably expecting to see in a support role, offering multiple ways to boost nearby units’ durability and frustrate your opponent’s plans to engage. High Templar, on the other hand, remain true to their counterparts in the PC games as a more offensive caster archetype that can deal impressive damage to opponents that overextend and discourage them from playing aggressively.

Sentries

Sentries are your defensive support choice, offering very little in the way of offensive output but bringing a couple of nice defensive options and one of the trickiest utility tools in the game. Guardian Shield is the first defensive choice, letting you choose a friendly unit within 4” and removing a die from every ranged attack made against it this round. The effectiveness of this ability turns on a couple of factors, but taking dice out of the attack pool is incredibly powerful since it lowers the maximum amount of damage that unit can do. Plus, since the ability isn’t a Reaction, it works even if you’re targeted by an Instant attack, making it particularly great in the Protoss mirror. The other defensive option is Restoration, which lets you clear all the debuffs off of a unit as a reaction to your opponent applying a new one. Functioning as a “counter” to the buff you really care about is good enough, but flushing off any others you’ve managed to accumulate is solid as well. And while there currently aren’t that many Debuffs you’ll have to worry about, this ability will likely really come into its own once Stukov and his ability to apply the Infested status to your units makes it out into the wild.

Generally, Guardian Shield and Restoration (as well as the Hallucination upgrade, which we’ll discuss below) define the Sentry’s role as a support unit that follows a handful of units around and makes them that much harder to remove. Having a unit of Sentries follow some Zealots around is a solid use-case that will help make sure that melee unit gets into position to do what you brought it for, but just about any unit will appreciate the extra durability they bring. And while Sentries aren’t that tough on their own, any attack your opponent sends against them is an attack they’re not putting into a real damage-dealer, so losing them is often fine if it means a more dangerous unit gets where it’s going unscathed.

Force Fields are an incredibly powerful trick, letting you corral your opponent’s key units by keeping them away from objectives and vulnerable units. An important thing to keep in mind is that Force Fields aren’t terrain, which has pros and cons. The major downside is that you can’t use them to block line of sight, but in exchange units like Raptors who ignore terrain still have to find a way around them. A well-placed force field can absolutely ruin your opponent’s day and change the momentum of the battle round. Played wisely, you can split your opponent’s force in two, protect vulnerable units from charges, and just generally be a tremendous pain in the neck, all for the reasonable cost of 1 PE.

Because the Sentries and Force Field are each less than 1" from each other and the nearby walls, the Marines can't walk past them.

Unfortunately, while a single force field is usually enough to stop bigger units like Siege Tanks, Queens, and Immortals from going wherever they want, the gap clearance rules make it harder to lock down anything size 2 or smaller. Thankfully, there are two ways to get around this: the obvious one is the Solid-Field Projectors upgrade, which lets your unit drop a second Force Field, though you’ll still have to pay for both. But there’s another, free option: use the Sentries to bodyblock. You can set them up just under 1” apart from each other, then drop a Force Field just under 1” from one of them. Line them up properly, and you can plug a gap of up to 10.5”, so long as they can stay alive. This won’t work as well if there are ranged enemies nearby who can shoot them off the table so another threat can run or charge through, but it’s a solid option that can slow your opponent down in a pinch.

Upgrades-wise, Hallucination is the go-to choice. It’s a 0 PE reaction that allows a unit within 4” to make an Evade roll against a ranged attack. It’s not quite as powerful as the High Templar Farsight upgrade, and we’ll discuss the differences in more detail below, but it’s still worth taking as another way to keep a key unit alive.

High Templar

On the other hand, High Templar serve as an offensive spellcaster that punishes opponents for poor positioning. Un-upgraded, they’re fine, if a little unimpressive. They’ve got a ranged attack that’s a little better than a Sentry’s (if only because it has a surge value), and their core ability, Feedback, means that enemy Psionic or Mechanized units that rely on Special Abilities have to be judicious about using them or they’ll take a pile of nonlethal damage, leaving them at risk of dying to a stiff breeze.

But if you’re asking how to use them, Psionic Storm is what you’re probably here for. It’s pricey at 2 PE, but you can use it to really mess with your opponent’s game plan. Hold your unit’s activation until later in the round and you can all but guarantee both a chunk of damage on a key unit and force your opponent to decide if they really want to keep standing in that particular spot. You can also preemptively cast it near objectives or choke points to discourage your opponent from sending units to contest them, potentially leaving you free to move in with impunity. And if an enemy unit is in your storm at the end of the Movement phase, you can charge it to force it to take more damage at the end of the Assault phase, though doing so will probably result in the charging unit catching some strays. Just keep in mind that Protoss are currently the only army without a 2-resource Tactical card, meaning that you’ll have to either flip two cards to cast this, or find a way to reduce the cost.

But don’t sleep on Farsight: it lets you choose a unit within 12” to be allowed to make an Evade roll against any number of attacks this round. This functions like a more powerful version of the Sentries’ Hallucination, letting you use it from further away and potentially providing a benefit more than once per round, but it does have two notable downsides. First, it costs 1 PE, meaning you’ll have to spend a resource to get the benefit. Given the magnitude of the effect, that’s probably fine, but keep it in mind. The other downside is the more interesting one: you have to use it in the Movement phase, not as a reaction to your opponent’s shooting. Forcing you to use it on the front end means that your opponent knows for certain whether the rule is in play, potentially informing which units they choose to attack. This isn’t necessarily always going to be a bad thing–if there’s only one unit available for them to attack then you know where to put this–but the closer and scrappier the game is, the more important it’ll be to make sure you get this right. By contrast, Hallucination is more limited both in range and number of uses, but the fact that it’s a reaction means you have some certainty that it’ll matter when you need it to.

The choice of upgrades also impacts how large of a unit you’ll want: if you take the larger, 4-model unit, it gets a 1 PE discount on its first Special Ability each round and adds 1 to the rolls to see how much damage its Psionic Storm deals. This is obviously worth considering for a unit you’re planning to cast Storm with, since every time it does so it’ll be both cheaper and more effective. But Farsight? At 1 PE, there are plenty of other ways to bring the cost down to 0, and you might be fine with hard-casting it in a pinch. Smaller units of High Templar can also afford to play a little more aggressively to make use of feedback, since they’re only 130 points. If you’re looking at High Templar primarily as a vector for getting Evade rolls on your Zealots and Adepts, consider whether you can get away with saving 70 points by only bringing 2.

Final Thoughts

Protoss units are strong, tough, and tricky, frequently requiring smart play and finesse to get the most out of them. But if you’ve got a good sense of how your units work and what their highest and best use is, you can absolutely tear through hordes of enemy units and leave them wondering whether being outnumbered was actually better for you.

In a 2000 point list, you’ll almost certainly want to bring an Immortal to serve as a durable central objective holder. It’ll also double as a way to deal with enemy armor, and you can add a little more punch to that side of things by bringing some Stalkers. Those Stalkers, in turn, combine with a couple cheap units of Adepts to give you plenty of movement tricks that will help you flexibly adapt to your enemy’s gameplan. And when it comes time to really get into a scrap over a contested objective, send in the Zealots to slice your foes to ribbons. Backstop all of this with some support units to taste, and Protoss units offer a solid foundation to build on, even if they’re a bit tricky to get the most value out of.
