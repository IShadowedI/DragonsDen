Magic: The Gathering Reality Fracture Review, Part 1 of 4 - The Mechanics

September 24th, 2026 via Goonhammer
At the crossroads of Jace’s rampant egomania and ‘what if I drew a fucked up, evil version of the Hamburger Helper’ lies Reality Fracture, Magic’s newest set. This is the first Planeswalkers-matter set since 2019’s War of the Spark, or should we say Planeswalker, singular, as Jace is the set’s focal point. Reality Fracture is filled with powerful cards and their alternate universe counterparts, offering both novel mechanical takes on beloved characters, and imaginative ‘what-if’ scenarios for Vorthoses. To that end, this set is built for pick-2 draft, with both versions of many characters appearing alongside one another in the same pack. After a couple of middling releases, this set is probably the most powerful since Avatar, and we expect a host of multi-format staples to solidify once players get their hands on Reality Fracture. This week, we’re covering the set’s mechanics, focusing on two new types of token

Reality Fracture officially releases to tabletop on October 2nd, and on Magic: The Gathering Arena on September 29th.

New Mechanics

Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Empower (Jace)

This is a new spin on Amass, but instead of a growing Army, you’re afforded a Jace token with the appropriate Loyalty counters. This is a mechanically intricate action, with niche cases like being able to rapidly ultimate a ‘normal’ Jace that’s been turned into a token, or borrow the abilities of Planeswalkers like Kasmina, Enigma Sage. Unlike the original Amass, the designers left themselves room to create different versions of Empower in future sets, now that Jace has left the narrative. These would create different tokens, but still retain the timing and counter placement of the core Empower mechanic.

Saffgor: In its simplest form, you can evaluate all instances of Empower of 2 or less as ‘Surveil 1-ish’, and 3+ as ‘Draw 1 card-ish’, because the Jace token can do so much more if it’s allowed to. Against decks worried about the value game, like all Planeswalkers he effectively gains you life, and in eternal formats Reality Scramble flips him into whatever other one Planeswalker you have in your Library. It’s shocking how many niche interactions are propelled by token Planeswalkers arriving via non-Planeswalker cards, and I even got a chance to cover a new 2-card combo enabled by Empower Jace in last week’s Puzzlebox. One last thing—that Surveil mode is usually available every turn he persists, and this helps colors which otherwise lack the ability to fill the yard (Red & White, mostly) to start putting things in the bin. For decks like my beloved Gau, Feral Youth or White reanimator by way of Queen of Dale, this is a godsend.

BPhillipYork: Empower Jace is kind of weird. Obviously he's very front and center in this set, and the ability to have random planeswalkers popping into play is an interesting one with all the cards that have "planeswalkers you control have X". Because this is like Amass in that you can't have multiple Jace tokens via Empower Jace, it's not too abusive. Obviously if you just want to pump up a Jace you've made a token of to ultimate very quickly it's a way to do that, but it'll take a lot of mana. If this mechanic returns with other planeswalkers, which I could easily see with Empower Chandra and Empower Liliana in other sets, it could be a pretty interesting set of abilities.

FromTheShire: This is a pretty neat and mechanically interesting ability. At this point I'm wildly disconnected from the story and its justification for these tokens so I'm not sure exactly how likely we actually are to see this pop up again on other walkers, but the space is certainly wide open. There's a lot of nifty little tricks you can pull now, and I'm sure more will be discovered as people start playing with the cards. In 60 card there are a couple of quite powerful seeming options like Jace's Machinations that give you a massive amount of loyalty for cheap, and I believe this is the first time we are going to have planeswalkers be legal in Pauper so it will be cool to see what people brew there. Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Heartwood Tokens

Red & Green have a new token to build around in Reality Fracture: Heartwood. This is like a less conditional Powerstone token, with it usually coming in untapped, and having the capacity to add {R} or {G}. Often, it’s tied to the signature spell of the Konstrari mages in the RG color identity, being a 3 mana Prepared Sorcery called Soul Tether, although there are several other ways to produce them in Gruul.

Saffgor: In terms of mana rock tokens that stick around, Heartwood is probably the strongest we’ve seen. Less conditional than Vibranium or Powerstones, more enduring than Treasure or Gold, and fairly accessible for RGx Commanders. We’ve already seen a half-dozen genuinely playable cards which create these, so I’d expect to see Heartwood at the Commander table with reasonable frequency.

BPhillipYork: Red/Green having a mana rock artifact token feels very off to me, like color-pie wise, which is part of the point of the set. These tokens though are potentially very broken. They enter untapped, there's ways to duplicate them again, or use effects like Doubling Season. In the end these will probably end up like Powerstone tokens, not all that impactful except for decks that really focus on them.

FromTheShire: The fact that these enter untapped, in the token doubling color, means that while they are going to be quite useful little mana rocks as a baseline sometimes they are going to juice a turn through the roof. The main question will be if you can make and utilize them well enough to justify them being more fragile than regular old land based ramp since you're in green already. We have been getting a slow trickle of RG artifacts matter commanders like Roxanne, Starfall Savant and Bello, Bard of the Brambles that enjoy these as well, especially Roxanne.

Returning Mechanics

Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Prepare

Prepare is back, and its inclusion here really makes Secrets of Strixhaven and Reality Fracture feel akin to a 2-set block. Creatures with a Prepared Spell are either Unprepared, or Prepared, and in the latter case a copy of their Prepared Spell sits in exile, waiting to be put into the stack & cast during its appropriate timing window. Once a Prepared Spell enters the stack, its linked object, the Creature, becomes Unprepared. Unlike Strixhaven, the majority of Prepare Creatures in this set share one of five Spells, representing the regimented doctrine of Hexhaven, so if you see a multicolored Prepare Spell, expect it on more than one card in the set.

Saffgor: I think, now having seen all of Reality Fracture, Prepare might be the most successful ‘double spell’ mechanic we’ve had since Adventures. That’s a high bar, but I do genuinely love the mechanic, and repeatable casts make Spells you’d otherwise not include in an eternal-format deck far more worthwhile. I think the actual scaffolding of Prepare is a bit weird (keeping a copy of a Spell in exile until you need it is weird, and didn’t “work” until the SOS FAQ), but once you have a general understanding it’s easy to love. One thing worth noting is that there’s cards in the set aimed at quietly ‘teaching’ some of its intricacies, with Heartwood Crafter supplying mana for Spells cast from outside your hand, and its Soul Tether being in exile, e.g. outside of your hand. Cards which teach their own mechanics scratch the design itch that a lot of modern Magic tends not to.

BPhillipYork: I like prepare, though it's basically an ETB with additional costs, or sort of a weird version of monstrous, a one shot ability unless you have ways to flicker it (though monstrous is the same). I think it's a useful design tool, and I'd like to see more things that care about using prepared spells show up. It's also neat to see iconic spells like Swords to Plowshares or Lightning Bolt though hopefully they don't just exploit them for nostalgia.

FromTheShire: Prepared spells have proven to be quite successful so far, with a number of them being playable and no hideously broken misses yet. As long as we don't wind up with a busted way to mass prepare at some point I think we should be okay. Credit: Wizards of the Coast.

Cameo Mechanics

As you can tell, the number of core mechanics in Reality Fracture is fairly minimal, which is a shock given the overall complexity of most modern sets. That's because part of the design of the set is predicated not on introducing new keywords or abilities, but calling back to previous planes and characters via 'Cameo Mechanics', a concept introduced in 2023. Split Second, Exhaust, Convoke, and more all return here, giving many of the Echoverse cards the energy of being from a set like Commander Masters—a celebration of Magic's history.

Saffgor: As the number of sets each year has climbed, I think this is a smart move on the part of the designers; gone are the days of five flashy new mechanics in something like a Ravnica set, because with ~6 sets each year that represents an information overload to all but the most enfranchised. Every set is someone's first set, so while there might be a bit of confusion over these one-off mechanics, with Commander being the most popular format basically all players will come to see them anyways. The average complexity is up, and it seems reasonable to expect the average willingness to digest complexity of a new player likely is as well.

BPhillipYork: There's a bunch of weird static abilities showing up, this one in particular is really kind of nuts. It's fun to have callbacks to specific mechanics and I wish they'd do more of this, so you could build a Commander deck around Arcane cards or something like that, though "everything has split second" isn't so much about split second cards.

FromTheShire: I would 100% be in favor of more sets like this. Magic already has so, so many mechanics (many of them functioning extremely similarly) that sometimes it really begs the question why we needed a different keyword for it in the newest set, only for it to be immediately abandoned. Maybe it all boils down to Kicker being such a good, foundational mechanic they can't get away from repackaging it as something new over and over again.
