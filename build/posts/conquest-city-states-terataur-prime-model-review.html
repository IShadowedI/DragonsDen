Conquest: City States Terataur Prime Model Review

November 24th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Hello friends, welcome back to the farmyard. Farmer DanDaBossMan here to give you a “taur” of our newest edition to the barn: The Terataur Prime. This beauty can run 60mph and put a world of hurt onto anyone who gets in the way. Boy, that's a lot of beef metal.

In today’s guide I would like to introduce to you the Terataur Prime and my experiences putting it together and painting it. City States are due an update very soon so a rules review will follow sometime soon.

As always if you want to get 10% off and support Tabletop Battles you can make your Conquest purchase by clicking here. You’ll also need to enter code “TTB” at checkout.

Building the Terataur Prime

To start with the model seems to be cast resin, giving it a lot more durability and the pieces are quite chunky. (Press F to anyone who’s tried assembly flimsy plastic Inquisitors). The main body comes in one piece and you get the choice of a flaming mace or an axe as a weapon, and a choice of two heads. Because I’m a glutton for punishment I went for the flaming mace. The resin is quite crisp with strong detail throughout making it a very nice model to paint.

Terataur Prime unassembled. Credit: DanDaBossMan

The only part of the building process that was difficult was the back pipes. Make sure you hold onto them while the glue dries as I found the contact points quite shallow and kept falling off while waiting to dry.

Overall, after being filed, having a soapy bath and a brush down, building the Terataur Prime was easy so I’d probably score it as 3/10 difficulty in the worst case.

Painting the Terataur Prime

Terataur Prime (front). Credit: DanDaBossMan

Before talking through the process and scheme, these are the paints (and any Games Workshop alternatives) used for those interested:

  • Base brass: Two Thin Coats: Battle Axe Brass (Games Workshop: Warplock Bronze/Runelord Brass)
  • Base silver: Games Workshop Leadbelcher
  • Highlight silver: Vallejo Model Color Silver (Games Workshop: Runefang Steel)
  • Base yellow: Warpaints Fanatic Fiendish Yellow (Games Workshop: Averland Sunset)
  • Highlight yellow: Games Workshop Flash Gitz Yellow
  • Base pink: Games Workshop Pink Horror
  • Highlight pink: Games Workshop Emperor’s Children
  • Base green: Mixed recipe (Games Workshop Caliban Green)
  • Highlight green: Games Workshop Warpstone Glow, Games Workshop Moot Green
  • Base brown: Games Workshop Mournfang Brown
  • Base black: Warpaints Fanatic Matt Black (Games Workshop Abaddon Black)
  • Base white: Game color White (Games Workshop Corax White)
  • Base red: Games Workshop Mephiston Red
  • Black wash: Games Workshop Nuln Oil
  • Brown wash: Warpaints Fanatic Soft Tone (Games Workshop Agrax Earthshade)
  • Red wash: Warpaints Fanatic Red Tone (Games Workshop Carroburg Crimson)

As you may be able to see, I’ve been migrating away from Games Workshop paints in the last few years towards dropper bottles. Most of my paints are on their last legs from drying out due to crusting on the lid.

Terataur Prime (back). Credit: DanDaBossMan

At its most basic level the Terataur Prime is simple to paint. There are large open, flat panels and body parts and it is made even easier painting bronze and silver as these colours are quite forgiving and easy to fix.

I started by base coating the whole model brass and silver with two thin coats (thanks Duncan) before applying a liberal black wash. Once dried I highlighted the brass by using a 50:50 mix of Battle Axe Brass and Silver, followed by Silver to edge highlight and apply to the most raised surfaces to give it a super shiny effect. For the silver itself, after the wash, I highlighted with Silver all edges and raised surfaces as per the brass to make it shiny. I like it shiny.

Next up was the cloth and pauldrons. Shockingly your mileage varies here as you may want to follow the box art and do brass, bronze or gold for the pauldrons. If for some reason you want to join the Battenberg Squashies I can reveal to you my forbidden secrets of getting yellow to stay yellow.

Firstly, make sure it's primed white, such as using Games Workshop White Scar spraypaint, otherwise you’re going to have a bad time. Second, apply a base coat of Games Workshop Averland Sunset or Warpaints Fanatic Fiendish Yellow, or equivalent. You will need two, maybe three thin coats here. Next a wash of Warpaints Fanatic Soft Tone, or equivalent, watered down 50:50 and applied to the recesses. Once dried, the hard part is getting a strong bright yellow. I used Games Workshop Flash Gitz Yellow (or equivalent) and watered it down 50:50. The watering down is the important part here. When applied raw, or near raw, the paint leaves thick paint stroke marks that are hard to get rid of, and leaves visible dark streaks underneath of the undercoat. You want a strong solid layer here so apply the watered down yellow and let it pool to a reasonable degree. Once dried, apply as many coats as necessary to get the solid colour. The more the merrier. It shouldn't take many before you get a solid coat. Once done, I used Game Color White as a 50:50 mix with the Flash Gitz Yellow to start edge highlighting before finally using the White as a tip highlight on rivets and any sharp corners etc. I would say this is a delicate process and to be fair it is, getting other paints on your yellow can be an absolute pain to fix. That said I’ve found using the watered down Flash Gitz Yellow is able to cover up mistakes quite easily.

Terataur Prime (left). Credit: DanDaBossMan

As for the pink, I started with a base coat of Games Workshop Pink Horror before washing with Warpaints Fanatic Red Tone as per yellow above. Next I highlighted with Games Workshop Emperor’s Children. Thankfully this paint is strong enough to not need to be watered down like Flash Gitz Yellow so multiple applications are smooth and strong. Finally I highlighted as per yellow by using a 50:50 mix of Emperor’s Children and White.

The laurels, while blue in the box art, I decided to paint brown as leather. I used Games Workshop Mournfang Brown, washed with Nuln Oil, and highlighted back with Mournfang Brown and a 50:50 mix of White.

Next was the flaming mace. Just looking at the box art made me sweat and anxious. I decided not to use standard flame colours cuz reasons. That said, it follows the same pattern so choose any fire colour you like. This required a little bit of experimentation to figure out how best to do it.

For the grill, I started by using Games Workshop Warpstone Glow over a White basecoat. Once dried, I applied a dab of White into the ends of each side of the grill. Using a little bit of the Warpstone Glow I wet blended them together before applying a final dot of White into the corner of each recess. I then used a 50:50 mix of Games Workshop Warpstone Glow and Warpaints Fanatic Matt Black (or Games Workshop Caliban Green if you’re feeling spicey) to edge highlight the top of the grill going almost all the way to each end. Finally I used Matt Black to highlight the middle third.

Terataur Prime (mace). Credit: DanDaBossMan

I hate doing fire, even though I play Tzeentch Daemons and took it upon myself to have rainbow fire. That said, it wasn't too difficult. Start by applying a 50:50 mix of Warpstone Glow and Matt Black, or Caliban Green, from the tip of the flame to about one third from the mace. Then apply Warpstone glow to the remaining third. A liberal black wash here on the tip of the flame to the middle helps darken it down a bit. Now, this is where fire diverges from conventional painting. The inner parts of the fire are the highlights and the outer parts are darkest. So I started applying a layer of Games Workshop Moot Green to the recesses of the fire, followed by a small stroke of White. Finally I glazed with a watered down Moot Green to blend it a bit. You may notice there are reflections of the fire on the mace. This was also anxiety inducing. While not perfect I used the same glaze to highlight areas near to the flame where some green would be reflected. Not my best work but it’ll do.

The hot back pipes were another anxiety inducing affair so let me help you out here. I base coated the bottom third with Fiendish Yellow into a solid colour. After this I began mixing Games Workshop Mephiston Red with the yellow in various stages, a tiny bit of red at a time. This technique relies heavily on 50:50 washes like the yellow pauldrons previously discussed. With an orangey colour, I began applying watered down paint to the middle of the pipe, followed by watered down yellow below it and blending them carefully. I then applied some Mephiston Red above the orange and wet blended that before applying some Matt Black above the red and finally blended the red and black. This took several attempts as hubris got in the way and I started blending bits before they were properly dry causing me to have to go over it again. For your own sanity, wait for the layer to dry before doing another pass. You’ll find that it probably isn’t perfect but it doesn’t need to be. Don’t get cocky here like I did.

Terataur Prime (back pipes). Credit: DanDaBossMan

The black pipes were base coated with Matt Black, followed by a 75:25 mix of Matt Black and White on areas that catch the light i.e. the top. I then edge highlighted with a 50:50 mix, followed by a 25:75 mix then a watered down Nuln Oil wash to bring it all together and darken the colours a little.

For the eyes, I base coated Fiendish Yellow and began applying watered down 50:50 coats of Fiendish Yellow and Mephiston Red to get an orange and dabbing the colour carefully with a very pointy brush around the eyes. Finally I added two dots of White to complete the look.

And there you have it, one Terataur Prime a la Battenberg *chefs kiss*. It may not win any Golden Daemons, but it's done, and isn’t that the best a wargamer can ever hope for?

Terataur Prime (right). Credit: DanDaBossMan

Final Thoughts

Overall the Terataur Prime was a pleasant model to paint and very little drama other than the self inflicted mental anguish that would make a Cenobite blush. If you’re not feeling confident with flaming effects I’d highly recommend the axe instead and not worrying about the hot back pipes. The box art is always just a suggestion anyway. I had a lot of fun painting this little big guy and I’m sure you will too. If you have any questions about this model or any techniques, don’t hesitate to ask!
