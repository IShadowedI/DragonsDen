BattleTech Fiction Focus: Ghost Stories

October 30th, 2026 via Goonhammer

BattleTech has a reputation as a relatively grounded setting as far as mecha franchises go. Sure, there's some inherent absurdity in any story built around the premise that towering bipedal machines are the future of warfare, and sure, a lot of BattleTech's numbers don't hold up to even the slightest interrogation (weapon ranges, armor technology, "FASAnomics," etc.), but there's a distinct lack of psychic teenagers, pacts with dark gods, sapient aliens, et cetera. It tells stories about human wars fought over human ambitions. No supernatural powers, no funny business, just people being people, and that's it.

...or is it? One thing about people being people is that people constantly tell stories, and those stories have a tendency to grow in the telling. The Inner Sphere is a huge setting, too, spanning worlds far beyond our own ball of dirt. If our world, even in an era where many people have cameras in their pockets every moment they're outside of the house, has room enough for rumors to breed in shadows, room for mystery, room for the unexplained, can you fathom how much more room the BattleTech setting has for strange occurrences which defy simple explanation? How much more space there is for those stories to spread and grow and fester?

Today, in honor of our world's designated Spooky Season, I am staring into the abyssal depths of BattleTech, plumbing its darkest corners for its strangest tales. Welcome to BattleTech Fiction Focus: Ghost Stories!

Automaton Legion Marauder. Credit: Valk

What Isn't Here

I want to make a couple quick notes about what I haven't included. I'm not going to unpack the controversial "Phantom Mech" ability displayed by Morgan Kell and others, probably BattleTech fiction's best-known example of "early installment weirdness." It's my intent to circle back around to cover classic BattleTech fiction in the future, and I'm sure I'll talk about it at some point, but it doesn't feel particularly on-theme for our Halloween episode here. On the other hand, "Thirteen," Craig A. Reed Jr.'s tale of an ostensibly-haunted Zeus, would be perfectly on-theme, but it's also basically lost media at the moment, as a story from the now-defunct "BattleCorps" publishing initiative which has yet to be collected into a modern anthology, so I won't be covering it, either. I'm also sticking to fiction, so there's nothing here from RPG modules or sourcebooks like Necromo Nightmare, Jihad Conspiracies, etc.. I'm also steering clear of the BattleTech: Gothic alternate universe for this article; it's something else I might cover in the future, but I didn't want to stuff it in here alongside "main universe" content. So if you're rushing to the comments to tell me I missed something, just be aware that, fallible though I am, I've consciously curated this collection.

Narrative Perspective and Canon

An interesting wrinkle to keep in mind as we approach the hazier portions of BattleTech canon is that third-person fiction is generally considered the highest form of canon in this shared universe. BattleTech sourcebooks are (almost always) deliberately presented as compilations of in-universe sources to introduce the idea that if they're incorrect, it's because characters in-setting, with their own biases and limited viewpoints, either made a mistake or consciously massaged the facts. Fiction presented from a third-person perspective, on the other hand, comes from an out-of-universe, omniscient viewpoint, and is considered to be an objective, rather than subjective, source.

Rarely,  published BattleTech fiction introduces ambiguity by bringing in first-person narration. This is, for example, how April Fools story "Ace Darwin and the Flying Pig" presented an in-continuity, main-universe story featuring the UrbanMech LAM without technically canonizing the UrbieLAM as a thing that ever definitively existed. Did Ace Darwin, known as a self-mythologizing fabulist, tell stories about encountering an UrbieLAM? Absolutely, that's 100% canon. Did he actually ever see one? Well, that we may never know.

I'll note throughout this article when a first-person perspective is used to obfuscate certain spooky claims, but honestly it turns up less in BattleTech's ghost stories than one might think! Even at the weird and wild edge of its fiction publications, Catalyst prefers to operate in third-person wherever possible. (Take note, prospective Shrapnel authors!)

Anyway, that's enough preamble. Why don't we dig into the feast?

Photo Credit: Thundercloud1

The Black Marauder

The elephant in the room of BattleTech's spooky stories is the mech (or... entity?) known to its creator, author Lance Scarinci, as The Dark One, and to fans as The Black Marauder. Its tale started on BattleCorps, found continued life in a dedicated anthology (simply titled Marauder), and most recently appeared in the pages of Shrapnel (in issue #10's story "Wolf Pelts and Raven Feathers").

Fittingly for an in-universe legend, the Black Marauder has taken on a life of its own in fandom, inspiring fanart, kitbashes, narrative scenarios, gaming events, and more. It has breached the containment of fiction in-universe, as well, with rumors of its exploits appearing in Recognition Guide: Classics and ilKhan's Eyes Only. But what of the original stories? Let's take a look!

Marauder

The first tale of The Dark One takes place in 3073, on a mining world called Warren in the Federated Suns' Capellan March. A relatively simple story, it's entirely built around crotchety old sergeant Grange leading the young Kevin Langstrom, an ambitious up-and-comer with his sights on greater glory than the Capellan March Militia can provide, to an old Marauder hidden in a played-out mine shaft. Grange feels the growing fires of the Jihad will require the use of every tool available, but he cautions Kevin with stories of the Marauder's mysterious past and dark reputation. A sinister-looking machine (consistently described as having the appearance of a classic [pre-Project-Phoenix] Marauder, but subtly and disconcertingly off in its dimensions) with light-swallowing armor and a complete lack of proper serial numbers, Grange's unit salvaged it from the surface of an asteroid in an uninhabited system, shortly after the Fourth Succession War. Strange occurrences and unnatural deaths followed, until Grange's CO ordered him to "get rid of it," and he buried it in the mine.

Most of the spookiness here comes from the story-within-a-story Grange tells of the Marauder's history. In the darkness of a mine, paranoia fueled by Grange's narrative, Kevin certainly perceives the Marauder as sinister, and he does hear a voice and lose some time after first entering its cockpit, but everything that truly ventures into the realm of the unexplained (the tech killed by an impossible PPC discharge from the shut-down mech, the pilot who vanished despite the mech's cockpit being locked from the inside, etc.) is told second-hand. We also see it all from Kevin's skeptical perspective, as he dismisses and diminishes the mech's alleged threat. It's not a bad introduction, a classic campfire tale, but it's very much just setting the stage for what's to come.

Kindred Soul

A running theme in the Black Marauder stories is that they are fairly eager to provide non-supernatural explanations for maledictions which follow The Dark One. As the series goes on, these increasingly involve weird science, secret Star League experimentation, that sort of thing, but "Kindred Soul" is a lot more straightforward. You can read it as a story of the malevolent mech's growing influence on Kevin Langstrom leading him down an escalating path of obsession, murder, betrayal, desertion, and predation. Alternatively, nearly everything here can be explained by Kevin Langstrom being kind of a huge asshole!

Despite the memorable visual of a bloody corpse providing "special lubricant" for the Black Marauder's balky leg, I found this the least compelling of the Marauder's stories. It's most interesting in retrospect... Langstrom's "Black Kevin" pirate identity in the following story is a big step in a more sinister direction from these humble origins, which raises the question... How much of that is the man learning to cultivate his fearsome mystique, and how much of that is the mech's growing hold on its pilot? On its own merits, though, this feels a bit like one of those prequel movies we never really needed to see, providing little more than an excuse for Kevin and The Dark One to step beyond the military setting BattleTech initially expected of them.

Ravager

The only Black Marauder story (so far) to be written entirely in the first person, "Ravager" is told from the perspective of pirate storyteller Eddie, who ran with Black Kevin in 3080, during his time as a pirate on Tortuga. While this technically means nothing in the story is verifiable beyond what gets echoed in later third-person stories, the interplay of layers of fact and myth here is more complex than you might expect, and I personally feel it's the most well-constructed tale in the sequence.

The most uncanny aspects of the Black Marauder's appearance, which are eagerly repeated in fan depictions (its ability to turn its neck like an animal, its eyes, its mouth), come from the story-within-a-story which kicks off Ravager, but Eddie takes a more sober tone as he gets into his own misadventures with Black Kevin. I find the story he paints particularly compelling. His Black Kevin is human... reliant on allies, forced to play pirate politics, capable of being tricked and even thwarted in his ambitions... yet still unsettling. He lacks moral compunctions even by pirate standards, he creeps out everyone around him, and he invokes a direct connection to cosmic horror for the first time in the Black Marauder stories.

"I've heard of a world." Black Kevin's voice, calm over my sobs. "A place where the Star League poked through, and the beyond bleeds into our universe. The night side glows, like perdition. Destiny, Eddie, and... destination. That's where their footsteps lead. Or begin. Defiance--even Tortuga--is just a stepping-stone. Clean yourself up. We launch in twenty."

I love it. I'm a sucker for a calm madman.

Despite Ravager's reliance on an admittedly-unreliable narrator ("I just tell it like I hear it... with maybe a few embellishments," says Eddie), it ends on perhaps the most overtly magic-destroying moment in the entire series. After describing how Kevin walked through the heart of Raider's Roost on Tortuga on foot with his Marauder following behind, cockpit open and empty, no one at the controls, Eddie ends the story by calling our attention back to a Hi-Scout drone carrier which briefly appeared earlier in the story, with an implication that Kevin forced his closest pirate ally, an addict called Twitch, to remote-control the Marauder to accomplish that display (and then let him starve to death in the room he sealed him in, because asshole pirate).

Ghost Hunting

Ravager was the last of the BattleCorps-published, Jihad-era Black Marauder stories. "Ghost Hunting," which first appeared in the collected Marauder anthology, jumps ahead to the ilClan era, June-July 3151, following Brotherhood of Randis Knight-Errant Perseus "Percy" Schell and Star Commander Lucien Clearwater, his contact in Clan Snow Raven's Watch, as they embark on a hunt for a recently-returned Dark One.

There's a decent twist in this story, but the best part of it is the characters. The hunt is personal for Percy, who's the grandson of a pregnant woman Black Kevin used as a human shield during his ill-starred raid on Defiance (planet, not manufacturer) in the last story. He's a believer in the occult with a well-worn (physical! paper!) notebook filled with myths and rumors of The Dark One, and has a personal conviction that, even though he'd be over a century old, Black Kevin Langstrom must still be at its controls. It's his Raven counterpart who really steals the show, though. "The Red Raven," Lucien Clearwater, comports himself as an impulsive, flamboyant, obnoxious pretty-boy, but with the brooding, calculating core of a Raven Warrior underneath. He flits in and out of Percy's circle of confidants on his own mercurial whims, and I found his antics invariably entertaining.

After three straight stories of The Dark One clearly being a mech piloted by a man, with our perspective on its side, the tone here shifts starkly in the direction of making it less mortal, less knowable. This isn't necessarily a supernatural shift, however. Lucien seeds the idea of the Black Marauder as, basically, an ineffable interdimensional being, some thing from the fourth spacial dimension accessed by jump drives intruding on our three-dimensional world, perceived as a mech because our minds can't comprehend the truth of it. That would also be a departure from BattleTech-normative occurrences, obviously, but rather different from a ghost or demon in flavor.

Ghost Hunting leaves off with Percy, his fellow knights, and the Raven solahma infantry who failed to die along the way preparing to continue their pursuit of The Dark One, but their story hasn't been picked up again yet.

Wolf Pelts and Raven Feathers

The most recent Black Marauder story, from Shrapnel Issue #10, looks to the Clan side of the hunt for The Dark One in the spinward periphery. Wolf ristar-turned-exile Taran Sender approaches the Black Marauder from a different direction, not chasing it directly, but rather following fragmentary reports of secret Star League neurological research. Sender has Enhanced Imaging implants and pilots an Interface-Cockpit-equipped, ah, Ryoken III, let's say. Already suffering side effects from his implants, he has become obsessed with the idea that the Star League may have quietly developed a less-invasive mind-machine interface system which Clan scientists could iterate upon to perfect their own interface technology and spare him from the EI-user's inevitable fate. It's a Raven Watch agent (Lucien Clearwater's successor, Lydia Spaatz) who steers him towards a confrontation with The Dark One through implications that it is also piloted via mind-machine interface.

A good chunk of this story follows the formula of those video games where you piece together the aftermath of a disaster through scientists' fragmentary logs. Lydia is able to provide Taran with data retrieved from the wreck of a Star-League-era DropShip associated with the "Project Butterfly" he's chasing, but the corrupted and incomplete nature of that data is used to tease both the characters and the reader with possibilities, inferences, and conjecture rather than hard confirmation of much of anything. Perhaps the most noteworthy of those logs is the last we, the reader, see, a passage which strongly suggests that at least some aspects of the personality of an SLDF Gunslinger test pilot whose death was recorded in an earlier log were introjected into a later test subject. There's an implication there that Black Kevin's personality shift could be due to this technology, and that his own persistence into the ilClan era could be as one of the ghosts in the Black Marauder's machine, but it's all very purposefully left ambiguous.

When the latter half of the story brings Taran Sender into direct conflict with The Dark One, he does the most we've seen anyone do to try to analyze its performance as a mech. We see it both through his sensors (as a blurry void, an absolute darkness) and through his eyes directly (as a Marauder painted pitch-black), and he draws a direct comparison to stealth armor technology. It fires flak ammunition from its autocannon throughout this story, at mechs as well as at Lydia's aerospace fighter, indicating the the dorsal gun it lost back in "Ravager" could have been replaced with an LB-X cannon. Sender also comes close to quantifying its PPCs in game mechanics, with the lines "The Skinwalker's ferro-lamellor skin shrugged off some of the damage, but his schematics showed he had lost a ton and a half of armor [from two PPC blasts]. Taran checked twice to be sure. I see." If you don't know how many pips of armor ferro-lam gives per ton... they're Clan-grade, he's saying the Black Marauder has CERPPCs at the time of this story. Most importantly for this narrative, when taking stock of The Dark One he does also find the smoothness of its movements familiar, perceiving it as also being driven by mind-machine interface rather than conventional piloting.

"Wolf Pelts and Raven Feathers" ends on a direct sequel hook, with Lydia Spaatz planning to get in touch with Brother Perseus. Four years later, that sequel has yet to materialize, but like the Marauder itself, it's bound to turn up when we least expect it!

Overall Thoughts on the Black Marauder

I found the Black Marauder stories more engaging than I expected, but tonally and thematically disjointed. While Scarinci clearly intended to examine his creation from a diversity of perspectives (he says as much in his introduction to the Marauder anthology), the relative mundanity of "Kindred Soul" really feels like it deflates the mystique around The Dark One a little, and the constant drumbeat of sci-fi explanations being provided for nearly every one of the Marauder's seemingly-supernatural feats feels a little over-anxious, like someone in the chain of author / editor / publisher didn't trust fan reaction enough to let any hint of spookiness stand without being immediately challenged within the same story.

"Ravager" is my favorite of the stories overall, but the ilClan installments have been solid, Star-League-super-science mythologizing notwithstanding, and Grange's tale way back in the original "Marauder" story stands out as probably the longest stretch in which The Dark One's feats are allowed to go without a clear alternative explanation being provided. If you've been intrigued by the fandom image of the Black Marauder, the original stories are well worth a read!

Hastati Sentinels Atlas II. Credit: Valk

Devil Take the Hindmost

This Clan-Invasion-era story from Shrapnel #2, written by Michael J. Ciaravella, was named in the introduction to the Marauder anthology as a spiritual successor to the tales of The Dark One, but seems not to have captured the fans' imagination in quite the same way.

"Devil Take the Hindmost" cribs heavily from Phantom of the Opera in imagining a mysterious Atlas-piloting "Phantom of the Coliseum" / "Angel of Battle" as a possessive patron/guardian of the Steiner Coliseum on Solaris. The story itself, with a broke up-and-comer MechWarrior challenging the Phantom to a nighttime duel as a last-ditch effort to avoid becoming Dispossessed, is a fun read, but the exposition introducing the Phantom is clunky. I do commend this story for leaving the Phantom highly ambiguous, but - and I acknowledge this is a nitpicky distinction - he comes across as more mysterious than spooky, a melodramatic patron of the arts like his inspiration, rather than a quasi-supernatural figure.

As a stylistic note, Devil Take the Hindmost is introduced by a note which seems to frame it as a tale compiled by the son of its protagonist for an in-universe investigator, but absolutely nothing about how the story itself is written indicates that it's anything other than a normal third-person-omniscient short story. Kind of a disappointing waste of the framing device.

Onikuma

This story by Charles Gideon is available as a free download from Catalyst's web store. It's a brief but effective tale of a son visiting his father, a disgraced DCMS officer regarded as a madman, to hear firsthand his account of a harrowing encounter with a warped, bestial thing which was once a Kodiak mech. Out of all the stories I'm covering in this article, this makes the best use of a first-person perspective to couch a fantastic narrative in factual ambiguity. There's not quite nothing to back the man's claims of a monster mech stalking the larger-than-life forests of the planet Dumaring, but "Onikuma" doesn't really care about trying to provide any coherent explanation, it just lets the story sit at the juncture of lunacy and possibility, and I really appreciate that. It's free, short, and spooky; I strongly recommend you check it out this Halloween!

Out of the Ashes

Cards on the table, this Dark Age story from author Jaymie Wagner, printed in Shrapnel issue #14, is one of my favorite pieces of BattleTech short fiction, and thinking about it was one of my major inspirations for writing this article. I've made a lot of hay here about the value of ambiguity in letting horror thrive in BattleTech, but "Out of the Ashes" is a nice reminder that some aspects of BattleTech's setting are plenty horrifying enough without the need for any supernatural explanation.

Let me back up. Out of the Ashes starts with the mundane horror of being on the losing side of a war of attrition, as Lt. Una Kaur of the Principes Guards struggles to hold together what's left of Republic resistance to a Capellan invasion of Tall Trees. After a devastating artillery barrage damages her comms and leaves her isolated from any other survivors, though, she finds herself fighting to survive against a foe more implacable and less explicable than any Capellan: a literal zombie Thunderbolt, shambling but enduring, roused from a hidden bunker by the shelling. What she eventually discovers when the Thunderbolt is downed is good for a shudder: the corpse of a Blakist Manei Domini pilot who died wired into the mech, their mind seemingly having been plumbed to train the Thunderbolt's drone operating system.

It's a lot of fun to bandy about tales of monster-mechs and ghost-pilots, but the Word of Blake are a great reminder of that oldest of horror tropes: Humans are the real monsters.

Thunderbolt. Credit: porble

Final Thoughts

I hadn't read most of these stories before I started researching for this article, knowing the Black Marauder only by reputation and "Onikuma" and "Devil Take the Hindmost" only by title. Having gone over the lot of them, I think my main reaction is that there really ought to be more space left for mythology in BattleTech. I wouldn't want ghosts and goblins to be the setting's bread and butter, but soldiers are absolutely no stranger to telling tall tales, and MechWarriors should be no exception. In the same way that music or food can bring an added touch of verisimilitude to a story, an added touch of humanity, so too can mythology.

I appreciate what we have - "Onikuma," "Ravager," and "Out of the Ashes" really are stand-outs among BattleTech short fiction - but I have a challenge to current and future BattleTech writers: Let the Inner Sphere be weird! It's a big enough place for a few more myths and mysteries.

Until next time, MechWarriors... maybe do take a minute to look a gift mech in the mouth. Or at least double-check the wiring on that neurohelmet!
