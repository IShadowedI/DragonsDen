How to Paint Everything: StarCraft The Miniatures Game Zerglings

July 7th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Ah, the Zergling. What a spectacular creature.

The Zergling is perhaps the most iconic unit in all of StarCraft, which puts it on a short list for one of the most recognizable gribbly in all of video game history. Zerglings are the most basic infantry for the Zerg, making up for their squishiness by being fast, cheap, and numerous. So much so, in fact, that Zerglings are spawned in pairs, rather than one at a time like every other unit in StarCraft. While Zerglings can be used in great numbers in the mid or late game to overwhelm opponents, these dangerous little dudes are much better known for creating the Zerg Rush, where Zerg players would spawn a small group of Zerglings as fast as possible and send them at their opponent’s base as fast as possible. If you catch the opposing player off guard, this small group of Zerglings would cause havoc in the enemy base, potentially putting their early game economy way behind or even outright losing them the game.

A Zergling - Credit: Dan "Swiftblade" Richardson

The Zerg Rush is classic. The psychic damage it’s done to StarCraft players has echoes through the consciousness of pop culture history. Google even did a whole little game about it for a while if you typed in Zerg Rush to the search bar, which prompted some poor bozo at The Guardian to write an article about it, burdened with trying to explain what the hell a Zerg Rush even is. Incredible stuff, no notes.

The Zergling, as seen in StarCraft 2. Credit: Blizzard Entertainment

Anytime you see a bunch of fast goobers making a beeline charge at something, you’re bound to hear it called a Zerg Rush. It’s the Kleenex of gaming strategies, and until Leeroy Jenkins came along it was easily the most well-known lingo from a Blizzard game. I think I even had a Zerg Rush t-shirt in high school. My fashion sense may have been truly terrible; I wince to even think about it now, but I was right on the money about Zerglings being very cool.

So, I was delighted to see how Archon brought the Zerling to life with the tabletop miniatures game. The sculpt is based on the model from StarCraft II, which makes sense given the game’s much higher fidelity, but in particular it feels very evocative of a piece of Zergling concept art that might just be my favorite piece of StarCraft art, period. The Zergling in that art is so gribbly, just a horrible monster, and you can’t imagine it has any other purpose in the galaxy than to scamper around and cause death and mayhem in its wake. Which, for a Zergling, pretty much hits the nail on the head. 

Credit: Blizzard Entertainment

When it came time to paint my Zerg, I stuck to something close to this scheme, which also happens to cut fairly close to the Archon studio scheme. Knowing that Zerg are known for quantity over quality, I wanted to create a scheme that both looks great on the table without eating too much time per model. I know I’m going to be painting a whole lot of these goofballs if I want to consider myself a real Zerg aficionado. 

Without further introduction, let's get into the scheme I used to paint my Zerglings, starting with the purple fleshy bits.

Flesh

The flesh is the best place to start here for two reasons. First, it’s the most common color on the model, with the only real competitor being the brown carapace parts. Second, every other detail is on top of the flesh, making the flesh the base color of the model. I can be a bit messier with my painting here without disrupting other details, which is great because I plan to do plenty of drybrushing to save me time, and drybrushing can be messy.

 

The basic Zerg flesh in the studio scheme isn’t quite purple or pink, but more like a dark magenta, so I’ll be using a combination of those two colors to get my desired effect. Since we want that nice purple base on which to paint pinks, I start by painting almost all of the model with Two Thin Coats Amethyst Rayne. A good portion of the details blend into each other here with the flesh anyway, so I’m not too worried about being super careful to stay in between the lines here.

Next, it’s time to start bringing in the pinks. Zerg flesh, particularly with these Zerglings, has lots of little raised details that are very conducive to drybrush highlighting, which saves a whole lot of time compared to traditional highlighting - while still looking great and blending well. To bring in more shades of pink, I use a medium-sized drybrush to apply a pretty heavy drybrush coat of Two Thin Coats Sword Hilt Burgundy, then wash it with Citadel Druchii Violet to darken it up a bit. Once the wash is dry, I give the model one more quick round of Sword Hilt Burgundy before adding a light drybrush highlight of Two Thin Coats Perisher Pink on the most raised parts of the Zergling Flesh. 

Carapace and Claws

With the flesh out of the way, I can get to work on painting all of the other details of the Zergling, particularly the carapace and claws. For the carapace, I want a nice, warm brown with more traditional highlights, and a classic bone color for the claws. For this, I started off by painting the carapace Two Thin Coats Noble Steed Brown and painting the claws and teeth Two Thin Coats Skeleton Horde. Once these parts are all done, I can hit them all with a coat of Agrax Earthshade to bring it all together in one nice fell swoop.

Pre-Agrax Wash Zergling

From here, it’s on to the highlight stage. To highlight the carapace, I pick out the raised details with an edge highlight of Two Thin Coats Dry Rust Brown, followed by a final highlight on the most prominent edges with Two Thin Coats Satyr Brown. If, at any point of this, I accidentally get some paint on the flesh that I don’t want, I can fix it pretty quickly with some Sword Hilt Burgundy. 

For the claws and teeth, I need to go back over almost all of the claw with another round of Skeleton Horde. This does feel like a bit of a chore; it’s no fun to paint over a color you just painted, but I’ve found in my time painting bone colors that applying a dark brown wash over a brighter base color looks better than applying the wash on a darker khaki color before moving on to a brighter bone color. Once that cleanup is all finished, I can move on to applying an edge highlight of Two Thin Coats Vampire Fang to bring it all together.

Final Details

There are two other major details here on the Zergling I haven’t mentioned yet: the little green growths on the model and the eyes. Ideally, these details are best left until after the Agrax Earthshade layer has been applied, since these areas don’t want to make use of that particular shade. For the green bits, I begin with a fairly bright base of Warpstone Green, before adding an even brighter highlight of Moot Green and blending them with a glaze layer of Striking Scorpion Green Contrast. This combo of colors really makes the green here pop, especially against the purple and brown of the rest of the model.

As for the eyes, I can put all the time I’ve spent in the mines of painting Chaos Space Marine armor eyes to use to get a nice yellow with orange glow effect super easily. First, I start with a dark yellow, like Two Thin Coats Dark Sun Yellow. Then I lean on contrast paint again by applying a glaze layer of Magmadroth Flame over the eye. Lastly, I go back over with a fine highlight of Two Thin Coats Yellow Flame to brighten the eye back up, adding some extra thin Magmadroth Flame again to keep the yellow-to-orange flame effect in place.

These Zergling models also come with modeled bases that have some rock features being slowly infested by Zerg creep. I don’t usually love modeled bases like this; by the time I reach the basing step, I’m pretty ready to be done with a model, but the good news here is that all the techniques we used before are still good here and painting the rock is super quick. For the rock, I just use the dead easy method of painting a layer of Two Thin Coats Carcharodon Grey, slapping on a layer of Basilicanum Grey, and then picking the details back up with another Carcharodon Grey drybrush. From there, I can just follow the same methods I used for the flesh to get the same purple-pink effect on the creep, and then add the last little detail of green with the same green method I used for the actual Zergling. 

After some final basing and painting my rims Steel Legion Drab, we have a Zergling ready and eager to serve the will of the Swarm!

This painting scheme I’ve landed on here is my ideal painting workflow for something like Zerg, where I know I have to paint lots of models. I know I’m going to need to paint a whole lot of these dorks, so I want something nice and quick that still looks great, and by utilizing drybrushing, simple highlights, and some easy contrast glazes, we end up with a scheme that is extremely time and energy efficient that lends itself super well to batch painting. These Zerglings do need to get out onto the battlefield quickly, after all. It’s kind of the whole thing that they’re famous for in the first place. 

I hope that you’ve found this Zergling Painting Guide helpful, and I look forward to providing more articles on painting these alien monstrosities for all of you to enjoy here on Tabletop Battles. Until then, have fun reliving the Zerg Rush glory days with your StarCraft TMG Zerglings!
