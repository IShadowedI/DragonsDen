How to Paint Everything: Char's Zaku in Gundam Assemble (Updated October 9, 2026)

October 9th, 2026 via Goonhammer
In our How to Paint Everything Series we cover how to paint well, everything, with a look at different methods and techniques from different painters and with an eye for different skill levels. In this article, we’re covering how to paint a Zaku II from Mobile Suit Gundam, in Char Aznable's custom colors.

Changelog

  • Update (Latest): 2026-10-09 Updated to include Rockfish's process.
  • Published: 2025-08-14

Greg

The pilot known as Char Aznable is one of anime's greatest and most versatile dickheads. Also known as Casval Deikun or Quattro Bajeena (I know), he spends about 15 years betraying people and switching sides, and pilots probably a dozen different mechs. As an ace Zeon pilot and later a freelancer, he has the right (often exercised) to paint his units in the signature red color scheme that gave him his nickname, The Red Comet. We're using the first and arguably most famous of his appearances, because it's the one that has a model: the Zaku II Commander type.

The actual colors in the anime are more of a salmon pink on the limbs and a sort of medium brown-red on the body, but I prefer the colors from Gundam The Origin, which replaces those colors with more of a true red and a crimson, respectively. It doesn't hurt that I don't have good pinks in my collection, either.

First, prime black. Then, block in your base colors. For figuring out what to put where, I referred to the best source I had, the Real Grade Char's Zaku sitting on a shelf over my painting desk. If you don't have that, Google some images of robots or just wing it.

Char's Zaku, basecoated

This took a few coats. The Vallejo Game Color Bloody Red I have - this might be common to all of them or just be a problem with my particular bottle - has god-awful coverage. It took four coats over black primer to get it smooth. The Vallejo Game Color Scarlet Red did better, which is great because the bad paint covers something like 70% of the model and the good paint only goes in a few spots. I put a generous amount of Vallejo Game Color Cold Grey on the black parts, and a single dot of Vallejo Game Color Squid Pink in both the monoeye and the scope on the gun. That was pretty much it for color separation. In all cases, try not to get too much paint in the recesses, but we'll handle that in the next step anyway, so don't sweat it if you do.

Next, we apply an all-over black wash (I actually used Warhammer Black Templar Contrast paint for this) to the black parts, and a targeted pin wash of Warhammer Agrax Earthshade to the reds. This is where you can cover any parts where the red seeped into the panel lines, but do try to be careful when applying the wash. Again, you don't have to worry too much, because we're gonna fix it, but it'll make the next step easier.

Char's Zaku, basecoated and washed

Then a bit of cleanup, layering on the base colors over the washes, to clean that up. I wanted the black to stay black, so I didn't change those areas, but bringing the reds back up does a lot to make the colors pop, since they look a little muddy after just base coat and Agrax. Leave some of the darker color in the corners or recesses, but this is a good opportunity to build contrast and visual interest. Gundams are from a cartoon, and there's nothing wrong with painting them to look overly bright and saturated. These models would probably look great in a cel-shaded paint job, but I don't have the chops to do it.

Char's Zaku, basecoated and washed and cleaned up a bit

You could probably stop here and have a perfectly tabletop-ready model, but a few more steps will be quick and worth it, if you feel comfortable with a bit of highlighting. The shape of these models makes them take highlights well - they just aren't as detailed with greebles as a Warhammer model would be. Owing to the subject matter, these are largely made of big chunky shapes built to take thinned layers as volumetric highlights, and have edges that are crying out for the side of a brush.

Char's Zaku, basecoated and washed and cleaned up a bit and highlighted

So, highlight the Scarlet Red with Bloody Red, and the Bloody Red with Hot Orange (this is more or less how I paint the guns on my Dark Angels, oddly enough), building up the color slowly and carefully. A little edge highlight of Cold Grey on the blacks, and we're done. That's it. Five paints, two washes, and just look at it. Get a load of this guy. He rules.

What I like about this approach is that it can also - and will be on the next one - be used on other Zakus to provide a cohesive but interesting scheme. The triad of a dark, medium, and bright paint, where one part of the model is the dark highlighted with the medium and the other is the medium highlighted by the bright, seemed to work out well and didn't require a lot of paints or thought. If you replace the various reds with blues, that's basically Ramba Ral's custom colors, and if you use greens, that's the mass-production Zaku sorted. Once you get into Black Tri-Stars or Anavel Gato type designs, you're on your own, but this will get you most of the way there.

Rockfish

Char's Zaku II. Credit: Rockfish

I've never painted or done anything at all Gundam-related model-wise before this, and while I'll be the first to admit this is quite the unconventional entrance to that particular niche of the hobby, it has been a fun little thing to play with.

As you can tell from the limited colour palette in the above recipe, things will be moving fast on this scheme. You could probably knock out all the base coats in fifteen minutes or less if you wanted, and not that much more for any of the rest.

We start with a very aggressive zenithal of Tuskgor Fur over Vallejo German Panzer Grey primer through an airbrush, but I will mention that you could do this with a similar selection of rattle can primers or simply base coat the whole model in the main colour. The remaining base coats are uh, variable in that the Corvus Black will take no time to throw down, but the rich red is slightly more awkward. I chose to create it by mixing Corvus Black into Khorne Red until I had a tone I liked. It took surprisingly little of the black to get there, so move slowly.

As usual, I chose to make use of a wash to help define the recesses a bit; for that, I went to a very heavily thinned-down Null Oil and simply slopped it over the whole model. There are places where you can get away without this wash since the main zenithal is so stark, and we are working with dark colours for the rest, but I still like to get the additional free definition in the areas we aren't going to highlight. Normally, I avoid a simple black tone like Nuln, but in this case, since anything Gundam tends to lean towards clean cartoon colour tones, the 'simple' colour tone of this wash works fairly well.

Note that it's pretty important I went with a very heavily thinned wash, as while Nuln Oil out of the bottle would probably still get most of the effect you'd want, it would also leave a lot of staining, which is very hard to fix on a zenithal base coat. You could, of course, go back to Gundam techniques by relying on pin washes, but I tend to avoid those where I can these days.  It is an exceptionally slow technique, and in schemes where the plan is to do soft area highlights anyway, then the most visible pooling will be covered up by happenstance.

Speaking of highlights, we can smash those out fairly quickly on a model this small that also lacks significant convolution. For those, we come back to our base coat paint colours and add in very tiny specks of Screaming Skull incrementally to create our various highlight coats. Since this is a pretty light colour we're talking about, use as little as you can possibly fit on the brush while mixing it in, and even then, you might find it to be too much, requiring a dab of the original base colour to tone it down.

I did around 3-4 coats of successively brighter highlights in the areas I wanted to work on. It's worth mentioning that on a small model like this, it's pretty important to control both the paint's consistency and the direction from which you apply it. While I don't thin it as much as a glaze, only to the point before the paint 'breaks' and flows more like paste than water, this means we have to treat it pretty similarly to how one might when working on a glaze. This means that we pull the paint towards the brightest point during a brush stroke, so place the brush at the darkest end, where it will start from and move towards the brightest. The reason for this is to control where the bead gets left behind when you lift the brush. By which I mean, with thin paint and all paints in general, but it's less noticeable with viscous ones, the point where you lift the brush away from the model will have just a bit more paint left there, which can significantly affect the look of your transitions. This can be avoided by being mindful about leaving this extra blob somewhere it can get covered or is otherwise invisible, for example, dropping it off at the brightest point means that it shouldn't be noticeable if a section is slightly more opaque or brighter.

I generally lean towards these thin-layered highlights over glazes for the sake of speed. If I can get a good enough blend in 3-4 coats rather than 10+, I'll save that time whenever I have the choice on such a tiny model. Quick tip about transitions, glazed or otherwise: a varnish coat can save even a surprisingly rough blend, since a lot of the most noticeable differences are caused by the variance in finish caused by the paint being thinned so significantly!

Nearly there! Just the lenses and base now, starting with the first, I did a very simple gem effect with just Caliban Green and Screaming Skull. Since these are small gems that aren't particularly the focus of the design, besides the mono-eye aspect, I suppose I found something simple more than good enough.

The base is actually my normal BattleTech scheme! Start with a thin layer of Vallejo Grey Sand, paint it Steel Legion Drab, and then wash it in Agrax Earthshade. That's it to the tone, I really don't feel like it needs a drybrush or really any extra work on something intended to be small scale, besides some tufts and bushes after a matte varnish coat, of course. For the plant life, I made use of a combination of Woodland Scenics Bushes, torn up a bit for the larger shrubs and then Gamers Grass Tiny Tufts in a Dry and Light Green colour tone for the grasses around the shrubs. (Remember to cluster plants! It looks far more natural to have bunched up growth rather than to randomly scatter little bits.)
