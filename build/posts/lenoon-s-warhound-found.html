Lenoon's Warhound: Found

June 21st, 2026 via Goonhammer

I’m sat on the train to Barking Riverside. Everyone - and I really mean everyone - thinks I’m somewhere else, the professional equivalent of sneaking out to hang with friends. I’ve snuck out to most decidedly not hang with friends, to see noone, to visit the most desolate and empty place I can reach and still be back in time for the end of my lunch break. It’s a kind of new town, a place to be created spontaneously by the pressures of London housing and by the fact it’s the last scrap of truly derelict land before Dagenham. One day it’ll have people living in it - and it does, already those brave pioneers who live in the first-built towers - but for now it’s a swish modern train station and, at least at noon on a grey Tuesday, nothing else. You can take a left out of the station here and walk on London roads and see no cars, no bikes, no humans, just the promise of what we will build and the empty shells of towers working towards the burst of the bubble. It’s very easy to forget that a street over, there’s a school that’s doing well, a co-op, a Tesco, all that stuff, and, of course, the people who live in them.

What do you see when you look out the window?

But for now I will walk in the last of London’s salt-marsh wilderness for twenty minutes before heading home and logging back into the world. It’s easy to get onto the Thames foreshore here. It’s perhaps not safe, or sensible, but I am all too frequently both, so I always take the time to walk between land and tide when I’m here. The Thames is a mighty, old, filthy, river, filled to the brim with the refuse of more than 10,000 years. There is so much to find here that we have a word for the people brave - and daft - enough to fish through the flotsam: Mudlarks. 

Mudlarks of London By Unknown author - The Headington Magazine, 1871, Public Domain, https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?curid=26533492

It’s a long and storied tradition, first of those who scraped the shores to survive, a dysenteric version of the rag-picker and bone-man, kids starving to death and surviving on scraps empirecapitalism gave to the river, the now of hobbyists, collectors and archaeologists who sift through both literal and metaphorical shit to find incredible wonders lost, dumped, gifted and sacrificed. The shift is stark. One group finds the waste of the other, but they put it in museums, in private galleries, in personal collections. I’m supposed to meet one of the modern variety to talk about the power of found objects, two in fact, one on both sides of the surprisingly tricky legal boundary between picking stuff up off the floor and semi-professional mudlarking, but times are different now and schedules are impossible. I increasingly live in bursts, like this one, Aaron abroad, mind unfettered, but that isn’t making interviews any easier. So I walk up and down for perhaps ten minutes, and my eye doesn’t have time to adjust. There are clay pipe stems and condoms by the dozen, little piles of cardboard that were once boxes, a billion fragments of brick, old flints that I’d check for signs of making but the water is thick, brown and glutinous so I’m not touching a thing with bare hands.

Screen Readers are a Great Invention

On the way back to the train, back to a different life and a different person, I pick up feathers and leaves, as if the road was cleaner. Acorns and galls follow - god alone knows how this oak has survived here. On the train there’s elastic bands and a strangely rounded piece of bottle glass. It all goes in my pockets, and to make space out comes my notebook. Even that is crammed to breaking with the found - quotes, overheard snippets, painstakingly copied poems and paragraphs. When I’m alone I try to open myself to the world, to hear/feel/see/touch it all, and take it with me. I love the finding. More than any project I’ve done before, the warhound is drenched in found things, but why tell you? I'd prefer to show.

Upon the Solstice Day

Lenoon's Warhound Titan

When I map all these places, all these finds, with distance eroded, it forms a map of my life over the last year and a half, a cluster of roots and outflung branches to north, west and (slightly) east. It is not a broad map. I live in routine and place (my place, in many, many ways). The map, of course, ends up on the Warhound, oriented to the sun above (or the moral law within, I can't remember my Kant), hidden where only I can see it. I have worked, near every day, on the warhound for five months and nine days. My knights have all, nearly, come to a finish on the Winter solstice in the last few years, a not always healthy point to aim for. But here we are on the longest day, on the blessed Solstice, when we are bathed in light to steel us for the beginning of the long, long dark. This is a high point for me, in my life, hobby, personhood. It has been more than a healing process - a restorative one, the greatest actual, non-child, gift I have received. We're nearly there up on whichever one of the thousand plateaus we inhabit, and it's nearly done - une autre fois, once more into the breach, one final bit of The Warhound to come.
