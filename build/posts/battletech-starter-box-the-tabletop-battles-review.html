BattleTech Starter Box: The Tabletop Battles Review

June 29th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Battletech is a game of big stompy mechs battling it out with big lasers, big guns, and lots of missiles. It’s the most popular tabletop wargame not made by Games Workshop, and has been going strong for over 40 years. This summer brings a moderate rules update for the first time in two decades, and some new box sets along with it.

The aptly named Starter Box is Battletech’s most introductory level product. It’s designed for people who haven’t really played a tabletop game before, aren’t sure if they’ll like it, but saw cool stompy mechs and are interested. As such, it’s a low buy-in product where you can go from getting the box to playing the game in only a few minutes, learning the rules as you go. It also does happen to have two very cool mechs, so all the veteran players will be interested, and if that’s you then you can skip straight down to the Miniatures section – I don’t expect you’ll be playing much with the rest of the box.

If you’re totally new to Battletech or tabletop gaming, this is the box for you. If you’re a little more experienced and have played Warhammer, Dungeons and Dragons, or another tabletop or role-playing game before, you’ll want to look at the Core Box, which we’ll review tomorrow. It’s a great introduction to the full rules of the game and sets you up to continue expanding your forces later. That said, you wouldn’t regret buying this box, even if you’re an experienced gamer.

We'd like to thank Catalyst Game Labs for sending us a free copy of the Battletech Starter Box for this review.

What’s in the box

Inside the Battletech Starter Box

Opening up the box the first thing you’ll see two minis and a bunch of record sheets. Under those are a pair of dice, some MechWarrior cards (set these to the side, you won’t need them for a bit), a two-sided 17” x 22” mapsheet covered with terrain and a hex grid used for movement, a punchboard sheet with some cardboard standees and various tokens, and two booklets: the Quick-Start Rules and What Will You Become, which is a mini-primer on the universe. If you have them, I strongly recommend grabbing some additional dice in paired colors. It’ll simplify rolling weapon attacks as you can do multiple identical attacks at the same time. I’d also look for a single white, black, and red die (12mm size is great here) for each mech, which you’ll use to track movement. Using these movement dice is described on the second page of the quick start rules, and I think it’s a big miss to not include them in the box.

The Quick-Start Rules are fundamentally the Battletech rules. They strip out some of the more complex aspects, and the record sheets used are only half the size of the full game, but there’s nothing you do in these rules that you don’t do later. This makes it a terrific starter product, as you’re not wasting time learning something you won’t use later, and the feel of the game is well maintained. If you like the Starter Box, you’ll probably like the Core Box and the full rules. These rules get cut down from the full rules purely by removing different things – there are no elevation changes here, which simplifies movement and shooting, there’s no internal structure or critical hits, and there are no physical attacks. That cuts out a ton of complexity and leaves us with the most basic Battletech rules loop: move, shoot, take damage. Using these rules teaches us how to move on the hex grid, how to use the GATOR system to determine what you need to roll to hit, and then once you get hit how to mark off armor damage and destroy different components of your mech.

Kontio Record sheet for the first mission

The half-sized record sheets are the other half of the simplification for the rules. On the left side of the sheet is the mech information – how fast it moves, what weapons it has, and a reference for what dice roll corresponds to what hit location. On the right side is something called the “paper doll,” which has pips to be filled in as each location takes damage. Two of the record sheets are marked out in the upper corner as FIRST MISSION, which will get used the first time you roll dice. On the back of those sheets is what’s called a TRO or Technical Readout, it’s a little bit of fluff information about the history of the mech, what it can do, where it’s built, and the components it’s built from. None of that is relevant to playing the game, but it’s something a lot of Battletech players love to read about and helps ground the mechs in the universe.

Back side of the Kontio record sheet

The remaining record sheets, not marked out for the first mission, have some additional rules on the back that change how the mech works. In the first mission, the Hammerhead is a simple mech – takes one point of damage, marks off one point of armor. In the second mission, you get a Hardened Armor additional rule, where it now takes two points of damage to destroy a single point of armor. These record sheets are laminated and reusable. Catalyst says you can use dry erase markers, though I struggled a bit to erase those (maybe the eraser I was using was bad). I used a wet erase pen, which worked perfectly and wiped right off with a damp paper towel.

Games (also called missions) out of this box take 20-30 minutes to complete (I think, as an experienced player I’m a little faster at play than someone new – I still think 30 minutes is a safe estimate of time), so I really liked folding in the extra rules after the first mission. You’ve got the basics down, and now can add a few more things. The back of the record sheet also shows off the full sheet and has a call-out for how moving up to the full sheets will expand what the mech can do, providing a nice hook into future play.

The included mapsheet is Muddy Grasslands on one side, and Dessicated Badlands on the other. It’s a fine mapsheet for the starter box, with both sides having a mix of open ground and scattered woods of both the heavy and light variety. There’s no elevation on the map, which simplifies play. One thing worth noting is that these are the same layouts as Desert #1 and Grasslands #1 mapsheets that were included in the old Beginner Box – if you already have those maps from previous purchases, you’re not getting anything new here other than new art.

Miniatures

Kontio and Hammerhead from the Battletech Starter Box. Credit: Jack Hunter

There are two mechs here, the Hammerhead (squat, wide, and beefy) and the Kontio (aggressive with shoulder guns and claws). Battletech minis are cast from PVC, unlike many other tabletop games, and you get them pre-assembled. Detail on them is softer than something you’d find on a mini from Games Workshop, but it’s still pretty good – and these are some of the best minis that Battletech has put out yet. While mold lines are inevitable, neither mech has much to worry about. They exist, but they’re fairly small and well hidden. I didn’t see any details getting obliterated by a mold line running through them on these. The only thing to note is that the panel lines on the head of the Kontio pass straight through where some lenses are depicted in the line art. This is a little disappointing, as it makes the head appear tall, but I think it’s just a limitation in the scale of the detail that can be cast out of PVC.

Unpainted Kontio

If you do want to clean up the mold lines, do it carefully with a sharp knife. PVC doesn’t quite scrape as smoothly as you might be used to from other minis, so running the back of your knife across them won’t get rid of them as effectively. Before painting I recommend washing the mechs with soapy water, as some of my more recent Battletech minis have had a bit of a mold release on them that prevented clean primer adhesion.

Unpainted Hammerhead

I didn’t paint either of these mechs to match a specific faction (I actually used local sports team uniforms) as I wanted them to be separate from the rest of my collection and stand out nicely from each other on the table, while still having matching basing. Click on the two links below to open up painting tutorials for both of them.

The Quick-Start Rules

In these quick-start rules, a game of Battletech consists of three phases – initiative, movement, and ranged attacks. Initiative, the first phase, is also the easiest – both players are just rolling 2d6 (in Battletech very nearly everything is done with 2d6), with the higher roll being the winner. Winning initiative means you get to go last, which gives you the advantage of reacting to your opponent’s move and hopefully positioning somewhere that you can’t be hit, but can still hit them.

The second phase, movement, is where most tactical decisions happen. Every mech has two movement modes; walking and running, with some also being able to jump. In the movement phase, players select one mech at a time (alternating back and forth), pick a movement mode, and then move up to as many hexes as they have movement points (MP) for that type of move. For a walk or run, turning or moving into woods will cost extra MP, but when jumping you simply move in a straight line from point to point, ignoring any terrain. The distance moved, and any woods that you end your move in will come into play when we get to shooting – the further you move, the harder you are to hit (but also moving at a run instead of a walk makes you less accurate). If you have a set of movement dice, it’s helpful to put one down next to the mech once it finishes moving – there’s a table on page 7 under Target Movement Modifier that indicates what the number shown on the die should be. This makes it easy to reference later, and saves you from having to write down or remember how far the mech moved.

The Kontio moves behind the Hammerhead, out of its firing arcs.

For the first few games, with only one mech on each side, this is very much a game of cat-and-mouse. Whoever loses initiative (moves first) is going to try to find a position where they move as far as possible and position themselves so their opponent has to end up in front of them. Whoever moves second is trying to get behind their opponent, generally at a walk if possible. As you add additional mechs, movement becomes a little more involved. You alternate back and forth, so winning initiative doesn’t matter nearly as much, as both players have moves they can react to. A good tactic when losing initiative is to guess where you’re opponent is going to want to move, then make a move that blocks that – either by ending up in their destination hex, or getting on the shortest path – you can’t move through enemy units.

Once all the mechs have moved, the game moves on to the ranged attack phase. Again like in movement this goes back and forth between players, and it’s divided into three steps: declaration, weapons fire, and damage. Experienced players will sometimes skip an explicit declaration step, but I think it’s still an important part of the game to be thinking about, as there are times when it’ll matter. In this step, the player who lost initiative goes first, picking one of their mechs, checking what it has line of sight to, and declaring which of its weapons are firing, and what it’s firing at. With only woods on the map, determining line of sight is fairly easy, and there’s a helpful diagram/exercise on page 5 of the rulebook to show when mechs do and don’t have LOS. In these rules, mechs can only fire at things in their front three hex sides, which is where maneuvering to get out of that firing arc comes into play.

After all mechs have declared targets for their weapons, you move on to actually resolve the weapon fire. Each weapon is fired one at a time, though if you have multiple distinct pairs of dice you can roll for identical weapons at the same time. Battletech uses something called the GATOR system to determine what number you need to roll – Gunnery, Attacker, Target, Other, Range. Gunnery is just the gunnery skill printed on the record sheet, usually 3 or 4. Attacker is based on what movement mode you used – walking is +1, running +2, and jumping +3. Target is based on how far the target moved – it’s the number shown on the movement die if you’re using one or otherwise referenced in the modifier table on page 7. Other has any modifiers from terrain, heat (not part of this quick start rule set), or weapon hit modifiers (you’ll see pulse modifiers on some weapons after the first game). Finally, Range is based on each individual weapon you’re firing, where each weapon has a short, medium, and long range band – it’s progressively more difficult to hit at the longer ranges. You’ll reference the range on your record sheet, then count the distance between mechs. Once you sum up each of the modifiers you get a Target Number, which you’ll then need to roll higher than. Each weapon could have a different number due to different ranges or other modifiers, but the first half of the calculation – your gunnery, attacker and target movement modifiers, and any terrain will always be the same.

A Hammerhead looks at an approaching Kontio

Once you’ve determined if a weapon hits, you determine where the weapon hits (or, for the missile weapons, how many missiles hit). Missiles (and some other weapons not used in this box) roll on a cluster hits table to determine how many missiles hit. Those get split up into damage groupings depending on the type of missile, and then each grouping can hit a different location – other weapons do all their damage in a single chunk. Each weapon separately rolls on the hit location table to determine which body part takes the damage, and then a number of pips equal to the weapon damage gets marked off on the record sheet. Once all the pips in a location get marked off, that location is destroyed, any weapons in that location stop functioning (and if it’s a torso that’s destroyed, the arm is gone too), and further damage to that location moves to the next location inward, following the arrows on the record sheet.

One very important thing to remember while applying damage is that none of it takes effect until the end of the phase. Every mech will get to fire all the weapons it declared in the first step, even if another mech shot and destroyed that weapon earlier in the phase. In the quick start rules, once the center torso or head of a mech is destroyed, the mech dies. In the full rules, there are a variety of situations in which destruction of the side torsos or critical damage can cause a mech to die earlier than that, but that’s skipped past in this box.

Your First Games

The first game you’ll play is Hammerhead vs Kontio, using the marked FIRST MISSION record sheets. These have no special rules or anything, and it’s going to be a pretty quick game. Your forces set up on opposite sides of the map, move in, and try to kill each other. This first mission really is purely about learning the game mechanics – without the special rules I found that the Kontio had a big advantage as it moves faster, does more damage, and is more durable, if slightly less accurate. That doesn’t really matter though, because this first mission is just to figure out how movement and shooting works.

A Kontio punches the Hammerhead in the back.

The second mission is a rehash of the first, but adds in special rules on the back of the record sheets. The Kontio picks up the ability to punch with its claws, and the hardened armor on the Hammerhead doubles its armor, effectively making it much more durable than the Kontio. I think there’s still enough speed and damage difference here that the Kontio is going to take it more often than not, but a string of luck with initiative rolls can easily tip it to the Hammerhead.

Moving past those first two missions adds in the punchboard standees for some additional mechs, and the option of a more complicated mission where you try to control some objectives. This is where we start getting into real Battletech rather than the first two missions of learning game mechanics, and you can really feel what the full game is going to be like. While I don’t know that we’ve really got perfect game balance at this point, as an introductory product I think it’s good enough. All of the mechs here are interesting to use and feel different, and while the Kontio moves fast enough it can produce a bit of a miss-fest with high target numbers, the claws and short range weapons counter that by encouraging it to stay close.

Final Verdict

Should you buy this? Yes. For new players curious about Battletech this is a great low cost way to take a few spins through the game mechanics and learn whether you like the feel of moving mechs around on a hex map and tracking individual weapon fire and damage locations. The half-size record sheets are perfect for providing enough of the core Battletech gameplay loop to get you hooked, without being as initially overwhelming to see as running face first into full sheets (which certainly are intimidating, but become second nature very quickly). If you like it and expand past the starter box, both included mechs are fun to use and things you can regularly expect to drop into forces you build.
