Historicals: SAGA Listbuilding Essentials

November 12th, 2026 via Goonhammer

We’ve finally convinced you to start playing SAGA, right? Now, instead of 2,500 points of army and dozens of units, you’re looking at 60+ different armies, usually four unit types and a grand total of six points to spend. Sounds simple, but once you dive in there’s a complex but masterable system at play. Let’s have a look at some SAGA list building essentials ranging from the very first steps all the way through to some deployment shenanigans.

Points and Army Size

Saga, like a huge number of other games, is a point buy system. You get between 4 and 8 points to spend, and allocate that spend to different units. All normal so far! A point always buys you a set number of models depending on the unit type. That means that usually a Saga army will be between 4-6 units in size. The most straightforward unit types are also straightforward with their sizes:

  • Hearthguard: Four models
  • Warriors: Eight models
  • Levy: 12 models

So 1 point can buy you four Hearthguard or eight Warriors or 12 Levy. This doesn't change with equipment. Eight Warriors with bows and on horses cost a point, as do eight warriors on foot with no additional equipment. Equipment choices are reflected in unit stats, not points cost.

One point of Warriors - eight models. Saga warbands clash for an unspecified reason

Your Warlord - again, regardless of equipment - is always free. This puts a lower and upper bound on the size of a Saga army.

Smallest Possible*

Warlord

Six points of Hearthguard (24 models)

Largest Possible

Warlord

Six points of Levy (72 models)

The first essential tip in this article is that both of these forces would be a bad choice in the vast majority of armies! Very few SAGA factions are set up to take advantage of all-hearthguard or all-levy.

*Smallest possible with the three base unit types - we’ll get onto the others eventually!

Four, Six, or Eight Points?

A four point game is a good starting point to get your head around the battleboards and start your army. It’s the lowest points level where the game functions as designed, and a four point play experience is still very satisfying. Starter 4 point lists tend to work the same way - one unit of each core type, with a point left over to tailor your list to your faction. This points level is quick, enjoyable and can almost certainly be made from virtually any historicals box you can buy provided it has - at minimum - 24 models in it.

A six point saga army. Credit: Mugginns.

The standard SAGA game size is six points, which allows for a nicely chunky board presence and lets you take a variety of units. Six point armies tend to be variable - enough points to buy the standards (see below), while tailoring your army to your faction board and play style. Playing at this points level is SAGA as designed, the ideal balance between complexity and playability, so always aim for six points!

Eight points is - mostly - reserved for Age of Magic, SAGA’s fantasy expansion. Age of Magic has a much wider variety of unit types and adds a magic system, so larger battles allow you to explore much more of what it has to offer. Eight points is also a good place to end up for other ages - not that you’re taking eight points of units, but that you’re painting and probably transporting eight points to events, giving you some flexibility for your list building by game. We’ll talk more about that later on!

Choosing Equipment

There are some basic suggestions that are worth following as you start out that will shape your armies. Equipment is reflected in Saga unit stats, so very briefly we can say:

  • Mounted: Faster but extra vulnerable to shooting.
  • Ranged weapons: Can shoot but are extra vulnerable to melee.
  • Heavy weapons: Hit harder in combat but are hit harder in return.

Generally, armour levels tend to vary by unit type. The basic rule of thumb is that Hearthguard are armour 5, warriors are 4, and levy are 3.

With these two points in mind, a basic starter saga army shouldn’t go too heavily into any one area. A shooting heavy army is extra vulnerable to melee, a mounted one to ranged attacks, a mounted AND ranged one is vulnerable to both.

Nearly all mounted - not always the best choice. My saracens in action.

Good advice to start out with would be:

  • Always take minimum one ranged unit if you can.
  • Dont load up on (3+ units) ranged unless your board has a lot of support for it.
  • Minimum one mounted unit if you can.
  • Always bring some infantry.

This gives you some shooting, some speed and the resilience of infantry units, and flexing up to two or three units of any one type allows you to specialise without putting all your eggs in one basket.

Once you know your army board, go crazy - my Saracens are all mounted with bows, and get shot to hell constantly.

Swapping Out

The choices aren’t over once you’ve spent your points. Unit sizes and equipment in saga are set on deployment, not on list writing, which gives you a huge range of flexibility between deciding what your point spend is and what hits the table. We’ll go into both unit sizes and equipment in more detail, but the important thing to remember is that you aren’t committed until you know the army your opponent is using and, depending on scenario, it’s possible that you can see some or all of their deployment before you decide!

That means you should know what good options are before you put a unit down. Two points of warriors can be deployed in multiple ways (see below), and units can be flexibly mounted or armed depending on your scenario and opponent.

Unit Sizes

There’s a key difference between SAGA and many other point buy systems, where your points aren’t buying you units but a certain number of models. You then split those models up into units of the same type. Two points of Warriors buys you 16 - which can be two units of eight, or, for example one unit of 10 and one of six.

The vast majority of SAGA units have to be between four and 12 models, so there are simple limits on your ability to chop and change your unit sizes. You can’t split a point of Hearthguard, for example, into two units of two. Likewise, you can’t combine every one of your 72 potential levy into one massive unit. Some larger models - elephants, cannons etc - can be deployed in units of one model.

Both of these units are a single SAGA point - Elephant and 12 levy. Credit: lenoon

Why bother? The answer is to balance SAGA Dice with unit effectiveness. Units generate dice for you to activate your faction abilities based on their size. Larger units shoot and fight better than smaller ones, though smaller ones may be easier to take through gaps in terrain. A unit of ten warriors hits harder - and survives longer - than a unit of 8. Let’s take two points of Warriors as an example, with three ways of allocating them. Each has advantages and disadvantages:

  • The larger the unit, the better it is in combat/shooting.
  • Larger units can absorb more casualties.
  • Smaller units are easier to move through terrain.
  • Multiple small units can make use of certain battle board abilities.
  • Units grant a SAGA dice at above minimum sizes.

So 16 warriors can be deployed as 2x8 warriors, 10 warriors and six warriors, 12 and four, or any variations between.

Why a Unit of 10?

A unit of 10 fights just a bit better than a unit of eight, and is a good place to put a war banner. Banners reduce your aggression (fighting dice rolled) to 0 for a single model, so a slightly larger unit allows you to have a banner while still chucking out a good amount of dice.

Why a Unit of Four?

Those four-model warrior units can still - in a pinch fight and shoot, but crucially they still generate a saga dice. Hearthguard always do, but warriors and levy only generate them above half strength (four and six models respectively). While you can only have a maximum of eight dice, you can absolutely sacrifice four warriors to a passive game in some terrain to get up to the cap.

A four model Hearthguard unit. Ok in combat, rubbish at staying at the back of the board generating a SAGA dice. get them fighting! Guy back left is clearly compensating for something with an extra large spear

Four model units are very vulnerable, but sat at the back, tucked into terrain and left all game they provide an extra saga dice while your other units do the fighting. This is a real mainstay of event SAGA play, though look out for the lesser seen variant of six ranged levy!

A good rule of thumb is that the more ranged or mounted units your enemies have, the larger your units should be. Multiple units of four warriors, while generating a lot of saga dice, are horribly vulnerable to shooting - a single casualty and they stop giving you that precious dice - and will fold almost immediately in combat. If you’re sitting down to play against a long ranged board (like the English in Age of Chivalry), multiple small units will get picked off very quickly. Boards where mobility is low and ranged troops are limited or absent (Vikings in Age of Invasions) will give you plenty of opportunity to sit a 4 man unit right at the back, untroubled all game.

Swapping Equipment

Swapping unit sizes as you deploy is big brained, but galaxy brain play is to swap out your equipment. Any saga unit that can be taken with options can change those options on deployment.

Most commonly this happens with warriors where several options crop up in many armies:

  • Mounted/on foot.
  • Ranged options - javelins, crossbows, slings, bows.
  • Special kit - pikes, sarissas, heavy weapons.

This also interacts with your unit sizes, so you can split two points of warriors into 12 mounted and four on foot if that’s an option for you. Again, you want to base this on your board, your opponent and your scenario. Let’s use my Saracens and two points of warriors as an example.

Saracens can take Mounted, foot, and mounted with bow warriors.

The board supports mounted and bow wielding warriors, so my base position is that I’ll take both.

One point of warriors on foot. Credit: Lenoon

I’m playing against Vikings - no real ranged threat and all on foot. Warriors go mounted with bows - I can shoot and scoot with almost total impunity without worrying about my warriors being armour 3 against shooting. I want some long-ranged charge threats too, so I’ll split a point into mounted without bows for a combat edge.

One point of warriors mounted with composite bows. Credit: Lenoon

I’m playing a scenario that requires me to enter terrain - four warriors go on foot so they don't pick up additional fatigue when in terrain.

The split - one point of warriors, half on foot and half mounted. Credit: Lenoon

My two points of warriors split into:

  • Eight mounted with composite bows
  • Four mounted
  • Four on foot

This gives me split that suits my board, plays well into my opponent’s army and can play the scenario too. This is where painting more than 6 points comes in handy - I have enough cavalry and composite bow warriors to switch the 4 points I usually spend on warriors between the three options should it be necessary.

Half Points

The point system is quick and easy to get used to, but can be a bit of a blunt instrument. What happens if you only want 12 warriors, and you don’t want to buy two points and split them into unequal units? Half points!

Half points are straightforward but occasionally worded in a confusing manner. You’ll see wording like “can be exchanged for two hearthguard, four warriors or six levy,” which essentially means “costs ½ a point”. You see this wording most frequently on larger unusual units like chariots or manubalistas, and the principle has been extended to all units.

Here's a half point of hearthguard providing a 6 model mounted threat. A Saga Game in full swing. Credit: Lenoon

Half points let you bulk up units without over investing in a particular unit type.

Hearthguard are the most common place people take half units - four hearthguard don’t always do that much, 8 hearthguard represent too many points in too few models, but six hits the sweet spot.

Consider half points for Warrior units where they have access to special equipment - composite bows, crossbows, chariots or sarissas - where you may not want to put all your eggs in one equipment basket

Levy half points can be used to add a small ranged unit or saga battery to stick in terrain, but both options are very vulnerable!

Top Tips and Tricks

Thracian Warband (Credit: paintbrush.mongrel)

That’s a lot, isn't it? Let’s boil it down to our top list building tips to start building Saga armies:

  • Always consider what your board is good at before you load up on a particular unit type.
  • Take a range of different unit types.
  • One mounted and one ranged is a good initial place to start.
  • You spend points in list building - you decide equipment and unit sizes at deployment.
  • Think flexibly about your unit sizes.
  • Tailor your equipment to your board, opponent and scenario.

Stick with these for your first couple of games and you won't go too far wrong - once you know your army (or armies, come on, may as well get more than one when your first costs all of about 30 quid), the SAGA world is your oyster. There's a huge variety of armies out there that can all do interesting things with unit sizes, equipment and variety, so get out and experiment!
