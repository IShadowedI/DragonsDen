Tabletop Battles Reviews: Warhammer 40,000 Battlesector in 2026

November 7th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Siltherine’s Warhammer 40,000: Battlesector is still getting DLC five years after its initial release, with an Ultramarines content pack dropping earlier this year which adds some Astartes units and vehicles that weren’t part of the Blood Angels faction that were in the initial release. And, as I’m writing this missive, news just popped up that a new Ultramarines campaign DLC has been announced. Not exactly sure why that wasn’t part of what just came out, but I suppose it is the Emperor’s Will. Which is, apparently, late-stage capitalism.

Battlesector remains a popular title with ongoing support, solid multiplayer activity, and frequent deep discounts but I think the real reason it has enjoyed such a long run is because it is probably the closest a video game has gotten to capturing the gameplay of tabletop 40k. I don’t think it’s completely satisfying as a surrogate for the ol’ 6’x4’; I’m always somehow left wanting more from the game than it is able to deliver and it comes across as more tedious than I’d like, but taken as a tactical video game adapting some elements of the tabletop game it is a success.

I’m not sure I would have had the same assessment back in 2021 when it was strictly a full price, Blood Angels vs. Tyranids affair with just a campaign mode and a skirmish option. It was fine, and the grid-based tactical battles were decent fun but the overall package felt fairly limited in terms of options. Over the years, the additional paid content has helped fill it out a bit as have new gameplay modes but my initial assessment remains the same - it reminds me more of playing 40k-based board games like the all-but-forgotten Heroes of Black Reach than it does playing a proper game of 40k.

But there are definitely echoes worth hearing. There’s army building, diverse units with impactful and flavorful special abilities, traditional tabletop wargame objectives, and detailed rules for elements such as line-of-sight, cover, momentum, and command. And there’s RNG of course, but rather than tossing buckets of D6s at each other all that is mostly handled in the software backend, with lots of numerical buffs and debuffs floating around presumably on some secret spreadsheet somewhere deep in the code. There’s no snaking a tape measure around the Promethium pipes as all of the movement is grid-based. The activation system is all based on action and movement points, and it is strictly and IGOUGO affair apart from overwatch and some off-turn abilities like counter fire and disengagement attacks.

Units are either (fully painted) single characters, squads, or vehicles. Indeed, the game has a host of heavy metal in addition to the meat shields so Militarum artillery sickos such as myself will enjoy wheeling the Basilisk into position or hosing down a host of Boyz with a Chimera-mounted heavy flamer. It’s fun that each unit has an HP stat that you can watch winnow away as attacks are resolved, and the range of special abilities gives you plenty to consider when you are planning a turn.

I find I especially appealing that the game has a strong mix of heavy, intricate war-game mechanics and detail like these abilities but highly accessible playability that doesn’t expose the player to too much min/max math or cognitive load - a quality that all of the best strategy video games share, generally speaking. But that quality is something of a trade-off, because for all of the under-the-hood work that is going on, the game can feel both a little opaque with tons of stats, capabilities effects, modifiers, and abilities to process but also somewhat overly simplified, at least at the surface level.

Compacting everything into an easily digestible video game format, that for the most part abstracts the boring things while keeping the focus of detail on the good stuff, presents an issue that I think is pretty common in turn-based war-games. At its best, it gives you a reasonable facsimile of tabletop wargaming which in fact is and has always been one of the key influences on video game strategy design. On the other hand, without the social layer of meatspace certain elements of the game can begin to feel tedious. I’ve abandoned quite a few games simply because they started to feel kind of boring due to the process of it all. It doesn’t help that the balance is such that it can take several turns to shift stronger units and the objectives tend to favor holding position rather than tactical maneuver. It can start to feel sloggy, and your friends aren’t there to talk about Star Trek or complain about the current rules edition, whatever. Unless you go multiplayer, and then some of the tedium can be relieved.

I’ve only really played hotseat multiplayer with my son and we’ve really enjoyed it in that capacity. I’m sure the online game is full of the usual die-hards, try-hards, sweats, and scrubs. I’ve played mostly in the single player modes and I’m pleased to report that the narrative campaigns are pretty solid, albeit definitely in that “tactical puzzle” zone that many such gameplay formats tend to fall into. The upgrade options and meta-progression give it a little meat on the bone in terms of long-term planning and using the requisition points you'll earn for completing missions. The horde/survival modes I couldn’t care less about and at this point I just roll my eyes anytime these time wasters are included in a gaming package. Crusade as a good, open-ended campaign alternative is a good way to extend play after the two big storyline games. The roguelike Planetary Conquest mode is OK, but shoddy AI and inconsistent quality in the random engagements makes it a bit of drag. Skirmishes tend to be where I spend the most of my time, setting up a couple of armies and crashing them into each other. It’s fun, and if you like list building it’s a great way to do it without suddenly realizing you need to run out and buy another Rogal Dorn.

In fact, the whole game could be seen a cost-saving strategy - the next time you are tempted down at the FLGS, maybe download this game and a couple of DLCs instead of making foolish purchase and save yourself, like, thousands of dollars. It’s not the same as 40k because there are so many squishy things around what that means, but if what you want to do is push Space Marines around and shoot at goofy Space Orks with capricious RNG determining the outcomes, Battlesector has got you well covered.
