Gundam Assemble Official Core Rulebook - The Tabletop Battles Hot Take

August 28th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Lynn: Wait, where am I? There’s a hex map here, and giant robots, but this isn’t BattleTech?

Jake: Alternatively, where am I? There's a hex map, models, and cards but this isn't Underworlds?

Lynn: No, on Thursday Bandai dropped the Official Core Rulebook v1.0 for their hotly anticipated miniature wargaming debut, Gundam Assemble, making it available as a free download from the game’s official site. I’ve been a fan of the Gundam franchise since before I knew what a “MechWarrior” was, and I have most of the launch wave of Gundam Assemble product on preorder with my FLGS, so I’m eager to dig into these rules… and Jake is busy today, so I’ve gotten the chance to do so! Well, he was busy when I wrote that!

Jake: I'll always make time for hexagon-based games! This is an exciting expansion upon the previously published Quick-Start Guide over on the Gundam Assemble website. These rules, alongside the announcement earlier this week that stores will be receiving Learn to Play kits a full month before the general release of the game, mean that we are even closer to getting to play this hotly anticipated game.

While this is our first look at the official rules, there's surprisingly not that much that hasn't been seen before if you've been following the game's progress. Between the Quick-Start Guide and the gameplay demo on the official YouTube channel, a lot of the game's framework has been known for a few months. What this document does do is give us a little more insight and help answer lingering questions. What exactly are the rules for making a team and building a deck? What are the various weapon abilities? And what the heck do Minovsky Particles do? If any of those questions have been keeping you up at night, you should get a sleep study done but you can also find the answers in this document.

Lynn: Now, there’s still a ton of unknowns about this game - most notably, we haven’t seen locked-in final text for any game cards outside those featured in example diagrams in the Core Rulebook - but this is by far the biggest chunk of meat we’ve gotten so far to whet our appetite for speculation. I’m going to read these tea leaves as best I can, but do keep in mind that we’re going off the same publicly-released information you and the rest of the internet are looking at, so our perspective is definitely incomplete.

With that caveat out of the way, let’s take a closer look!

Gundam Assemble Models, Heroic Beginnings starter set. Credit: Greg Chiasson

Presentation

Lynn: The layout of this rulebook is simplistic, with strong “Word document converted to PDF” energy. The title page looks like a placeholder, and art is only present in the form of illustrative diagrams, nothing’s there purely for flavor. The text hasn’t even been organized to ensure clean page breaks; on occasion there are boxed text examples which continue across a page break, lonely headers at the bottom of one page with their section starting on the next page, etcetera.

That said, the illustrative diagrams and examples of play read clearly, and there’s enough of them to cover a lot of scenarios, getting out ahead of some edge case questions and even highlighting a few non-obvious synergies (Duel Gundam and Oggo? Name a more iconic duo!). The writing tries to keep its tone on the “technical writing” end of rules text, with rare intrusions of Bandai marketing lingo, and I’d say it mostly succeeds at that, aside from one or two glaring oversights, which we’ll circle back to later.

Jake: Strongly agree that the layout feels more like a draft version of the rules than a final product, especially when you compare it to the previously mentioned Quick Start Rules. The glossary is also serving double duty in being both a glossary of terms presented earlier in the rules and also a place where a handful of rules are introduced for the first (and only) time. That seems to be an oversight that will hopefully be addressed. I have no idea when that would be, though. Given the delay in having materials printed and boxed up, I'm assuming this is more or less what we'll be getting as a rulebook inside of the boxes that are released in October.

That said, what it lacks in graphical pizzaz, it makes up for with copious examples. In particular, I was pleased to see multiple pages of different examples for determining Line of Sight.

Lynn: On the whole, while there’s no flash here, there is substance, and that’s the part that’s most important for actually getting this game in motion.

Jake: Oh yeah, there's also the glossary at the back. As expected, it serves as a quick reference so you can look up game terms and get definitions. However, this particular glossary also includes a fair number of items that aren't mentioned anywhere else in the rules which feels odd. Mechanics like Challenge, Remote Weapon Systems, and Repair show up here without ever being mentioned earlier in the book. Likewise, a lot of keywords such as Fueled, and Reinforcements get detailed in the glossary without also being mentioned earlier in the book. I think it could really benefit from one more organizational pass.

Lynn: As I'm editing this article, I could totally smooth over the part where Jake mentions that the glossary serves double duty twice, but the irony is too delicious for me not to leave it in. ( ^3^)~<3

Gundam Assemble Miniatures, Wings of Advance starter set. Credit: Greg Chiasson

Revelations

Pre-Game

Lynn: We get some clarified definition of what listbuilding looks like here. Depending on the scenario being played, your squad in a game of Gundam Assemble will typically consist of either three or five units, and your force may include a carrier if (and only if) specified. As previous material has suggested, basically the only limitation on squad creation, outside narrative scenarios, is that you can’t take exact duplicates of any unit which doesn’t have the “Mass-Produced” tag; beyond that you’re welcome to field three different RX-78 Gundams, three different Char Aznables, a tag team of the Wing Zero, Duel Gundam, and an Oggo, or whatever other mad mélange of mobile suits strikes your fancy.

Jake: I genuinely can't wait for Team Chars. Also, going off the section on carriers, players will be able to pick which ones they want to use outside of narrative scenarios that choose for you. I assume this means we'll eventually have a pool of different carriers to pick from and not be limited to White Base vs White Base mirror matches. (Lynn: In fact, already announced releases include a Musai-class for Zeon and the Archangel for the Seed-heads in attendance.)

Lynn: You’ll also build a deck of exactly nine tactics cards (or exactly [number of phases played x 3 + 3], if you want to play past the typical two-phase game length), with your deck-building options constrained by the squad you’ve chosen to take. Every unit has a Signature Tactics card associated with only that specific unit (e.g. only that specific flavor of RX-78, or what have you) which can only be included in your deck if you’re fielding that unit and which can only be used by it, and other cards may be specifically limited by other keywords (with the examples given all being faction tags like “Earth Federation,” “Zeon,” etc.). You can’t include multiple copies of any particular tactics card, and there doesn’t seem to be any carve-out for “mass-produced” tactics like there is for units.

This deck construction mechanic ensures that you can’t see all the cards in your deck without access to additional card draw (something the rulebook does suggest is a mechanic that exists), since you only draw three per Phase of play (so you’ll draw six out of your nine in a standard game). That should be a balancing mechanism, of sorts… even if the Sekiha Love Love Tenkoyken card, or what have you, is absolutely busted good, you can’t guarantee you’ll see it in any given game, and even if you do, you only get to use it once.

Jake: Coming from a game where you built a small deck without duplicates or any guarantee of seeing every card in your deck, this feels oddly familiar. Barring any external card draw, you'll see 66% of your deck in a matchup if you're playing the standard 2 phase game. This is enough to build some reliability into your deck, especially once the card pool builds up a bit.

Looking at the product lineup on Bandai's website makes it seem like we're going to get roughly three tactical cards per model in each product. This works out nicely given that you're going to have three models in your force and a nine card deck. However, the Deluxe sets weirdly only show that they include a single tactical card. I suspect this is a mistake because having 13 models and only one tactical card doesn't make a lot of sense.

Lynn: Look, you can all share that tactics card and you’ll like it!

Terrain

Lynn: While I believe we got water rules somewhere along the way prior to this, the Core Rulebook is our first look at what spaaaace brings to the table. Debris Fields have… the exact same mechanics as water, on the face of it, slowing movement initiated within the terrain and penalizing attacks made against targets inside a Debris Field or by units inside a Debris Field (with the penalties stacking if both the attacker and target are in Debris Fields, even if it’s the same field). Given the penchant of Zeon and its analogues (looking at you, ZAFT) for deploying an entertainingly diverse array of amphibious mobile suits, we’ll probably see individual units which interact with Water differently than they do Debris Fields, but in terms of core rules, they’re the same.

What isn’t the same are areas affected by Minovsky Particles. These don’t affect movement, but impose a (non-stacking) -2 Accuracy penalty when targeting any non-adjacent unit when either the attacker or the target is inside a Minovsky Particle field. From what we’ve seen of the map included in Deluxe Set 01, that can mean a very big chunk of the battlefield applies a heavy nerf to basically all ranged combat in certain scenarios, which… should certainly help in forcing canon-typical knife beam saber fights, I suppose!

Jake: I think it's amusing that Minovsky Particles, a plot device made up to ensure cinematic battles with mobile suits in the anime, wind up being implemented in such a faithful way. You're going to get up close to smash each other and like it.

Gundam Assemble Miniatures, Zeon's Rush starter set. Credit: Greg Chiasson

Carriers

Jake: Carriers were shown in even the earliest of preview images, although they've undergone some visual changes. They've had a few different layouts of the hexes on their base – from a straight line of three hexes to a more Y-shaped five hex base to a four hex diamond. It remains to be seen what they'll look like in the final version of the game. (Lynn: Though I'd say that the wording of these rules implies the "straight line of three hexes" base.) Regardless of what the production version of their bases look like, carriers have a few funky rules and their own section of the rulebook devoted to them.

When moving a carrier, you have to decide if it's moving forward or backward. Whichever direction you choose, that hex of the base moves one space and then the middle hex of the base has to wind up in the hex just vacated by the moving section. Yes, this is wordy and sort of introducing facing in the game when it doesn't come up anywhere else. It feels like it'll be pretty intuitive once in play, though.

Attacking with a carrier has some special rules as well, because all attacks originate from the front hex. You'll need to make sure you're doing sick drifts if you want to swing around an obstacle to get line of sight on targets. It also means the butt of the carrier will block their own line of sight. Sneaking up on the vulnerable section of a carrier feels like it'll be a valid tactic.

In the scenarios that use carriers, they double as the spawning point for your units when they're (re)deployed. They're also units with stats that can be attacked, so what happens if they're destroyed? It turns out they're disabled. This means they can't move, attack, or contest objectives but they still exist on the board as a deployment space.

Lynn: One additional constraint on the movement of carriers is that all the hexes they occupy need to be at the same elevation at the end of movement.

Jake: Oh yeah, that's going to really limit where carriers can be on the maps especially since so far they all seem to have a fair bit of elevation variation.

Push, Pull, and Collision

Jake: Another revelation is the inclusion of mechanics for pushing and pulling models. The implementation is fairly straightforward in that pushes force the model to move away from whatever is pushing it while pulling drags it toward the source. If it's a multi-hex push or pull, then every individual hex has to be further or closer as relevant.

One fun bit is that if you can't be pushed or pulled due to an intervening model or terrain, it causes collision damage. We're out here shoving mobile suits into each other! Any time collision damage occurs, it inflicts two points of damage on everyone involved. So if something is shoved into terrain, the unit suffers two damage. If two models wind up bumping into each other, they both suffer two damage.

Lynn: Also worth noting, some scenario objectives can be picked up by your units, and being Pushed or Pulled (or Dashing, but at least you’re the one who decides whether you do that!) automatically causes you to drop an objective you’re holding! Based on things I've heard from our Crisis Protocol friends, I’d imagine that mobile suits which can force movement will be a very strong inclusion for certain scenarios.

TP-007B Zaku II. Credit: Fowler

Clarification Needed

Jake: As expected for a brand new game, there's a lot going on in this document. It covers the bases pretty well, but we noticed a few places where it would benefit from some clarity. I'm hoping at least one more version of the rules is published with the game's release (version 1.1, perhaps) but failing that we could at least be well served with an FAQ.

One thing that jumped out to both Lynn and myself was that nowhere in the rules document does it mention whether you can shuffle around the parts of your turn. In my own reading, it felt a lot like it was implying you would need to Advance before you could do any of your options for Primary Actions. This goes directly against what has been shown in the Quick-Play Guide where it mentions you can perform an Advance, do a Primary Action of choice, use Command abilities, or play a Tactics card in any order.

Lynn: This is particularly important if you’re planning to Advance and Dash and need to change elevation as part of that movement. Let’s say you’re two hexes away from a two-level-high elevation change, and you need to get up there. If you can Dash, then Advance, you can Dash the two hexes to be adjacent to the cliff and then use the three movement of your Advance to scale the elevation change, but if you need to Advance first and then Dash, you simply can’t cover that same distance.

Another aspect which seems unclear to me is whether you can pay Timeline costs which would carry your token past the end of the second Phase. It’s described that if you go off the end of the Timeline, you loop back around and finish paying the cost by moving additional spaces from the start of the Timeline… in other words, if you spend 4 Timeline when you’re two spaces from the end of the Phase One Timeline, your next action will be two spaces into Phase Two. But can you spend time that’s never going to be played, if you’re near the end of Phase Two (and thus the entire game)? I just don’t know.

Jake: There are also a few places that seem to sport errors of things changing in testing but not being updated in the document. One of these was on page 40 when it's talking about which model can play a Signature Tactics card. The example calls out Epic Shot being usable only by "Hero of Side 7" but the card pictured doesn't have that indicator on it anywhere. Instead, it lists "Legendary Hero." I assume this is something that changed at one point in testing but didn't get corrected in this iteration of the document.

Lynn: Jake and I were both confused for a bit by "Legendary Hero," as well. We initially interpreted it as a "this is unique" marker, the way "Legendary" is used in Magic: the Gathering; it wasn't until I noticed that the Wing Gundam Zero and Buster Gundam cards seen in the rulebook have different descriptors in their black-background keyword tag ("Wing of Destruction" and "Longshot," if you're curious) that I realized "Legendary Hero" was actually the specific descriptor for that iteration of Amuro's RX-78-2!

Speculation

Lynn: The… I’d say yellow flags around game balance are still on display here. Aside from what I assume is the starter box’s RX-78 card, we see unit cards for the Oggo, Wing Zero (Endless Waltz), Guntank, and Buster Gundam. The Buster Gundam is somewhat incomplete, since it comes with interchangeable weapon cards for the Anti-Armor Shotgun or Hyper Impulse Long Range Sniper Rifle and those aren’t seen here, but looking at the other four… the Wing Zero, in particular, has some ridiculous potential (including the innate ability to passively double the range for scoring critical hits?!?), and the Guntank also seems to project serious threat (how does a Range 7, Strength 9 attack which ignores LoS sound?). It’s hard not to feel like relative punching bags like the Oggo will be relegated to narrative play.

Of course, from a lore and vibes perspective, of course Wing Zero is going to stunt on a friggin Oggo; I’m just less sure the whole “any three bodies, no points” approach is going to work for the competitive play we’re theoretically going to see for this game.

Jake: There are certainly plenty of balance levers available to (theoretically) make it work. Between adjusting the victory points for killing units, the timeline costs of their abilities, and the cards available it should (again, theoretically) be possible to balance three headline Gundam suits against three generic Zaku IIs, but I have been playing games long enough to be at least a little wary of how it'll turn out.

Lynn: It is worth remembering that the flashy super attacks I’m worrying over both have a Timeline cost of 4, so you’d see them a maximum of… roughly five times a game, and that’s if the unit in question never dies and never has to do anything with a Timeline cost other than shoot, which seems unlikely.

Jake: Given how important the scenarios seem to be in gameplay, it's hard to form solid opinions without having some of those to work with. There have been some teasers dropped here and there for the first few product releases, but I really want to sit down with the actual scenarios. Going off some earlier reveals, we're going to have the two scenarios from the Starter Box to work with for a few months before more get introduced. I'm particularly interested in the Scenario Pack 01 that is slated to come out in Feburary 2027, especially because it's supposed to be aimed at the more competitive organized play format.

Lynn: My biggest question mark regarding scenario play are the Garrisons. These only have 1 HP, and your opponent scores the same amount of VP for shooting your Garrison from any range that you do by “Rescuing” an adjacent friendly Garrison. At first blush I feel this seems absurdly balanced in favor of killing the enemy over rescuing friendlies, but I might feel differently in play? And certain deployment maps might affect that? We’ll see.

Jake: It turns out war is hell. Blowing up garrisons is easier but just as rewarding as protecting them.

Lynn: AoE effects seem like something that’s going to need to be monitored closely. In particular, given how they’re carried out (one Strength pool is rolled, then the results are separately applied to each target), a buff to Strength which would apply to any target is applied to the entire attack, even though Accuracy modifiers from terrain and the like apply to each target separately, which could lead to some heinous buff interactions if the devs aren’t careful.

I’m spending this section fretting, though, so I should also say that I’m heckin' excited to see what Gundam Assemble has in store for us. I’m already impressed by the game’s roster mixing in deep cuts amongst the obvious stars (I can guarantee you I have never before typed “Oggo” this many times in a day), and there is so much depth this game could mine if it finds its footing for a long production run. I’ve got the majority of Wave 1 on preorder, and I can’t wait to see what squads I can put together and get to smashing my anime war dollies against each other!

Demo game from GAMA. Credit: Fowler

Overall Vibes

Jake: There are definitely a lot of cool mechanics on display here and a solid framework of underlying rules. The timeline continues to impress me as a game system. The way "respawning" works to keep both players involved in the game sounds like it might address some blowouts that happen in similar systems. Being able to pick your custom team out of the whole arsenal of Gundam IPs is great!

That said, I'm with Lynn in that I'm seeing some yellow flags depending on how the game is handled after release. The way cards are being packaged in with various releases immediately brings to mind the early days of Warhammer Underworlds where players who wanted to play a certain set of models were still heavily incentivized to purchase unrelated releases just to add to their toolbox of generic cards. There's also the issue that mass produced units can be fielded multiple times. If someone really wanted to run a full force of a mass produced unit that's only available in one of the $100+ sets, they're going to have to shell out $300-$500 just to do so. That's an awful lot just to run three to five RB-79 Balls.

The initial release cadence is also quite rapid. There are currently 15 different releases from October 2026 to February 2027. On the one hand, this rocks because that's a ton of little robots to collect, build, paint, and play with. On the other, that's an awful lot of stuff to try and keep up with if you want to play this as your "main" game.

I hope the developers are aware of these pitfalls and willing to address them. Particularly because I'm eager to get my hands on the game and play! I feel like it'll be a wonderful addition to a game library, especially if it plays out as quickly as I suspect it will.

Lynn: This looks to me like a relatively casual, beginner-friendly wargame, perhaps even a game to be enjoyed with the fabled “beer and pretzels” (or ramune and pocky, as you prefer)!

There are aspects of the mechanics that excite me… like Jake said, the Timeline is pretty clever, but for me the standout might be the Jumping mechanics, which really feel like they get the vibe of Gundam movement right: If you start from an elevated position and move to a hex at the same elevation, you treat that as perfectly level movement, no rolls to see if the jump succeeds, no extra costs, and you can freely move over enemies that are on lower elevation. If you jump upwards to an even higher elevation, you only pay the difference between your current elevation and the elevation you’re jumping to; you don’t have to climb down and climb back up. In Gundam, a franchise where rocket-boosting around is commonplace enough even the Guntank can do it, this feels only right and proper.

Despite the “gamey” abstractions in the system (some of which, like the respawn mechanic and the “Upgrade” pick-ups, really kind of rub me the wrong thematic way), this does look like a labor of love, even from the limited exposure I’ve had to the game’s cards so far. I think this game looks likely to translate the feel of its mobile suits pretty well, even if the actual scenarios might look a bit more like a computer simulation than a life-and-death struggle to me.

While I could be proven wrong, I don’t have high hopes for the competitive balance of this game, and honestly, that feels kind of freeing for me. Readers who’ve explored my other content will know that I’m a fiend for competition in BattleTech, but it’s comforting to imagine that I won’t end up a cutthroat competitor buying SEED expansions I don’t want to chase meta cards here, and I can just have a bit of fun watching Suletta fight Zechs every so often.

This feels like the place where there’s going to be a smash cut to me-six-months-from-now taking a SEED squad to a tournament or something; we’ll see how things go. ^_^;

I want to say a sincere thank you to my co-author Jake for his continuing coverage of this game, and I hope to see all you Gundam guys, gals, and non-binary pals again in the not-too-distant future! Til’ then, Tobe, Gandamu!
