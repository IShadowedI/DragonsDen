Conquest Meta Snapshot: Chios World Championship Predictions 2026

September 15th, 2026 via Goonhammer

If you’re a Conquest player, your eyes are either already on or will soon be drawn to the World Championships taking place soon in Chios. The qualifiers from across the world have been selected following many extremely strong performances in the last competitive season, with the Championships themselves taking place on the 28th and 29th of September.

As we noted in this article’s predecessor last year, even qualifying for an event like Chios takes a tremendous amount of hard work and skill, and every player present will be one of the best in the world. Winning an event like the world championships takes a tremendous amount of skill, at least a little bit of luck, and an understanding of and plan of attack for the prevailing metagame.

In this article we’ll be looking at that metagame and talk about how each faction is positioned to perform at Chios. Competitive balance in Conquest has improved markedly compared to some previous pre-Chios metagames, with even the strongest factions capable of being unseated and even the weakest factions in with at least a chance. As such, we’ve broken down the factions into three rough tiers: top-tier, middle-tier, and low-tier, rather than try to cleanly rank every faction in order. Factions will be roughly grouped within their tiers, but we need to emphasise that these overall groupings, and even the difference between strongest and weakest factions in the game, is currently pretty damn tight.

Top-Tier

Nords

While the gap between best and worst factions in the game is narrower than it’s been for some time, there are still at least a few factions that stand out as serious contenders, and first of those is Nords. While in terms of absolute mechanical power they don’t exceed either of the other top-tier armies, Nords sit at the top of the list by dint of the breadth of different list options they can bring that pack serious punch. Nords have one of the widest lists of powerful and transformative warlord options in the game, with serious contenders in the form of virtually all their options. The Volva remains as good as ever, and the Koungyr and Jarl both have interesting list options, but the Vargyr Lord and Shaman in particular have both emerged as serious top picks.

Of the two, the Vargyr Lord is a build-around warlord that produces an extremely powerful half-bloods list. Generally combining a high volume of werewargs and goltr, the Wild Beasts supremacy pushes the single most powerful ‘go turn’ in the game, with light werewargs connecting for extreme damage charge + clashes and medium goltr able to fill any gap or respond to any counterattack with march + charge + tramples. The sheer momentum this list sets off the supremacy turn, coupled with its extremely high scoring potential due to the early ground seized by so many lights and mediums, puts it in an extremely strong place into almost any matchup.

The Shaman, by comparison, is a consummate generalist. Virtually no list configuration fails to benefit from a Shaman, he’s an extremely efficient character just by himself, and taking him gives you full access to whatever might be the best fit for a list seeking to respond to other factions the Nord player considers the highest threat. Mountain Jotnar in particular are tremendously efficient medium monsters (when combined with the Shaman’s extremely cheap Jotun Shepard mastery, they’re just about the most cost-effective monster in the game), and Nord half-bloods and infantry all see reasonable place in a Shaman list. Even regiments like Huskarl, historically somewhat tricky to find a use for in the Nord roster, have potential as a high-defence meta pick against things like Sorcerer Kings ranged and spellcasting poke.

Sorcerer Kings

Credit Para Bellum Games

DISCLAIMER: This ranking and the subsequent assessment is based on two rulings previously confirmed by the rules team at Para Bellum but currently being re-litigated. These are that a Marid Sahar unit benefits from its own Aqueous Branding rule, but does not contribute to the maximum limit of three successful casts of the same spell under a Sorcerer Warlord’s Omnipotence ability. If either of these rulings change, move the Sorcerer Kings down, but only to the bottom of Top-Tier. Yes, free offensive spellcasting actions with a static wizard value on mainstay scoring regiments are that valuable.

Immediately after Nords, we have Sorcerer Kings. One of two factions that comprised the entirety of the competition-viable metagame in 2025, the hugely overperforming Fire Court-focused elements of the faction from 2025, and modestly overtuned Air Court monsters of early 2026, have both been brought down to earth, only to be replaced by the feature-complete, flexible and extremely powerful Court of Water. While the other courts (or a mix of them) remain viable in competition play under a Maharaja warlord, it’s the Court of Water that’s our pick for the most competitively dominant Sorcerer Kings army heading into the World Championship.

Core to the power of the Water Court are Marid Sahar, which still seem to be persistently slept on by some parts of the community. The underlying chassis for Marid is solid; decent stats, decent speed, decent offensive potential, and capable of fighting their way out of a scrap in a pinch. What makes Sahar one of the best units in the game is their Free Spellcasting Action, coupled with Aqueous Branding, and the relatively easily triggered automatic additional hits from Blood to Water. Free actions are always the most powerful ability to bolt to a unit in Conquest, and the tremendous flexibility and supplemental damage offered by a free Blood to Water means that Sahar are just outright one of the best units in the game. In practice, properly piloted Sahar perform like a more expensive but also more dangerous, durable, flexible, mainstay City States Chariot that can’t be neutralised by engaging it in close combat. They aren’t even particularly vulnerable to attrition, as a Sahar command stand with a single wound engaging the enemy still puts out a high number of spellcasting hits that cause resolve damage thanks to the static spellcasting value.

While three to five regiments of Sahar are powerful enough to form the backbone of a competition-viable army without too much assistance, the Marid Apsara and Shaytan have added a powerful heavy monster component to a pure water list as well, releasing in time to be legal for the World Championship. The Apsara is flexible and has some interesting force multiplication potential, but the Shaytan just straight kills things, bringing a huge volume of accurate, high cleave, terrifying attacks on a fast platform - effectively answering the one natural counter that high enemy armour poses to the high volume of hits Sahar are capable of meting out. They can be a bit vulnerable to enemy ranged attacks but are frustratingly difficult to bring down in melee, and overall serve as a perfect complement to their smaller brethren.

Put all of these together and you have a pure court list with access to the strongest rituals in faction and no capability gaps. The list constrains enemy movement with rituals (making approaching terrain a potentially lethal experience), exerts extreme medium-range offensive pressure with ranged attacks and spellcasting, can’t be countered by engaging it in melee, concentrates force excellently, has ample scoring units, is pleasingly quick once it’s on the board, and because it’s an easy pure court list to run, unlocks your choice of warlords to personal taste (using a Maharaja if you want to give Mahabarati an extra court bonus, but a Sorcerer or Raj otherwise). It even serviceably buffs both ranged output and defence, and heals very well with some setup (although most units will need ritual healing rather than spellcasting, as there’s little need for more than one or two Mortal units). Just an absolutely top shelf list, albeit without the faction breadth that categorises a faction like Nords.

Yoroni

Comprising the other fifty percent of the competition-viable metagame in 2025, Yoroni saw some developer attention over 2026, mostly to sidegrade their Geisha monsters to be still very good but no longer spammable brawler models with no downsides and no weaknesses. This has caused Yoroni lists to revert to a mix of old and new archetypes, which are still very strong off the back of the fundamental strength of modular regiments, good quality monsters and their highly flexible unique command stack mechanic. In short, it turns out it's quite hard to nerf a faction whose main power is that everyone is extremely dummy thicc.

While they fall at the bottom of the Top Tier factions due to lacking the breadth of list options of Nords or the sheer mechanical completeness of Water Court Sorcerer Kings, Yoroni lists still have a good mix of versatility and power. Modular regiments see play as both powerful brick units (with Mahotsu support, they are capable of putting up a shockingly durable brick that can lumbers into heavy engagement without many things seriously threatening it if it’s large enough), and as their usual fast, flexible, very dangerous, reasonably durable two-stand MSUs - in both cases forming an able backbone to any Yoroni list that can then be led and supplemented to taste by the Yoroni player.

The wildcard for Yoroni this year are the two Oni Kishin heavy cavalry units that have recently been added to the faction. Fast, durable, dangerous heavy regiments with a price tag to match, these are particularly noticeable for being heavy regiments with flank. This combination is extremely powerful and rare as it allows response to the first enemy medium regiment deployments with your own powerful counters without any risk of delaying your own deployment. The only downside these units have is that both the Oni Kishin Raiu and Kiba have a slight mechanical matchup issue into both Nords and Sorcerer Kings, since both can make heavy use of cavalry (Nord Goltr, Sorcerer Kings Marid Sahar, etc) which neither Oni Kishin units matches up into well mechanically (but can overwhelm with raw damage output).

Middle-Tier

Old Dominion

As we step from the Top Tier down into the Middle Tier, it’s worth reinforcing that all the factions here are still very capable, and where you draw that particular line really can vary from player to player. Straddling the boundary between tiers is the Old Dominion, who are sitting alongside Nords in terms of the potential breadth of warlord options and therefore lists they can play with their current roster. Virtually every warlord except the Theokrator is competitively viable (and the Theokrator is extremely viable as a standalone monster).

Personal favourite list archetypes include a Fallen Divinity list supplemented by extremely aggressive mortal light regiments (Cultists with Hierodeacons, and Hassasins - although Moroi and Kheres can perform here too), a generalist mixed-force creed and legion-based Archimandrite Warlord list with lots of dark power generation to fuel his power use, and frankly any of the martial characters to taste with a good mix of legion infantry and cavalry. Of these, we judge a list of legion medium regiments supplemented by a Theokrator as the most likely to see play at Chios, but if ever there was a year to see the Fallen Divinity present, this would be it.

Ultimately, despite their status as a casual meta boogeyman, we generally put Old Dominion at the top of the middle because much of their perceived power comes from new and inexperienced players not understanding how to play against them - but the overall force mix Old Dominion can presently access easily outweighs any very limited negative impacts from the recent introduction of the Brittle rule. Overall, Old Dominion are in that wonderful category of army where the most interesting thing about them is what dedicated faction players might unearth in the new rules and field to surprise their opponents at Chios, which marks them as a faction to watch.

Wadhrun

Wadrhun Matriarch Queens Conquest Credit: Magos Sockbert

Wadhrun are a faction sitting in a fascinating transitional space between old and new list ideas. Fundamentally, it needs to be acknowledged that the same Thunder Chieftain, Thunder Riders, speakers-centric list that’s been their competitive mainstay for multiple update cycles is still the competitive default, for all the same reasons it has been for all this time; it has outstanding lights, mediums and heavies that operate very effectively together, Winglord Predators remain outstanding for a wide variety of reasons, Hunting Packs with shock are the deadliest little goblin light unit in the game bar none, and the extreme threat range of Thunder Riders performing march + charge + tramples remains a coup de grace that’s difficult to play around but catastrophic if you don’t.

However, at this stage, the Thunder list archetype is a very known quantity. Any opponent is likely to have as much experience playing against it as a Wadhrun player is playing with it, meaning it’s an army that’s difficult for any but the most creative players to really pull off surprises with. That’s led to the search for alternate list archetypes for Wadhrun, and there are some promising candidates: the Scion of Conquest is capable of leading quite a wide variety of different list archetypes including more infantry-centric lists with things like Chieftains (who provide very capable buffs to their warbands and nearby units even when not the warlord), and a recent experimental but quite innovative Scion of Death list with multiple Matriarch Queens has emerged, which plays a very interesting game around just enough resilience mechanics (loose formation hunters winning ranged duels, chosen of death having their resolve shored up by the Queens themselves, etc) coupled with Matriarch healing for a list that’s more resilient than it looks at a first glance.

Overall, though, it’s still the Thunder Chieftain we’d most expect to see at Chios - but it will be extremely interesting to see if an alternate list emerges.

Weavers

Recent updates and releases have seen the Weavers recover wonderfully from the malaise of their initial release, with a roster that’s now really quite complete and with a variety of different options for competitive play. The two most likely competitive archetypes are actually built around units, not warlords, with Gallowglass Wardens and Warriors forming the backbone of one style of lists, and Wyrd Knights forming the other.

Gallowglass units are tough, capable, and the Wardens in particular play good host to any character that joins them thanks to their various ranged benefits applying in play rather than at list construction (meaning a Seanchas Sidhe for example effectively becomes a fourth Warden when shooting).

Wyrd Knights, on the other hand, are just straight up on cocaine. Evasion 2 and 4 wounds as a baseline defensive profile will always carry some risk especially from something like Marid Sahar, but the sheer speed, threat range and offensive output on a unit that are relatively easily accessible as a mainstay if needed, make them the basis of a very powerful archetype that pairs especially well with a Court of Spring Warlord, who also provides healing shore up any durability issues.

If there’s anything holding weavers back, it’s that even the better units on their roster are still pretty reasonably priced. Gallowglass aren’t cheap, their characters aren’t cheap, and while their light mainstays are cheap, you get what you pay for. There are also a few dead ends on the roster, with the Moth Rider cavalry having fallen off with small recent nerfs (the Ciannir Riders in particular having met the same grisly end as most other ranged units struck by the curse of exchanging Fluid Formation for Acute Senses), but the broad potential for an event-winning Weaver list certainly now exists, which represents an excellent change of fortune for the faction.

Dweghom

Dweghom Ancestor painted by Josh Gagel

Dweghom stormed into early 2026 in an extremely commanding position off the back of Magma Sorcery and Steelforged, both of which were subsequently nerfed, which has left the faction somewhat on the back foot looking to re-find its edge. Which, frankly, is probably… Magma Sorcery and Steelforged.

Steelforged, certainly, remain an absolutely outstanding unit even post-nerf. They’re reliant on Tempered Creed tokens but rarely struggle to fuel themselves in a properly configured list, and provided they can trigger one or both bonuses, are still the same ‘heavy regiment power on a medium regiment chassis’ that they’ve always been. That, in turn, means the Tempered Creed remains the optimal line for competition dweghom lists.

The choice of warlord, however, offers some versatility. Steelshaper and Tempered Sorcerer warlords are both very viable; the Steelshaper is generally excellent and the only meaningful source of healing in a faction that makes excellent use of it (even just chip healing to keep stands topped up is good in a faction with so much Defence 4). A Fire School Tempered Sorcerer is straightforward and strong. But for my money, I stand by post-nerf Magma Sorcery still being an extremely respectable competitive archetype on any list played table setups reasonably close to the tournament pack suggested terrain layouts. While it’s true that the magma death laser no longer gets to pick out any unit anywhere on the battlefield, the threat range of pyroclasts extending 6” in all directions from any Erupting terrain piece still presents an extreme zonal threat and the general ability to put damage on enemy units from a very long way away. This pressure in turn combines excellently with tempered list core elements (fireforged, steelforged, etc) to produce a powerful and consistent gameplan, albeit one that’s less powerful than it was in days of yore.

City States

Down toward the bottom of the middle tier but not quite edging into the low tier, we have the City States. Bad news: it’s still the Polemarch Warlord, folks, plus as many Chariots as you feel comfortable fitting. Good news: turns out those things are still really good; the Polemarch is one of the single best warlords in the entire game, and the general force composition of a City States list is very sound. It just hasn’t changed much in some time, putting it in the same ‘known quantity’ quandary as Wadhrun, but without any of Wadhrun’s promising alternate builds.

Where my read on City States differs from the broader wisdom is generally around the use of light troops. Quite common in the broader metagame are Thorakites units, usually with an embedded character and making excellent use of the Thyrean Auxiliary. And this is a very good unit, but I don’t think it’s positioned well into the top of the metagame. Against Nords and Sorcerer Kings, this unit setup is overwhelmingly likely to trade into a single enemy unit and, in doing so, trade down, unless you exert either the heroic effort or tactical nous to arrange for them to execute a second clash in a subsequent turn.

My personal preference as a result is for the use of Hoplites, which when led by a character (and frankly even without them) have the single best durability to points ratio of just about any unit in the entire game up to and including Steelforged. Paying 50pts for a Haspist Auxiliary nets you 7 wounds with Defence 4 Resolve 6 when a character is leading the unit, and at 35pts per further regular infantry stand, you can scale up defence faster than your opponents can scale up offence. This is an abuseable strength, but it doesn’t seem to have fully percolated into the broader community yet.

Around these pieces, the broader frills of the faction remain sound. Companion Cavalry are offensively cost-effective the same way Hoplites are defensively cost-effective; Inquisitors are well-rounded brutes, and while the various giants are slightly less cost-effective than their Nord Mountain Jotnar cousins, they’re at least within spitting distance with an added layer of utility and are therefore eminently solid.

For established City States players, the faction is feeling a bit long in the tooth by now ( the search for any non-Polemarch warlord is getting desperate), but the single list archetype with some variations remains competitively quite viable.

Low-Tier

Spires

Straddling the bottom of middle tier and the top of low tier, Spires are a faction who received a rework this year that took their previous faction identity built around complicated schemes, plans, buffs and debuffs and replaced it with a wide variety of ways to kill their own troops so the opposition doesn't have to (the typical expected value of which is left as an exercise to the reader).

However, notwithstanding that, there are a few strengths Spires can leverage to produce good results at an event. While virtually all units in the faction this side of Incarnate Sentinels are at least somewhat fragile and made more so by the need to carry wounds to activate their Strain abilities, there remain some units that are a serviceable mix of fast and dangerous. The Abomination in particular has become a cornerstone of Spires lists, and although it’s still very fragile by the standards of monsters, at 180pts it usually carries its weight. In addition, the recent change to the Directorate subfaction rules open it up as an alternative to Underspires for players who want higher risk and higher reward, with both being quite viable for competitive play (within the overall limits to the faction).

Overall, the biggest risk to Spires armies is generally Spires players themselves, as the faction’s fragility ruthlessly punishes mistakes - a Spires army generally struggles to survive errors in judgement, position or list construction (Merchant Princes are bad in competitive play; I will die on this hill). This creates a lot more scope for failure than success at an event as challenging as the World Championships, putting Spires close to the bottom of our meta snapshot, although by no means entirely out of the running.

Hundred Kingdoms

Hundred Kingdoms recently received a partial rework to accommodate new Orders regiments, which has left the faction in a slightly confused design space. The new regiments are serviceable enough; many Hundred Kingdoms are quite enthusiastic about the Martyrs of Saint Lazarus, for example, who are slightly inconsistent but overall still quite good human equivalent to Dweghom Flame Berserkers - but none will be legal in time for Chios list submission.

(In the case of the Order of the Sword: Good news for spectators.)

This leaves Hundred Kingdoms players looking to the existing model range, which is quite complete even without the new Orders regiment, but left somewhat discombobulated by a series of slightly confusing sidegrades and nerfs within the faction. An unexpected nerf to Battlefield Orders range has left the Imperial Officer edging ever closer to being the most useless character in the game once he’s actually deployed to the table, for example. Only a small number of rules and supremacies now make much use of the Feudal army rule to share keywords with their dynastic ally, and while a series of new warlord options have been introduced that represent a broadening of options, virtually all of which represent a small nerf in power compared to prior incarnations. Given the faction was reported to have the lowest win rate in the game prior to the update (although such statistics should always be taken with a large grain of salt), this begs the question as to what Hundred Kingdoms should be reaching for in competition play right now.

For our money, the most well-rounded competition list in the Hundred Kingdoms probably remains a Theist-Feudal alliance, with a Theist Priest Warlord (or dynastic ally). This alliance combination was already a well-rounded mix of durability, speed and hitting power, and it actually saw a small increase in power with the change to several Theist spells which can flow to the entire army during the Spirit Shines turn. In practice, setting up the full power of that fully armed and operational battlestation typically represents too many activations before the punching starts on a pivotal supremacy turn, but the high watermark has nevertheless risen and there are some additional interesting options with the new Theist spells outside the big go turn as well (being able to give Archangels Flurry, for example).

Even with the confusing downgrades and sidegrades to many of the Hundred Kingdoms’ subfaction combinations, it’s still absolutely conceivable that either a Theist Feudal list or some other dynastic alliance (an Order Warhorses list, for example) might appear at and even perform well at Chios. If we look all the way up to the top of the ladder, the difference between Hundred Kingdoms and Nords, Sorcerer Kings, Yoroni, Old Dominion etcetera isn’t that large. A list tooled in the right way, that’s ready for the opposition, in the hands of a practiced Hundred Kingdoms player still has a chip and a chair, which is a very positive thing to say about the game overall.
