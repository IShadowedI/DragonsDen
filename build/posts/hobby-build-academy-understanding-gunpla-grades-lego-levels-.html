Hobby Build Academy: Understanding Gunpla Grades, Lego Levels, and Finding the Kit for You

August 3rd, 2026 via Goonhammer

Welcome back to another edition of Hobby Build Academy! I'm your Professor Marcy, and my assistant as always is the lovely Elly! this week we're going to talk about understanding grades, skill levels, and more, in order to help you find the best kit for your level of comfort, or how to get started with kits that feel fun to build and rewarding even as a total beginner. In a lot of cases, you might be surprised at how fun and engaging even "simple" kits can be these days, but understanding what you might be getting yourself into is one of the first important steps to enjoying model kit building without getting frustrated or feeling like you've wasted your money.

Gunpla Grades: Mind the Curve

An example of the various differences in grades: A RG Zeong, HG Zaku, and EG Conan Edogawa!

Marcy: So what's the highest level Gunpla grade you've built?

Elly: To be honest, I don't think I've ever gone beyond HG grade? The Deathscythe I built when I was a kid in Puerto Rico was a bootleg, and it was so bad at fitting together, but for more official kits I don't think I ever went much above the types you usually see in stores.

Marcy: I've only built up to RG myself, and it's  been a very long time since I did that; frankly, I spent a lot of time with SD kits, to the point that I could build them in about half an hour or less depending on what type they were. I know what you mean about the bootleg and unofficial kits, though; I had quite a few of those in the SD range too.

If you're a newer builder, perhaps some of those abbreviations above didn't make a whole lot of sense to you; even if you are somewhat casually familiar with Gunpla, the types of grades and kits that are available can still be a bit overwhelming! The best thing to understand is that these kits generally work on something of a sliding scale of difficulty, moving from easiest and most accessible to the more difficult and challenging. But did you know: Gunpla grades also correlate to their scale as well as their implied difficulty!

Entry Grade is the "beginner" model that Bandai makes. EG models are often very simple and have a few parts, running in the 1/144 scale range, and you can often find EG for various popular Gundam kits such as the Gundam Wing or classic RX-78, but something that may not be that well known is that Bandai has numerous licensed model kits in the EG range that are quick, fun little models of characters like Ultraman, Goku, Pac-Man, Doraemon, and Detective Conan!

Elly: Does this also include the Pokémon kits? I know we've seen them around and I've had some interest in building a few of them, like Koraidon, but I don't think I've seen them labeled as EG.

Keropla, based on Keroro Gunso, are basically EG models, even if they don't say it.

Marcy: Correct! A very helpful way to understand the level of a kit is either the grade, or the age listed on the box. Entry Grade kits are listed as 6+, and that includes kits like the Pokémon kits, which are sometimes subtitled "Model Kit QUICK", Haropla kits like the ones we built last week, Keropla (Keroro Gunso / Sgt. Frog) kits, and other small sub groups. If you see 6+ on a box, you can generally assume it is in the Entry Grade range, and is often a quick build model for beginners.

Elly: I noticed the RX-78 we built at the pop-up is similar, and when we were building it, didn't require nippers to remove the pieces.

Marcy: Entry Grade kits are made to be twist off, so while you can use nippers to remove pieces more cleanly if you like, they'll generally separate from their sprues without tool assistance, again to help beginners and children to build kits. They also tend to require very minimal sticker placement. Anoter way to spot them is price: most reputable retailers don't place EG models above 18.99~ USD.

GQuuuuuuX is one of the newer HG, featuring a lot of articulation that older kits lack.

High Grade is the most common Gunpla kit, and the one you've likely seen, built, or interacted with if you bought a kit from a hobby store or similar retailer. They're often fairly affordable, in the 20~30 USD range, although some kits can be more (a good example is Chalia's Rick Dom from GQuuuuuuX, which runs around 40 due to size and extra parts). Like EG models, High Grade, or HG models, are 1/144 scale, and have very good detail and often come with stickers and decals to give them more customization. Although HG kits are pretty common and listed as beginner to intermediate kits, they can be quite challenging in some cases.

Elly: Yeah, like when they come with waterslide decals and you spend a lot of time applying them!

Marcy: Exactly. HG is 8+, and "intermediate", which is often something you may have to determine from how the kit looks or what it says it comes with. Some kits are very simple, while others can be finnicky and hard to build; also, older HG kits are sometimes a bit more fragile and fussy than newer ones. A fairly common opinion in Gunpla circles is that HG kits after Iron Blooded Orphans / Witch from Mercury are far superior than those before, which is roughly around 2015~ forward.

Elly: HG are pretty quick to build, and I have been pretty happy with how they can be, decals and stickers aside. I think they make a great starting point for people who aren't buying for a child, and feel like they can follow instructions pretty easily; the HGs I've built, even some of the older ones, have never given me a lot of frustration and still build up easily with just a pair of nippers to remove the pieces, and sometimes a file for bad cuts.

This RG Zeong, coming soon to my table. And your eyes.

Real Grade (RG) is the last of the 1/144 kits, and are functionally a step-up in difficulty from HG kits but without going into larger scaled kits. There is a sizeable step up in difficulty from HG to RG kits, because they are the same scale, but with far more complex parts; for example, some RG kits have an interior skeleton or deeper articulation than HG kits, and also tend to include some of the larger mobile suits from Gundam series that are simply more complicated due to their size, such as the Zeong. Newer prints of RG kits and newer releases will have an age listed, which is generally 15+.

Perhaps the biggest thing you might notice between an HG and RG kit, aside from more detail and articulation, is price. RG kits tend to be almost twice as expensive as HG kits, if not more at times, ranging in a lot of cases from 50 to 100 USD depending on the kit and where you find it. Generally you're paying for the quality of the kit or a special release, and if you're looking to build your favorite mobile suit and want a challenge, RG is a very satisfying place to look. Also, as mentioned, you'll find a lot of the "weirder" mobile suits here, like the Zeong.

Elly: You really like that thing.

Marcy: It's cool! One of the mobile suits ever!

MG boxes can be tricky; a lot of them don't always have the age on them like newer models, but just remember MG are a big step up in size and complexity.

Master Grade is the step just below the hardest models available, and MG kits are 1/100 scale, making them quite large, roughly around 7 inches tall and about the size of your average "deluxe" action figure or Transformer. Master Grades are similarly 15+, but the fact that the age is the same as RG doesn't mean you should view them the same; MG kits are extremely detailed, and the bigger size means that they can be a little more difficult to assemble due to various gimmicks or use of waterslide decals and stickers. Master Grade models also tend to be the level at which painting becomes more of a concern; while the kits are still made in colored plastic and do not require painting, they often struggle to go from "toy" to "cool model" without some application of paint and detailing, speaking to the larger investment of time and skill. Most MG kits start at 100 USD, and often range from that to 150 USD.

Elly: I'm excited to finally build my first MG!

Marcy: Honestly, me too! I've never invested in one.

Elly: Are you going to say Zeong again-

Marcy: No! But only because I think the RG Zeong is a better kit.

Aside from their price tags, PG boxes tend to come in these very simplistic and "elegant" boxes, and are pretty hard to miss.

Perfect Grade is the final "main" grade of Gunpla, and also the most difficult. They are the tallest kits, standing around at least 12 inches tall, or 1/60 scale. Perfect Grade really are a world of their own, and tend to have a lot of intricate gimmicks like lights, deeply complex articulation, and things you won't find in smaller kits. They are also extremely expensive! Most PG kits range in the hundreds of dollars USD, and really are meant to be invested in by experienced model kit builders who have the money, time, and dedication to spend on them. Much like MG kits, these are models that you will want to invest time and skill into; painting, or detail painting, can add a lot to these, and they often come with or have purchasable LED sets you can use to really give them some cool interactive action. They're also almost exclusively the domain of waterslide decals, so be warned.

Elly: Are there other grades or styles of Bandai kits? Like the EG models we mentioned earlier, there seem to be a lot of kits that don't have a strict grade listed.

Marcy: There are. The RE/100 line is a great example, which are 1/100 scale models made with what Bandai calls "modern" tools. Despite their size, they're fairly similar to RG kits over PG kits, and tend to be fairly affordable. There's also the METAL BUILD line, another 1/100 line that includes metallic die-cast pieces and other more complex accessories, and a very high price tag; most Metal Builds are hundreds of dollars; these sit between PG and MG in terms of complexity, especially including having to work with metal. Finally, there are some kits listed as FULL MECHANICS that are 1/100 scale, but are often listed as 8+, putting their complexity around that of a HG kit, but at the size of an MG.

No matter what you think of them, SD Kit box art often goes hard as hell.

Super-Deformed, or SD, are kind of an outlier. SD kits tend to exist in their own little world, and can be a little complicated to place because of that. Often they're mistaken as being beginner or kid friendly, but SD kits actually often carry a 15+ rating by Bandai. Having built quite a few of them, I can understand why: they are quite small, often with a lot of little pieces and gimmicks, but I will say most adult builders, even beginners, can probably start with SD kits. Kids, however, might be better off with EG kits, as SD kits can be very unforgiving with their decals and have many, many pointy accessories and details. Fun fact: there are even MG SD kits!

Lego Levels: Numbers are Your Friend

This Japanese Plum Blossom build is the perfect example of "18". A small kit, but not meant to be moved; it actually has free, unsecured pieces in the vase to mimic soil.

Elly: So, for LEGOs, there are a few complications to finding a comparative list of skill levels. LEGO doesn't use a "grading" system like Gunpla do, and instead tend to divide sets into ages, but once you leave the 6+ age range on LEGOs, kits tend to be pretty liberal by what an "18+" kit can imply, and you'll find that many 9+ kits can be intricate and dedicated builds that may take some serious time. As an example, LEGO lists the Ferrari 488 model kit, the Imperial Remnant AT-AT, and Chicken Jockey kits as 9+. That's--

Marcy: Chicken Jockeyyy--

Elly: Stop it. 

Marcy: Sorry.

Elly: Anyway! To understand that more, the AT-AT kit is listed as having 1453 pieces, while Chicken Jockey has 344, and the Ferrari sits in the middle at 882. The SEGA Genesis kit is listed as 12+, but only has 479 pieces; even the flower and botanical kits are listed at 18+ and vary in pieces from 300 to 1500.

Marcy: I'm getting a sense that the age system for LEGOs is more... vibes based?

Elly: I'd say that is fairly accurate, actually. What you'll often find is that kits listed at 18+ are either due to theme or complexity, and the same is true for kits that fall under younger age ranges. The AT-AT kit is far more complicated than the Eevee kit, but Eevee is listed at 18+, for a reason that might make more sense when you read it: 18+ kits are often meant to be displayed, not played with. While you certainly CAN play with them, they aren't generally designed for that, and instead are designed to be built and put on display; this is also why, at least I think, the plant, painting, and also why a lot of kits like the X-Files or Twilight kits are 18+.

Marcy: So for LEGO curious builders, our general suggestion is to look for kits you think you'd be interested in building. Since LEGO covers such a wide variety of tastes and properties, you're likely better off looking for things that catch your interest, rather than worrying about having to buy in at a certain level to get started. You can also find a lot of great kits as lower age ranges, and you may want to consider the amount of pieces and size of a kit as the marks of complexity.

Elly: These 2 botanical kits could not have been more different. The cacti took me about 20 minutes, the Poinsetta an hour, but both carry a similar rating.

Elly: The botanicals line is a great place to start; they are fairly easy to build, relaxing, and a good introduction to LEGOs, while making for great display pieces; they have some of the most unique and bespoke LEGO pieces I've seen. If you really want a challenge, the painting kits, like Van Gogh's Sunflowers or Starry Night, can be great if you have the wall space to hang them on once you're done building them. The Pokémon sets are also a lot of fun, but I'm pretty biased there, since I love Pokémon!

Picking the Kits For You

Marcy: Probably the biggest thing we can offer is that you should look for kits that interest you, whether they are Gunpla from Bandai, LEGO kits, or other model kits that aren't Bandai produced. For example, we recently picked up a pretty cool kit for Elly from Aoshima, the Hachiroku from Initial-D!

The Aoshima Snap Kit line is functionally similar to HG Gunpla; they require no glue, but are still a bit more challenging than an EG style kit.

Elly: Yeah! I'm super excited to build that one. and, well, the biggest tip we can give for kits or lines that don't come from Bandai is to look for comparable information; if you think you'd prefer to build model cars, planes, or boats, then age ranges and various instructions on the box can often give you a pretty good idea of what you'll need. A lot of model kits in these ranges can require glue, but some, like this Aoshima kit, is essentially similar to a HG Gundam, requiring no glue and instead snapping together. I also suggest looking up whatever information you can find if you're unsure of a kit that isn't from a brand you know, ensuring whether you'll need glue, for example.

This Aoshima Trueno kit requires glue, so its always important to check the box, or the internet, especially for kits that aren't translated.

Marcy: What about non-LEGO kit brick style models?

Elly: Generally my experience with those has been very similar to LEGO themselves; I tend to look at the age and the amount of pieces. About the only note I'd make here is Nanoblocks, which tend to be pretty simple to build, BUT are also incredibly tiny! I've seen them popping up all over the place lately, and they can be a lot of fun, but the pieces are SO small that I don't really recommend them for little kids who might swallow them, and frankly, if you've got trouble with fine motion skills, they might be difficult too.

Hopefully, this little primer on various models and how to judge difficulty and skill will give you a great start on your own hobbying journey! Next time, we'll be back with a new round of builds, and we'll be talking about some of the ups (and downs) of using waterslide decals and more!
