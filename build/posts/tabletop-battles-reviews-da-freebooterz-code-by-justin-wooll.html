Tabletop Battles Reviews: Da Freebooterz Code, by Justin Woolley

July 10th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Games Workshop

Alright, I'm making it official. As of now, I'm an Ork guy.

My road here has been long and winding, quite different from the from-worst-to-first turbo boost the Night Lords received after I read Aaron Dembski-Bowden's extraordinary trilogy. No, with our greenskin pals it's been a chip here, a crack there, a slow erosion of my antiphathy to their version of grimdark comedy.

It's not that I haven't enjoyed humor in the 41st millennium, far from it! Sandy Mitchell's Cain stories are top-tier stuff, if a bit repetitive at times. But humor is a very subjective thing, and one reader's "ork punches grot in face" guffaw is another one's groan. It was less that the humor wasn't somehow 'sophisticated,' so much as it often felt like the writers were trying too hard for the laughs.

The first crack in the ice came courtesy of Painboyz, the Mike Brooks story I read as part of the Blood of the Imperium anthology. "I’ve read other Ork stories that may be entertaining enough," I wrote in my review at the time, "but when it comes to the humor it’s either trying too hard or not hard enough."

Denny Flowers has done a lot to move the ball forward. He's been terrific at dolloping humor onto an underlying sense of menace, which makes for a terrific cocktail in Grotsnik, Da Mad Dok (review) and Ghazghkull Thraka: Warlord of Warlords (review). His Riderz of the Storm (from the World Ablaze anthology, reviewed here) I found "sidesplittingly hilarious," and with Da Freebooterz Code, Justin Woolley found the net.

I loved this book.

Image credit: Wizards of the Coast

Year of the Ork

With the launch of the game's Eleventh Edition and a return to Armageddon, it sure feels like everything's coming up green- halcyon days for the faction much the same way as we saw with the Krieg refresh not that long ago with new goodies for both tabletop and library. This certainly made Hobart, Australia-based Woolley the right quill to pull off the rack. "Anyone familiar with my work for Black Library will know that this novel fits squarely in my wheelhouse," he notes in the book's Special Edition introduction. "I'm drawn to writing them, and the Adeptus Editorium staff seem to think I do a good job because they keep asking me back."

A look at Woolley's portfolio certainly bears this out. After getting his foot in the door in 2019 with Redemption Through Sacrifice digital short (later included in Only War: Stories from the 41st Millennium anthology), he's also penned Prisoners of Waaagh! and Long Live Da Red Gobbo. Outside of the Black Library, he's also written a sci-fi comedy series Shakedowners, and his Goodreads profile even notes that his first book, written at age six, was "a 300-word epic about pirates." One might say that Da Freebooterz Code is the story he was born to write, and he takes full advantage here.

Image credit: FFG

Da Writin'

At its heart, the novel is a faceoff between two main characters. On the one hand, you've got a Lord Captain Antoinette von Hume, scion of a proud and noble Rogue Trader family1 and commodore of a small fleet of voidcraft. Arrogant, haughty, and with a ramrod sense of duty that even the Emperor himself would struggle to find flaw with.

In the other corner is Skeeg Horntoof, an ork who through a sudden turn of events finds himself kaptin of Gork's Revenge. Horntoof is an unusual member of his species, a finkin' ork's ork, if you will:

"Unlike other orks, he saw the future as something that could be planned for. Horntoof's ability to to plan was the reason he'd found himself an exile from ork society. He'd been kicked out of the Bad Moons- an ork clan famously obsessed with wealth- because his ability to plan had made him too rich, so which that he was having a negative impact on the economic stability of the local tribe by single-handedly causing deflation in monetary value by overcirculation.

"Of course, the orks didn't understand this; they just considered it zoggin' unfair that this git's teef grew faster than everyone else's and weird that he kept talking about forward estimates and interest rates, so they left him marooned on a lifeless moon with an atmosphere barely thick enough to keep him alive."

Woolley pits the two of them on a collision course, and when Horntoof manages to steal von Hume's flagship and blow up almost the rest of her fleet, the blood feud between them drives the rest of the book as she looks to reclaim her Warrant of Trade, her flagship, and her daughter (in roughly that order of importance), who is hiding away on the flagship overrun with greenskins.

As he contends with the fury of the Rogue Trader, Horntoof must keep his own gang under control. Towards that end, he decrees that they shall adopt Da Freebooterz Code, an unusually progressive and forward-thinking code of conduct:

Image credit: Games Workshop

As you'd expect, not every ork is as enthusiastic about the Code- or Horntoof's unusual captaincy- as he is, and having to navigate ork politics at home while raiding abroad is more than enough to keep Horntoof busy. He has to contend with the machinations of his first mate, mekboys chafing at his leadership, and the remains of the humie crew of the captured flagship causing all sorts of mischief and trouble- and that's before von Hume attempts to collect on her blood vendetta.

Da Finkin'

Right around page 200 of the 283-page novel, it struck me that I was over two-thirds of the way done and it had barely felt like I'd started. Usually by this point I feel like I've sat with the characters and plot for awhile, and I'm looking to see how the author brings everything to a close. Yet with Da Freebooterz Code, the pages just flew by. This is a book that is unabashedly fun.

Woolley has an excellent grasp of pacing and momentum. There are no long detours or sidebars, no unnecessary sideplots or excessive internal dialogues. He sketches his characters with affection and depth, and then ladles out perfect portions of each as he moves across the points of view and story beats. It's a pity that the lighthearted tone of these kinds of books can sometimes leave them overlooked when it comes to awards and recognition, because it takes just as much skill to pull off well.

"The fact of it was that Bludkurla had never been the same since those pink warp squig-things had come in through the walls. Mad Mek Brokebonez said it had been a failure of the something inverter on the thingy-wotsit and the force field had given out. The result had been a sudden, over-whelming influx of weird stuff. There were snarling pink beasties with enormous mouths and massive teeth dripping with slime. Ghostly blue squigglies that were all sucker-covered tentacles with no heads. Red spider-things that were as big as a grot with rows of pointed fangs all the way down their gaping throats. The orks had to fight them all.

"It was the best Tuesday they'd ever had."

"There were two things I loved as a kid," wrote Woolley, "well, more than two things- but two that stand out were pirates and Warhammer 40K. When I was asked to write a freebooter novel, I couldn't say yes fast enough." This is one of those books where the writer's love of the source material pours from the page. Indeed, there was a certain underlying vibe here I found similar to some of Robert Rath's work, where a passion for history reflects perfectly through a grimdark lens.

Woolley himself notes that Horntoof and his first mate, Black Skad, were partly based on the relationship between real-life pirates Stede Bonnet and Edward "Blackbeard" Teach. "Their dynamic- a mix of camaraderie and rivalry with two very different levels of seafaring ability- helped shape the crew of the Gork's Revenge." No less so is the adversarial pairing of Horntoof and von Hume. "Many pirate vessels really did live by a set of codes that were, at least by the standards of the day, quite progressive," he goes on. "Contrast that with the authoritarian, hammer-fist of rogue trader Antoinette von Hume, whose Warrant of Trade stands in for the historical letter of marque issued to privateers."

All in all, that fascinating historical context, the terrific writing, and just the right amount of humor make Da Freebooterz Code one of my breakout reads this year. I genuinely hope this isn't the last we've seen of Kaptin Horntoof, because the Black Library has a winner here- both in the character and its writer.

Finally, I'll leave you with the book's opening line:

'I was dere,' he would say afterwords. 'I was dere da day Horntoof slew von Hume.'

Perfection.

Footnote

  1. I can tell I've played a bit too much of Owlcat's terrific Rogue Trader CRPG, because without fail every time I read someone address her as 'Lord Captain,' it was in Abelard's voice.
