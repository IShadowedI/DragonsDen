Tabletop Battles Reviews: The Wicked and the Warped, by Robert Rath

September 18th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Games Workshop

Have you ever disliked something you read in a book so much that you physically took it out on the book itself?

I've done that exactly once in my life, but it's as fresh as yesterday. Growing up in the 80's, my childhood crush was Daryl Hannah after seeing her play a mermaid in 1984's Splash. Two years later she starred in The Clan of the Cave Bear as an orphaned Cro-Magnon woman, Ayla, taken in by a tribe of Neanderthals. Adolescent me found the story so compelling that I dove right into Jean M. Auel's book that the film was adapted from.

I progressed smoothly though the Earth's Children series, hopping from The Clan of the Cave Bear into The Valley of Horses- before running into trouble of the worst kind with The Mammoth Hunters. See, the second book of the series had introduced us to another Cro-Magnon, Jondalar, who served as Ayla's love interest after she had departed the tribe of Neanderthals. The two travel alone together for a time, before meeting the titular mammoth-hunting tribe in the third book. There, Ayla gets her head turned by brash ivory-carver Ranec, and when she succumbs to his charms and sleeps with him I was so incensed that I hurled the book across my room.

It rested there for three days before I was grudgingly able to resume the story.

I've had issues with other books over the years, but none have ever been quite so visceral as that one (ahh, youth). I found Robert Heinlein's second-half Stranger in a Strange Land transition so disagreeable that for months I was tempted to split the book in two and toss the back half into the rubbish bin, though a feeling for the sacredness of the art form stayed my hand. Throwing in a moment of volatility might be okay, but willfully damaging was off-limits.

It would take me many years more to begin to learn that just because I didn't like the choices and author had made doesn't necessarily mean that those were bad choices. Auel's love triangle powered much of The Mammoth Hunters through to its conclusion, delivering a tremendous payoff when Ayla chooses to continue journeying with Jondalar rather than remaining in the camp with Ranec. Stranger in a Strange Land's somewhat controversial mid-book pivot certainly didn't stop it from winning the prestigious Hugo Award in 1962.

And similarly, Robert Rath's choices in The Wicked and the Warped may not have been ones I enjoyed, but by no means does that make it a bad book.

Image credit: Games Workshop

The Story

The Wicked and the Warped begins when an ancient Necron world once lost to the Warp reappears in realspace following the rend in the galaxy caused by the Great Rift. Having marinaded in the immaterium for millennia, it immediately attracts the attention of Trazyn when it begins broadcasting a distress signal.

Trazyn, of course, sees ripe opportunity to acquire items of historical significance. Though a deeply skeptical Orikan shows little interest in accompanying him, when the matter rises to the attention of Triarch Praetorian Executor Phillias (also returning from the first book) it quickly becomes a group expedition- willing or otherwise.

That group- accompanied by a human Inquisitor plucked from suspended animation in Trazyn's galleries as a sort of canary in the coal mine for warp sensitivity- finds a world filled with the remnants of Necron civilization, yet also indelibly suffused with the essence of Chaos. As their expedition gets mired deeper and deeper in this surreal and mysterious world, can they discover what's really happened to the world of Amenthis in time to make it back out again?

The Sophomore Slump

As I discussed in my recent review of The Infinite and the Divine's Illustrated and Annotated Edition, the book was Rath's debut novel in 2020 and its resultant success was by no means assured. Thinking there was a good chance he might not get a second book offer from the Black Library, he swung for the fences with that one- and connected. The combination of historical-centered drama combined with a delightful comedic element proved such a hit with readers that it eventually dethroned none other than Dan Abnett's Horus Rising to take the '#1 book' shelf at Warhammer World.

While Rath has written two other novels since, with 2022's Assassinorum: Kingmaker followed the next year by The Fall of Cadia, he almost certainly faced a different kind of pressure in writing the return of everyone's favorite Necron 'odd couple,' Trazyn the Infinite and Orikan the Diviner. In short, how do you recapture the magic?

This question has haunted creatives for as long as people have been creating. and it's probably safe to assume that the unlikelier the initial success, the greater the pressure can be to avoid the dreaded 'sophomore slump.' For every The Godfather Part II, there's at least one (if not more) Southland Tales; for every The Bends by Radiohead there's another like MGMT's Congratulations. If, as Princess Irulan notably observes in Frank Herbert's Dune, a "beginning is a difficult time," a follow-up is no walk in the park either.

Imagine what this was like for Rath as he sat down at his writing desk for The Wicked and the Warped the first time. While obviously no party to his internal deliberations, I imagine with regards to the book's tone they may broadly have gone something like this: with the first book a dramatic story with comedic elements, do you...

  1. Lean into the drama a bit more
  2. Lean into the comedy a bit more
  3. Try and strike roughly the same balance as you did the first time

While past results are never a guarantor of future performance, we can probably assume that the third option there- dancing with the one you came with- is generally the safest best. So with that being said, I give Rath a lot of credit for picking the option I would have assumed to be the biggest gamble: option number two.

And boy does it lean in.

Image credit: Games Workshop

Laugh til' You Cry

Comedy in 40K isn't quite the no-man's land as we see with romance, but it's probably fair to call it a distant second. You have the Orks, of course, Sandy Mitchell's Commissar Cain, and then... well, that's pretty much it, right? That isn't to say that the grim, dark future doesn't have moments of humor (our early introduction to Guardsman Hawke in Graham McNeill's Storm of Iron certainly comes to mind), but humor and comedy aren't the same thing.

The Infinite and the Divine was a masterpiece, not least because of how deftly Rath managed to weave its elements together into an extraordinary whole. From Orikan's use of rewound time to rig up a tribunal hearing in his favor to Trazyn's Catachan "gift" for his rival, the book's hilarity was often clever, subtle, and often unexpected. I still occasionally chuckle when I recall the scene I mentioned in my review:

"I'm being deliberately vague here to avoid any spoilers for the new reader, but perhaps my favorite example here was when Trazyn- who is able to shift his consciousness into other, lesser Necron- masquerades as one of Orikan's soldiers, affecting a deliberate slowness and stupidity sure to trigger the intellectual snobbishness of his rival. It's side-splittingly funny watching Orikan grow increasingly exasperated and on-tilt, but only because Rath has put in the work throughout the book of showing Orikan's smartest-guy-in-the-room arrogance."

Those wanting more of the same in The Wicked and the Warped may well end up disappointed. Rather than longer-buildup humor, Rath plays in a much more immediate, slapstick space that frequently inviked the animations of my youth, from Tom & Jerry to Looney Tunes. If you've ever seen two characters fighting over an item, with one of them grabbing it before running away in the opposite direction only to run into a low-hanging tree branch when they turn to stick their tongue out at their rival, well, you'll know exactly what I mean here. In fact, something very close to this exact gag occurs early in the book.

In another moment, Trazyn gets Orikan to look down a hole before shoving him in. Later- in a gag that immediately brought Daffy Duck to mind- Orikan gets ready to charge an enemy. Trazyn, channeling classic Bugs Bunny in turn, has his 'I wouldn't do that if I were you' moment, Orikan ignores him, then jumps back to safety moments later shot up and trailing black smoke.

But perhaps most of all, the constant squabbling between the two seemed to border on Flanderization, a topic I recently went into some detail on in my audiobook review of Sandy Mitchell's Death or Glory. Part of the problem is simply down to the book's narrative structure.

In The Infinite and the Divine, Orikan and Trazyn spent as much of the book apart as they did together. The jabs and needling, then, were funny because the humor had room to breathe. Rath's decision to pair the two together for nearly the entirely of The Wicked and the Warped means that the pacing of their interaction seldom gets a reprieve. They're together all the time, so there's no room for elaborate schemes or breaks between squabbling interactions. Imagine having dinner with a witty, sarcastic couple who enjoys taking the piss out from one another as a form of entertainment. Then try to imagine spending a week camping with them.

Image credit: Games Workshop

So What Worked?

This wasn't the book that I was hoping for when it was announced, but Robert Rath is an exceptional writer and there were still plenty of things that worked here- even if (for me) they couldn't quite escape the shadow of what didn't.

For one thing, I really enjoyed how Rath looked to showcase a different side of the two characters by hobbling their strengths. We saw plenty of Orikan's temporal shenanigans and Trazyn's ability to shift bodies in the first book; here, Rath forces them to have to go without recourse to those powers for much of the story given the nature of the realm they're in. This was canny storycrafting, deftly avoiding painting himself into the proverbial corner in a way that made total sense for the narrative and allowing him to peer with his author's eye into other elements of the characters.

There were also some genuinely terrific passages when Rath allowed his characters to connect with one another on a deeper level. A conversation between Trazyn and Orikan on consciousness and time travel dove into the metaphysical left me wanting more of these kinds of discussions, and passages dealing with dreaming and the nature of the soul gave the book a glimpse of the depth its predecessor enjoyed.

"That was one aspect I really wanted to explore in this book," he notes in the Special Edition's introduction, "how Orikan's ability to manipulate time makes him alienated and cynical... He carries the scars of all these abandoned timelines, having experienced every insult and slight that some alternate version of Trazyn has thrown at him."

"With Trazyn," he continues, "I was interested in digging into how divorced he feels from the galaxy. His soullessness keeps him from authentically experiencing life and belonging, while his immortality and sense of time forces him into the role of an observer in a galaxy that's passed him by."

There are also some terrific Easter eggs and clever touches in here, particularly as we get a look at Trazyn's collections. An offhand (and hilariously irreverent) reference to Voyager and its Golden Record. One of the last statues in existence of one of the purged primarchs. Scarabs killing their foes with heat like honeybees. A cameo from a larger-than-life figure from one of his past novels.

Rath has one of the best "attention to detail" eyes in the modern Black Library, and in particular the sequences in Trazyn's gallery on Solemnace had the same "blink and you'll miss a reference" feeling I associate most strongly with Robert Anton Wilson's Illuminatus! Trilogy. You also see some of this in the way that Rath masterfully manages to tie in the first book to the second, but not in a way you'd ever expect.

Final Thoughts

Even if ultimately The Wicked and the Warped didn't click for me, I was heartened to see how much enjoyment people have been sharing from reading this one in the usual Black Library watering holes. Just as with Marc Collins's Eidolon, the Auric Hammer (which placed second in the Black Library Book of the Year for 2024), it's a good reminder that just because I didn't enjoy a book doesn't mean that others won't- and it certainly doesn't mean that the book is bad.

If there's a lament, it's that I'd have tremendously enjoyed a book where we were able to better explore the inner workings of both Orikan and Trazyn- as Rath noted in the introduction- without the distraction of the dialed-up silliness.

Given the book's ending, it seems highly likely that we're now two-thirds of the way through a trilogy, with (at least) one more Trazyn/Orikan book forthcoming. I'll be keen to see which direction he ends up leaning for it, and whether it will be more like this book or a return to the more balanced approach of The Infinite and the Divine.

As long as neither one shacks up with a Cro-Magnon ivory carver, I'm sure I'll be okay.
