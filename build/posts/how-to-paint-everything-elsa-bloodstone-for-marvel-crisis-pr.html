How to Paint Everything: Elsa Bloodstone for Marvel Crisis Protocol

August 6th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Elsa Bloodstone is a relatively new addition to the Marvel Universe, having first appeared as a novice vampire slayer in a four-issue miniseries in 2001. She was somewhat reinvented in her next appearance five years later (in Nextwave #1) as a sarcastic, world-weary monster hunter, and it is this version of the character that has been a mainstay across Marvel’s supernatural-themed titles ever since.

Apart from that original appearance, in which she had blonde hair and a much skimpier costume, Elsa’s look has been very consistent: a striking orange-and-black outfit with a grey overcoat. It stands out amid all the blue and red (or black leather) that Marvel’s heroes tend to favor, so we’ll want to make sure it stands out on the tabletop too.

Preparation

With only 12 pieces to glue together, assembly was fairly straightforward. I like my MCP models to look consistent, so normally I’m content to use the standard bases, but for Elsa I decided to do a little bit of extra work. She’s standing with one foot up on a tombstone, which would look pretty silly sticking out of a New York sidewalk. Before priming, I added a bit of dirt texture to the base using Vallejo Diorama Effects Brown Earth, following the lines of the sidewalk cracks to create a “plot” of dirt for the tombstone to be sticking out of.

After giving the texture a few hours to dry, I primed the whole model in Vallejo brush-on black primer, my usual go-to primer.

Getting Started

Once the black primer is completely dry, I usually start by drybrushing the entire model in Army Painter Deep Grey. I find that this step is really useful for revealing details in the model (especially since I’m priming in black), and it often gives me a head start on black or dark details, in this case Elsa’s boots, belt and gloves.

Following that, I like to do the bases first. It may seem counterintuitive, but I’m going to be doing a lot of drybrushing on the base, and it’s a messy process that will be much easier to clean up before I’ve spent time detailing the figure.

Color guide by Jefferson Powers

For the concrete areas of the base, I started with a solid coat of Army Painter Deep Grey, followed by drybrushed layers of Army Painter Ash Grey and then Reaper Misty Grey.

Note: I am in the slow process of switching my paints over from Reaper to Army Painter, so I’m using a combination of the two – I don’t really think one brand is intrinsically better than the other; it’s just that none of my local shops carries Reaper paint any more.

I left the tombstone black with Deep Grey highlights, adding some lighter highlights while doing the Ash Grey drybrush layer, which left the tombstone a few shades darker than the surrounding concrete.

With the concrete part done, I then filled in the dirt area with Army Painter Leather Brown, then hit it with a Strong Tone wash to get it to a nice, shaded brown which contrasts pretty well with the dark grey tombstone and light grey concrete. I used a flat brush for the drybrushing and managed to get it done without splashing too much paint on Elsa’s feet – I would be able to pass off what little is there as highlights on her leather boots.

Trenchcoat

Normally it’s easier to work from the inside out, starting with skin, then doing clothes and saving things like belts and shoes for last. However, I knew I would be doing a lot of drybrushing on the outer layers so I decided to do them first.

Elsa Bloodstone has worn a few different trenchcoats over the years, but I went with the one from her appearances in Nextwave, a warm grey coat with a black collar. I started with a solid coat of Reaper Mountain Stone, followed by a heavy drybrush in Reaper Wolf Grey. I did the coat before the belt and boots so I wouldn’t have to worry about touching up the belt if all this drybrushing got out of control.

Finally, I very carefully painted in the collar with Army Painter Speedpaint Grim Black, in order to darken it to black but still retain a little of the shading created by the drybrushing.

Boots, Gloves & Belt

With the Deep Grey highlights over the black primer, the boots, gloves and belt were basically done, but I wanted them to have a slightly bluish tint to them, so I gave them a light drybrush in Reaper HD Nightsky Blue, a great dark blue shade that is unfortunately long since discontinued. I followed that up by brushing on some edge highlights in Reaper Snow Shadow, a light blue. I liked the shading, but the overall tone was far too light, so I did a wash over the blue areas in Army Painter Dark Blue Tone – this gave me the blue-black tint I wanted, and also served to soften the light blue highlights a bit.

Shirt & Pants

Working with orange can be difficult, and it was here that I began to question my decision to prime this model in black. It took several coats of Army Painter Burning Ore, but eventually I got a good base of orange, which I then hit with an Army Painter Sepia Tone wash to get some shadow tones. This worked okay on her shirt, but it went on a little blotchy over the legs, which have a lot less texture for the wash to settle into.

After the wash had thoroughly dried, I touched up the areas I didn’t like with the Burning Ore mixed with a bit of Army Painter Brigadine Brown. Finally, I added some highlights using Army Painter Lava Orange.

Skin & Hair

I wanted Elsa’s hair to be a different orange than her shirt and pants, so I used Reaper HD Burning Orange, another discontinued color – I’m going to be sad when I run out of this; it’s a great dark orange that I’ve never been able to find a good replacement for. With the Burning Orange setting the midtone, I did a wash with Army Painter Strong Tone, and once that had dried, I brushed in a few highlights with Reaper Highlight Orange.

I’ve gone back and forth on different ways to do skin before finally settling on this simple two-color technique. For all the exposed skin areas, I started with a base coat of Army Painter Topaz Skin, then brushed on highlights in Army Painter Tourmaline Skin. You can get a fairly effective range of facial tones with just a few quick brushstrokes, hitting the forehead, bridge of the nose, cheekbones, upper lip, chin, and jawline – almost the entire face, but leaving the darker skin tone to show through at the lips and under the nose, eyes and cheekbones.

Painting and photo by Jefferson Powers.

Finishing Touches

I was so focused on all the other details that I nearly forgot to do the rifles, but they were pretty straightforward: Army Painter Gun Metal and Dark Tone wash for the metal bits, Leather Brown for the wood stocks, and Tomb King Tan for the wrappings around the stock, and a final Strong Tone wash to fill in the details.

After that, I did a very light drybrush of Army Painter Ancient Stone over the whole model (including the dirt areas on the base) to add some additional highlights. Then I went over the edge of the base in Army Painter Matt Black.

After giving everything a day to dry, I added a coat of Army Painter Matt Varnish, thinned with a little water. I find that this varnish tends to dry with more of a semi-gloss/satin finish, so the next day I went over the model with what has become my go-to final varnish: a 60/40 mix of AK Interactive Ultra Matte varnish and Army Painter Speedpaint Medium. It sounds a little strange, and I’m not sure why it works, but I find that the AK Ultra Matte tends to be a little too chalky on its own, and cutting it with Speedpaint Medium does something magical that gives me a perfect matte finish.

And that’s Elsa Bloodstone done and ready for the table. I’ve got Spectrum (AKA Monica Rambeau) coming up in my painting queue, which means I’m that much closer to having the full Nextwave team. Come on AMG, where are Machine Man, Boom Boom and The Captain?
