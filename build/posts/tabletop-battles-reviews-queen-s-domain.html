Tabletop Battles Reviews: Queen's Domain

November 4th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Saying that Dark Souls casts a long shadow over the entire action-RPG subgenre is somehow both a completely true statement while also wildly underselling it. The game has become a cultural phenomenon since its 2011 release, and the staggering popularity of Dark Souls has spawned a new wave of interest and homage to its spiritual predecessor, the first-person dungeon crawler King’s Field. In the last few years, games like Lunacid, Dread Delusion, and Labyrinth of the Demon king have put their own spin on the King’s Field formula, riding high on the popularity of “retro” style indie releases that aim to emulate the look and feel of late-PlayStation era games.

This new wave of dungeon crawlers, or “field-like” games, all face the same problem. In a subgenre designed specifically to emulate another game, how do you step out of the shadow of King’s Field? How do you go from being simply a love letter to something that stands tall on its own merit, especially now that this renaissance of retro dungeon crawlers becomes crowded with new titles?

Rather than trying to stand out by subverting the genre and adding some new twist to the gameplay formula, Queen’s Domain instead takes the legacy of King’s Field head on, to the point where even the name Queen’s Domain is an unsubtle play on words. For the small team at Freshly Baked Games, success lied in both fully embracing what made the King’s Field games a cult classic before Dark Souls put From Software on the map, while also innovating and evolving on the dungeon crawler formula in ways that feel decidedly modern.

In other words, Queen’s Domain’s plan to escape the shadow of King’s Field is to simply make a better King’s Field. Not only does the game succeed, Queen’s Domain knocks it out of the park.

Credit: Freshly Baked Games

Stranger in a Strange Land

Fittingly for its retro gaming stylings, Queen’s Domain is light on story, with the only sizable chunk of direct storytelling happening in the game’s intro cutscene. It's a pretty generic fantasy adventure setup, the evil Queen has returned from beyond the grave and is gathering an army of evil on the mysterious island of Neasied. The King sent out an expedition to stop her, but hasn’t been heard from in some time. You play as Prince Berium, who is leading a second expedition to find out what happened to the King and, if needed, finish the job he started.  As you approach the island however, the Queen summons a terrible storm that claims your ship and scatters your crew.

After that, the game kicks off proper as you awake on the shores of Neasied, without a weapon or plan to speak of, and take your first look around the island. Queen’s Domain’s environments are fantastically realized, and range from some traditional mysterious ruins and near pitch-black dungeons, to stranger stuff like esoteric arcane forests and a few late game areas that are a complete left-turn. While all of these areas are unique visually from one another, the one thing that they all have in common is that they’re all dripping with atmosphere.

Credit: Freshly Baked Games

Queen’s Domain nails the feeling of mystery and dread that comes with exploring Neasied by often using the limitations of the PlayStation era graphics to its advantage. The lower resolution obscures details and intensifies the darkness, and it’s often difficult to discern details from a long distance. True old school gaming sickos can even stick with the default “retro” filter applied to closer represent the resolution found on older screens, but personally I found this setting to muddy the visuals a bit too much, and considering how well executed those environments are I wanted to ensure I was getting a clear look at them.

The strong visual atmosphere is coupled with a fantastic sound design. Queen’s Domain is a quiet game, its soundscape never really becomes bombastic, but it's never quiet. There's always the sound of something, like your own footsteps or shuffling lizards, alongside the groans and hisses of monsters as they wander the world. The whole effect works best with headphones, since you often hear enemies before you see them you’ll keep a sharp ear for any sound that's out of place. A few times I was even stopped cold in my tracks, ready for a fight, only to discover the noise was just a rat scuttling underfoot.

I also nearly leapt six feet from my chair the first time I heard the truly awful screeching sound of this game’s treasure lizards, so Queen’s Domain knows when to get loud too.

The synth-heavy ambient soundtrack keeps to the background during the games runtime, rarely ever asserting itself to really dominate the scene, but acting as a real workhorse to bring the whole mood together. Earlier areas in the island sun tend to be more mysterious in tone, while the sounds for later underground bits elevate the danger by upping the tempo. It’s hard to say that there’s any standout tracks here, aside from song that plays when you go to the shop that heavily features duck quacks, but every single music piece for each area accentuates the mood and improves immersion.

Credit: Freshly Baked Games

Flash of the Blade

After washing up the beach and wandering up the shore a bit, you run into a few of your companions from the boat who provide you a weapon, a rusty blade, and point you in the direction of the king, who went through some big tomb doors that they haven’t any idea how to enter. Their company is short-lived, and by the time I broke down the nearby barrier to unlock my first saving crystal, the knights had mysteriously died. With rusty sword in hand, it was time to confront whatever monsters awaited for me on this bizarre island.

Combat in Queen's Domain at the most basic level is basically the exact same as it was in King’s Field, though very early on the game provides you with some extra tools that add some fun twists to the formula. At its core, combat is much more deliberate than most modern games and centers around your stamina bar, which empties with every attack before refilling. Each attack with your weapon is a commitment, and like its spiritual predecessor, attacks only do appreciable damage when the stamina bar is full. Spamming attacks with an empty bar will only succeed in tickling a monster. For maximum damage, attacks can be held and charged as well. Since there is no dodge roll or block to speak of, managing your space by constantly moving and baiting out attacks before punishing them is key.

Credit: Freshly Baked Games

It's a glacial system by modern standards, but Queen’s Domain does have a few tricks up its sleeve to make the combat more engaging. Each one of the over fifty weapons has a secondary attack alongside their primary attack, which can range from a lunging strike in the case of the starting sword to a magical attack that charges up as you deal damage. You quickly find a magical dagger belt fairly early on as well, which gives you access to ranged attacks. Ranged weapon option includes a variety of throwing daggers, chakrams, and darts that have a limited number of uses but don’t cost stamina, instead slowly recharging over time, making them great for pulling enemies away from groups or keeping up damage while your stamina refills. Each weapon also has a damage type, and every enemy has resistances and weakness to a certain types of damage, making weapon swapping between one of your three equipped weapons key in any given fight.

Even with all of these tools, rushing headlong into fights is a pretty bad idea. Healing items don’t replenish, and dying doesn’t send you back to a checkpoint but is instead a hard game over, forcing you to reload your last save at one of the aforementioned saving crystals. These are plentiful around the island, but they're also the only place you can save, so an unexpected death can wipe a big chunk of progress.

Combine all of this together, and it creates a combat loop that feels less like an Elder Scrolls game and more like a survival horror title. You need to mind your space and make sure you’re using the right tool for the job, all while ensuring you do everything you can to avoid getting hit and draining your precious supply of healing items. It makes combat against multiple enemies a delightfully tense affair, as you’re constantly switching between your weapons and toolset while kiting along the monsters that pursue you. Especially with the huge roster of weapons and monsters with different moves and abilities, including some truly out there ones later on in the game, combat remains tense and engaging throughout the whole game.

Credit: Freshly Baked Games

The Magic of Discovery

The combat is good, probably the most solid system out of any of the new wave of Field-like games, but where Queen’s Domain truly sings is in its exploration, which rewards experimentation and curiosity like very few titles do. Queen’s Domain is filled to nearly bursting with puzzles and secrets, and rarely uses any part of it’s map as set dressing. Neasied itself feels like the best combination possible of a great Metroidvania game for its upgrade hunting and secrets and the original Dark Souls with its interconnected map and player freedom. There truly is something remarkable around nearly every turn.

Exploration is good at the start, but it blows wide open when you acquire the Holy Sword Endu after the first boss. Endu has an air dash ability that can be used no matter what weapon you have equipped, and can be upgraded with wind gems to gain access to additional dashes. Suddenly, multiple places on the map that were inaccessible before open up, and every gap becomes a question of “wait, can I make that?” I found myself not only trying to make jumps and cross gaps that felt impossible in other titles, but almost every single time was rewarded for doing so with an item, chest, or upgrade. And your suite of tools for exploration only gets larger as the game goes on, encouraging the player to double back on old zones to grab items they could only glimpse the first time around. Impressively, I never felt frustrated when I backtracked to grab these upgrades. Usually, I was rushing back with barely contained excitement, eager to test if what I’d just discovered is exactly what I’m missing to grab that item, solve that puzzle, or open that chest I couldn’t before.

An early example of this came when I explored an area I’d previously been through before to farm some healing items, when I noticed a ramp I hadn’t before. Climbing up, I saw that hey, the elevation is about level with a platform on the other side of the clearing, which I couldn’t figure out how to reach before. It looked like a pretty decent gap, but with the third air dash I’d just unlocked, it was suddenly very doable, leading to a short climb up to a statue I had seen a few times before that I couldn’t interact with. After seeing several of these things, I decided the must be important, but there wasn’t a clear answer on how to interact with them. After trying a few different things, I found that my magical torch lit the statue’s ablaze, and I nearly jumped out of my seat when I remembered I’d seen a door earlier in my adventure that said I needed to light three statues to pass through. It’s one of dozens of examples of amazing discovery throughout the playthrough, and it made every moment of exploring this strange island a delight.

Credit: Freshly Baked Games

All Hail the Queen

Queen’s Domain isn’t just a solid homage to the King’s Field series that came before it, this game takes the lessons to its predecessor and expands and innovates on them in nearly every single way into a game that feels modern, refreshing, and exciting while remaining wonderfully retro. It deserves to be considered on every single one of its own merits, and it's an absolute triumph of a game. I haven’t felt this sort of wonder and immersion while exploring a game world since Elden Ring, and its hard to overstate how impressive it is that this game, which was made by a team of just two people, has a world that stands toe to toe with one of the most critically acclaimed games of the decade. Queen’s Domain isn’t just my game of the year, it’s one of my favorite games that I’ve played in multiple years. Whether you’re a fan of the King’s Field series that inspired it or just someone who loves a grand video game adventure, I highly recommend playing Queen’s Domain, which feels destined to go down as a gaming classic.
