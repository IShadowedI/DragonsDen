Tabletop Battles Reviews Cyberpunk Edgerunners 2

October 20th, 2026 via Goonhammer

This review contains no spoilers, and talks broadly about the plot and themes of the show without providing specifics. If you want more in-depth information, well, stay tuned choom. 

Cyberpunk Edgerunners 2 opens with a broad gambit: what if a sequel can actually be better than the original? There's some precedent for it, certainly, but sequels tend to struggle with the legacy of what came before them, and in Edgerunners 2's case, that is a pretty impressive wall to climb. But if I told you that Edgerunners 2 only wanted to be better than the first series, I think it would be doing both of them a disservice. Perhaps to get the controversial out of the way in the intro: if you are a fan of cyberpunk themes, Cyberpunk the game, and also the original series, then Cyberpunk Edgerunners 2 is an easy sell. But exactly why takes a bit more discussion and delving into what the sequel is wanting to do and what makes it successful, and perhaps if you've been on the fence or are curious, this review can give some push to giving the new series a try.

Last Chance for a Slow Dance

Edgerunners 2's primary protagonist is a young boy named Roman, whose obsession with film serves as both a framing and thematic device for the entire series. Roman is obsessed with the concept of film being a superior form of media than Braindances, a lost art form that can connect the viewer and the creator through the forced disconnect of the camera. The show regularly utilizes a filter to create a retro film grain effect, as well as sound mixing to make things sound like one might expect when recorded on a handheld camera. Roman wants to be a director, a creator, someone who proves the value of film in a world that sees it as nostalgic entertainment at best.

That runs paralell to a bigger theme in the second season, that of humanity in a world filled with artifice and implants. Of the 4 protagonists at the center of Edgerunners, Roman is the only one who has minimal implants and chrome: King is an older Edgerunner who faces down the reality of life after cyberpsychosis, Talia is an burgeoning implant addict, and the mysterious D seems more than happy to use hacking implants and more to get to the bottom of his revenge plot. All of these characters end up circling each other through fate, happenstance, and of course, The Narrative, but the one who remains slightly at a distance and slowly sucked into the center of it is Roman.

Early in the series, Roman's love of film but need to live gets him tangled up in the web of the others, and the series continually makes an interesting contrast between Roman the person and Roman the director; forced to actually take part in what he's recording, Roman's point of view to the audience shifts from watching his actions and watching his camera. Much like the artificial world of hacking and implants, Roman finds himself also having to separate himself from viewing and acting.

10:15 On a Saturday Night

Edgerunners 2's major theme, as perhaps rote as it might sound, is humanity and the ties that bind. The opening song, 10:15 Saturday Night by the Cure, is accompanied by a flat color montage of the characters and world that we're going to view, black, white, and big flashy blocky colors fly at us. Director Kai Ikarashi absolutely understood the assignment, because one of the first impulses I had while watching the show is that it reminded me of Cowboy Bebop: grainy, colorful, and combining flat and dynamic aesthetics in a way that is both "Cyberpunk" while doing something original.

Like Bebop, and perhaps unlike Edgerunners, Edgerunners 2 is an ensemble cast story that doesn't immediately rush to push the characters together; by the midpoint of the series the characters have all started to enter closer and closer orbit with one another, inevitably drawn together because they have to be, and it is perhaps one of the things that I think will instantly turn off people who are looking specifically for Edgerunners "Again". Ikarashi and writer Bartosz Sztybor have done an interesting job of ensuring that this series is not just a referendum on the first, nor is it doing the same things again, while also dropping small hints, narrative concepts, and webs of connection that do tie the two of them together; after all, this all does take place in the same setting and city.

The show plays with the concept of film, storytelling, and connections as a driving narrative factor, while also heavily playing with musical layers. Every episode is a song title, tied to the themes of the show and the episodes events, and across the 10 episodes of season 2 there's a deep investment in those connections tying back to the concept of what makes us human, or just what it means to live, be remembered, and remembering. Roman is obsessed with filming the world around him, Talia wants to be the version of herself she feels only she understands, King lingers in his obsessions with the past and its consequences, and D wants revenge in the memory of others. Without trying to compare the two, I found Edgerunners 2's cast far more dynamic than the first series; while David, Rebecca and Lucy are all fun and amazing characters, their story is an entirely different examination of the Cyberpunk setting, and in 2, we have one that even the game itself struggles with: living.

Then Everyone Went Home

Living isn't something the Cyberpunk universe has always done a fantastic job of exploring, and in a lot of broader senses, something that cyberpunk media doesn't always engage with either. We certainly get numerous novels, movies, games, comics, and more that conceptualize the dystopian worlds of cyberpunk universes, often filled with wet dreams of tech run amok and evil corporations, but they don't often build a lot of grounding in what living is actually like; if one were to go by Edgerunners or Cyberpunk 2077, the answer apparently is that one lives in total misery until your life is summarily ended with a bang or a splat.

Edgerunners 2 takes the somewhat initial lower stakes and point of view to show that, well, people have to actually live in this universe, and that life is not as pointless, brutish, and short as it may seem otherwise. Roman lives with his father, goes shopping for groceries, and tries to make a living with his films; King, despite his Edgerunning, has connections to people and relationships, while D is someone whose loss of these things makes him driven to exact revenge.

Talia is perhaps the most interesting character, a woman obsessed with her pursuit of transhuman perfection; it is pretty difficult to not read Talia's struggles with identity and her pursuits as a trans allegory, with the reminder that the Cyberpunk universe has had some... spotty history with that concept. In Edgerunners 2, at least, Talia's struggle is put forward as one of a person who wishes to be free to be the person they believe they want to be, and at times the allegory works and at others it doesn't; remember, Cyberpunk is a world in which Cyberpsychosis is real, which does mute some of her zeal for changing her body with the idea that it will literally kill her, eventually. Still, she's an interesting and frustrating character, the type that you want to both yell at and support with how she acts, in the way that good but flawed characters often do.

There are more characters, revealed in the midpoint, that shift the story into new directions, but I'll avoid revealing them except to say that one of them is an abominable little creature that also is very entertaining, and another that allows you to really feel the Studio Trigger energy from their character design, if you're familiar with their wider body of work. By the end, I was vastly in love with this cast, and while the finality of the first series made it feel like a done and settled affair, I wouldn't mind seeing more of these characters again, but I can also deeply respect the story that is told.

Give Me Another Day

The animation of Edgerunners 2 is some of Trigger's best, bar none; if anything, watching the show for the animation and cinematography alone is a treat. I've had a pretty mixed reception to many of Trigger's works, and the legacy that preceded them in places like Studio Gainax, but one thing I've always held respect for is that Trigger knows they are working in the realm of cartoons. While Edgerunners 2 tells a grounded story in the Cyberpunk universe, it isn't afraid to get cartoonish, particularly with the above mentioned character.

Even aside from them, there's fun uses of wipes, smears, and forced perspective animation that shows off some of the reasons why animation can do things that live action camerawork just is not capable of. At the end of episode one, there's a sequence of fluid monochromatic animation that feels electric to watch, and later episodes produce eye-pleasing action sequences; one that I particularly enjoyed is an almost comical segment in which disembodied hands hold all manner of guns out of a car window while an endless stream of palm-trees scrolls by in the background on a flat color backdrop; the combination of cartoonish comedy of it with the action happening just works together to create an engaging sequence, which the series continues to replicate.

Ikarashi is primarily known as a key animator, having only directed two works so far in his career, with Edgerunners 2 being the second (the first, Heavenly Delusion, actually has a lot in common with the type of style and stories he likes to tell). But this is a man whose animation credits are on the likes of SSSS. Gridman, Promare, and Mob Psycho 100, which is to say that his pedigree for style and animation tricks certainly lives up to that pedigree. D's flashbacks and nightmares are presented in striking primary flat colors, the film grain on Roman's camera feels deeply creative and well researched, and the majority of the series has that realistic, grounded feeling that the best animation creates: that even though these are not real actors in real places, they still feel and act human.

Edgerunners 2 is a triumph in a lot of ways: it has amazing animation, a great story, good characters, and a banging soundtrack to accompany it, but it also manages to live up to the expectation of a sequel to Edgerunners. And frankly, if you just want more of David, Rebecca, and Lucy, may I suggest you watch the cutscenes of the Wuthering Waves collaboration instead? Because they were also written by the main author and serve as a fascinating epilogue from Lucy's point of view, and perhaps if that is all you wanted, will suffice. But if you're looking for a great exploration of what it means to live and exist in the world of Cyberpunk, of what it means to be a human, of connection, film, and stories, then Edgerunners 2 is where it's at, choomfie.
