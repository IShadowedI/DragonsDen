The Paint Review: Monument Hobbies ProAcryl 1-Step Review

May 18th, 2026 via Goonhammer
 

Monument Hobbies is the latest paint manufacturer to enter the Contrast-Style Paint market, debuting their offering at this year’s Adepticon to wild enthusiasm, but how does their new 1-Step paint measure up?

Thank you to Monument Hobbies for providing a set of these paints to Keewa for review; Kevin purchased his own.

Contemptor Kevin: A few days before Adepticon, I looked at a random Monument Hobbies ad on Facebook and asked Keewa whether or not Monument was debuting new paints at Adepticon. He told me yes: Monument Hobbies 1-Step Paints! I was able to acquire a set, including the exclusive “Rust” paint and the Secret Sauce. I have had the chance to use a bunch of them and been impressed. I used some via brush, others through airbrush, and a few through both.  

Keewa: Up front, I must admit to being a bit of a ProAcryl fanboy. I use their paints extensively as my go-tos, both in my professional and personal painting, so as with all my reviews, I’m determined to be as objective as possible, but you might want to bear that fact in mind if I seem too effusive with praise or whatever.

The fellows at Monument kindly sent me the 1-Step starter set of 12 paints. The set doesn’t include the Secret Sauce medium (Kevin has some of that, though - so he’ll fill you in on how it handles), but it will release at the same time as the starter. Once I get my hands on that, I imagine that I shall become unstoppable, or at the very least, marginally less stoppable.

To start with, I got some nice watercolour paper and made some swatches. I like to do this when I review paints. Obviously, these paints are not intended for use on paper, but it’s useful to get a sense of how they handle, their opacity, and their viscosity. To start with, I put down a sample of each 1-Step paint and feathered it out with a wet brush to make a gradient from opaque to transparent. With contrast-type paints, this is essential because if I’d just put down a blob of the raw paint, it wouldn’t show the gamut of tones available; you’d only see the darkest shadow tone.

Following on from that, if ProAcryl had a standard paint with the same name, I painted a sample of that to see how close the colour matching was, and if no paint with the same name existed, I tried to find the closest in the standard range. Finally, I tried to find out which Army Painter Speedpaint is most similar to each colour, which wasn’t easy! 



From my swatches, it’s apparent right away that only some of the same-name paints are close to their ProAcryl equivalents - Blue, Warm Yellow, Shadow Flesh, and Royal Purple are all very close or an exact match for their regular ProAcryl equivalents, but Dark Green Brown and Orange are way out, they’re not really even close, Warm Brown also looks weird on paper, but on plastic it does look quite similar.

Crimson is closest to Burgundy from the regular paints, while Orange is much, much closer to the Orange Red than its namesake. There isn’t a “Dark Green” in the normal range; Black Green is closest, but is a bit more yellow-y in the transparent end. Graphite Green is a beautiful semi-desaturated blue-green colour that I really like; it doesn’t have much of an equivalent in the general range, though. Dark Blue Black is exactly what it says on the tin, it’s like Blue Black, but darker, can’t really argue with that. Over on the dullest end of the spectrum, Graphite Grey is closest to Dark Neutral Grey.

For speedpaint equivalents, I had a difficult time nailing some of them down. 

Colours

Dark Black Blue

Contemptor Kevin: The standout paint from this box, in my opinion. “Dark Black Blue” is what it says on the tin: It is an extremely dark blue color that can pass as black. With some moderate thinning, it will appear more blue than black, and a more saturated blue than something like Leviadon Blue (Citadel) or Cloudburst Blue (Speed Paint). I’ve used this one extensively, as the armor color for my Jim Raynor mini (through the airbrush), and on my Orks for their cloth. The balance of the tint and saturation has the paint read as black, but can have a shadow added to it quite easily (via Black Templar or Coal Black or something).

Keewa: I like this one too, I agree with Kevin’s assessment that it’s a bit like Cloudburst Blue, which I use to shade all sorts of things.  

Crimson

Contemptor Kevin: Purity seal color. It’s technically a red, but with a heavy purple tint.  In order to get a decently vibrant red from this line of paint, I would start with a thin coat of Crimson and then paint my way up through orange.

Orange

Contemptor Kevin: A bright, vibrant orange. 

Keewa: I take issue with this paint being called “Orange” when it’s more red than orange, I guess you could call it Hot Orange.

Shadow Flesh

Contemptor Kevin: Somewhere between a pink and an orange, probably closer to pink. Over an off-white, this can lay down a warm tone that is common to all types of living flesh, from caucasian to african to orkoid.  

Dark Green

Contemptor Kevin: A saturated green, similar in tone to GW’s Ork Flesh Contrast. For me, this layered well over the Shadow Flesh undertone to give my cartoony Nob a vibrant Ork panache.  

Blue

Contemptor Kevin: It’s a nice, vivid blue, and it is a very close match for the regular ProAcryl Blue. It is also an extremely close match between GW’s Talassar Blue and Army Painter’s Magic Blue SpeedPaints.  

[caption id="attachment_242431" align="aligncenter" width="1024"] (Top Left: Magic Blue [AP].  Top Right: Talassar Blue [GW].  Bottom Right: Blue [PA])[/caption]

Royal Purple

Contemptor Kevin: A rich, vibrant, and deep purple. Analogous to the Royal Purple in the regular Pro-Acryl line, but now theoretically available to use as a contrast-style paint. This will wind up in a lot of my underpainting and shadows.  

Warm Yellow

Contemptor Kevin: What it says on the tin: It’s a bright yellow but not a cold yellow (like Pale Yellow) or ochre yellow (like Averland Sunset). I personally find the regular Warm Yellow paint to have weak coverage, so having it as a heavily-pigmented contrast means I get to keep most of what I like about it (the pigment) but with a medium I prefer.  

Keewa: Kevin is right about the regular Warm Yellow, it hardly covers anything at all - it’s a yellow after all, but because it doesn’t have any white or black pigment in it, you can really tell, it’s almost transparent. I like Warm Yellow, don’t get me wrong, and the 1-Step version of it is going to get a lot of use in my arsenal for things like painting lamps, fire, cheap OSL, Bad Moons etc.

Graphite Grey

Contemptor Kevin: Graphite Grey is a midtone grey. This one is going to be fantastic for quickly adding neutral shades to whites (when heavily thinned down) or for doing quick non-metallic metals for a cartoony army. It’s a fair bit brighter than the GW mainstay Basillicanum Grey. 

Keewa: Quite a light grey, really, I didn’t really like this one - it’s a bit too pale and too weak to be super useful. I would rather have had some kind of black in the set.

Graphite Green

Keewa: I really like this colour, it’s almost a ghostly desaturated blue-green that’s in the same sort of area of the spectrum as Nighthaunt Gloom, I’m sure I’ll get plenty of use out of this one.

Dark Green Brown

Contemptor Kevin: Probably the most unusual of the 1 Step contrasts, this color falls into a similar category to such paints as Ratling Grime or Gaghrak Sewer from the second Contrast wave. It offers an organic grime color, and one I’m sorry I did not have a lot of time to play around with more.  

Keewa: really nothing like the Dark Green Brown in the main line, this one is both grimy and naturalistic, it’ll be useful to paint drab army uniforms, add streaks of grunge to tanks, and even as a super-natural ork skin.

Warm Brown

Contemptor Kevin: A brown softer than GW’s Snakebite Leather Contrast, this one creates a rich leather tone when laid over a light brown or a tan.  

Keewa: Although it didn’t work so well on my paper-swatch, in practice there’s tons of punch in this one. I’d call it a kind of Gore-Grunta Fur analogue, it’s got that orangey flavour with a really deep saturation, letting you think it out into an orange-brown glaze.

Rust

Contemptor Kevin: Apparently, this one was an Adepticon exclusive paint. Rust is a reddish-orange which, when it dries, does indeed look more orange and thus rusty over a 1-Step Graphite Grey. If you did want to use it for rust, you would probably then want to edge highlight it with a more vibrant orange to bring out the rust. Rust also works well as a grimy off-red. I used it for some ankle-bands on my Nob to show that while they were once red to help him run faster, they have since picked up enough grit and grime on whatever planet he’s on to have lost the initial luster.

Keewa: Rust is one I would really have liked to have as well for weathering purposes, damn! 

How They Paint



Contemptor Kevin: I used these paints in a bunch of different ways. First, I took an Ork Nob I primed in Wraithbone years ago and took the entire line to him for brushwork. The paints worked identically to those of Citadel’s Contrast, save for having dropper bottles onto a Servo-Skull silicon pallet. Over the off-white, we got a nice, bright cartoony look, as to be expected.

While painting a complex model such as an Ork, we can see the strengths of this set, which is bold colors and easy mixing and layering to change tones. We only had one grey, so layering the grey over the Blue or Dark Blue Black works to give us a different looking gunmetal versus axe metal. Likewise, with Warm Brown I mixed in some Shadow Flesh to addd variation. We can also see the immediate weakness of the set: there are some missing colors. In my opinion, the main “missing” color was a good bone color. While Dark Green Brown mixed with Warm Brown (and heavily thinned) was able to give a decent enough approximation of the color on the wraps and horns, I’m not sure it would have worked quite as well on something that did not have a lot of green, like an Ork.  



These paints flow well through an airbrush: I used Dark Blue Black on my Jim Raynor figure over a zenithal highlight. I probably should have thinned it a bit more than I did to keep more of the blue tone showing through, but it still flowed nicely. I used the yellow, orange, and crimson for the Point Defense Drone’s stand/rocket burst, and it created an easy flame effect.  

Credit: Contemptor Kevin Stillman

They don’t reactivate when you layer them on top of each other, if anyone was concerned.  

That being said, they’re still contrast paints. For all the selling points and persistent marketing as a “one-step solution” to getting an army ready, they are still tricky to use over bright, smooth basecoats without suffering from tea staining. I’ve found them most effective for use as a filter: You paint the shadows, midtone, and very bright highlights, then use a filter of 1-Step over them. This flattens to the bright highlights closer to the midtone, and lets the excellent color choice for 1-Step carry the day. 

Keewa: For my tests, I got four old Ork boys in various states of paintedness, slapped a zenithal prime on them and spent some time trying to get to grips with the ProAcryl 1-Step.

I’m not a particularly adept “Contrast Painter”, I do use various contrast-speedpaint-type paints in my work, but usually as airbrush filters, targeted washes, or liners, rather than the intended “slap it on and let it do its thing” approach. That’s just me hedging, I know, getting my excuses in early when you see how shonky the work is, I have that much self-awareness.



Ork 1 got a nice covering of Graphite Green for his skin, this colour, man, I love it.



Ork 2 gets some Dark Green Brown for his skin-tone, this is a much more naturalistic choice for Ork skin.



Ork 3 gets Dark Green as his skin tone - this is the punchiest and most saturated of the greens.



Ork 4 is a wild-card, he gets all the colours that don’t really fit the other schemes, in this case I mixed Dark Green Brown and Shadow Flesh to make a sort of human-esque tone.



Ork 2 gets some nice brown trousers. Warm Brown is a really rich colour and here it made a nice smooth gradient over a zenithal prime.



For Ork 1’s trousers I used a straightforward single coat of Dark Blue Black, and for his vest I used a mix of Dark Blue Black and Warm Brown to create this kind of really dark brown colour.



Since Ork 3 is vibrant in his greenness, I painted his t-shirt with a single coat of Orange - the orange is really really punchy, his bright blue trousers are similarly just one coat of Blue.



Our Wildcard, Ork 4 gets a Crimson vest, yellow armour plates, and purple trousers. 



To test how the 1-Step goes over metallic paints, I quickly blocked out the metal bits on the orks with ProAcryl Silver and messed around. On Ork 1’s axe, the Dark Blue Black and Blue covered nicely over the metal to give it a cold look.



The 1-Step paints mix quite well. Using Shadow Flesh and Dark Green Brown, I was able to make a decent bone-shade for this skull.

I warned you that I wasn’t a contrast adept, didn’t I? Ok, so the models aren’t very good, but what does that mean for the paints? 



They flow smoothly from the brush and have a much more generous working time than the Army Painter equivalent, so if you move smartly, you can apply a coat of 1-Step, void your brush, then with it damp (not wet), you can move the paint around to reveal highlights and push more paint into the shadows. They also dry to a nice matt finish like the rest of the ProAcryl paints, bonus! I found it really handy to have a nice white (like Bold Titanium White) on hand to fix any mistakes. 

As an irredeemable airbrush-botherer, I have, of course, used the 1-step paints through the airbrush, and I’m delighted to report that they work very, very well. The flowing characteristics of the 1-Step formula mean that it sails through the airbrush with ease, without a clog to be seen. The transparent nature of the paint makes it absolutely perfect for applying filters to blend transitions. I used some of the 1-Step Blue to glaze the transitions here on this blue shield and draping fabric.

It’s not all sunshine and rainbows, however; I must temper my praise with a bit of frustration - the bubbles, oh god the bubbles. Bubbles are something of a bane when you use these paints; they have a very annoying tendency to froth up like they’re based on a medium of washing-up liquid and hand soap. When that happens, you need to be very, very vigilant and make sure you either puff them out, tap your miniature on your desk, or pop them with your brush - otherwise you’re going to get some weird craters on your model as the surface dries before the bubble has popped fully.

Secret Sauce

Left: Stormcast painted with Royal Purple mixed with Secret Sauce.  Right: Stormcast painted with 1-Step Royal Purple

Contemptor Kevin: My three favorite ProAcryl Paints are, in order: 1) Payne’s Grey, 2) Bold Titanium White, and 3) Royal Purple. So when the time came to test the Secret Sauce medium, I picked Royal Purple because I could give it a direct test to the pre-mixed 1-Step Paint.  

And what do you know, the stuff actually does exactly what it says on the tin. It took a thick ProAcryl paint and turned it into a contrast paint! But comparing it to the pre-mixed 1-Step Royal Purple, we can see the advantages of the pre-mixed version. Getting the ratio of Secret Sauce to paint requires experimentation. My back-of-hand estimates were that in order to get a good contrast effect, you’d need 3 parts Secret Sauce to 1 part paint. I don’t know if that holds true for all paints, and different paints may have different ratios. The problem is that if you add too much Secret Sauce, your paint will be too thin.  

I have not had time to try this as a thinning medium for use via the airbrush, but I do not see how that could possibly be a *bad* use of this stuff.  

Keewa: Glaze and Wash medium is already a really good choice for airbrush-thinning (when combined with plenty of water), but I’m excited to see what Secret Sauce can do when it comes to making really transparent filters through the airbrush!
