Tabletop Battles Reviews: Whiskerwood

November 13th, 2026 via Goonhammer

NOTE: This game is currently in Early Access, and this review deals only with the state of the game as it exists in late 2026; when the game is released into 1.0, we may revisit this review.

I’ve previously talked about my abiding love of city-building games in my review of Timberborn, and I am an absolute hog when it comes to games about assembling an increasingly complicated metropolis. In a few ways, Whiskerwood and Timberborn have a lot in common - a citybuilder in a voxel environment with anthropomorphised animal pops, plus verticality and stacking buildings - but while Timberborn is set in the dawning of a post-humanity apocalypse, Whiskerwood takes place in a quasi-17th century world, and while the beavers of Timberborn appear to be entirely alone and isolated in their settlements, masters of all they survey, in Whiskerwood, your “Whiskers” (Mice) are decidedly not.

For you see, the Whiskers of Whiskerwood are colonists, yes, but they are subalterns (in the Gramscian sense) of a greater empire, and, as well as being town planner and resource manager, the player in their role as the chief of these Whiskers (the “Tail”, i.e, you) must function as a sort of intermediary between the oppressed Whiskers and their oppressors, the “Claws” (Cats) and (to a lesser degree) the Weasels. The Claws function as a distant aristocratic overclass, while the Weasels tend towards seafaring renegades, pirates, traders, and smugglers.

From such humble beginnings...

You begin your colony by choosing one of three different starting states. Far Frontier is what you’d call the “standard” game; the state dispatches fewer loyalists, but smugglers demand more goods. Then there's Pirate Seas, which has more pirate ransom requests but tax ships are more infrequent and smuggled goods fetch better prices. Finally, in Monarch Loyalists, the state dispatches far more Monarchist whiskers, and they are willing to tolerate more hardship in general, but are fundamentally anti-industry.

With a scenario selected and a map generated, you can choose a virgin island (the size of which can be altered at the game’s start), abundant in some resources and lacking in others, in a local archipelago and from there, building out your colony the way you’d expect, beginning with access to primitive houses and gathering structures, with a limited get-started packet of resources, so far so normal, this is the ground floor of every colony sim, but what’s this? Two days from now, the Claws will send their envoy for your first.. tax payment? Yes, the cats want taxes from you, and they’re charging you for the supplies you arrived on the island with, plus the costs for the colonists’ passage.

Where the focus of many other citybuilders and colony sims lies on accumulating resources to make your pops happy, to survive the winter, and level up your buildings, in Whiskerwood you’re required to do that while also satisfying the ever-increasing material demands of your feline overlords. Your tax requirement starts on day two and continues every two days, with the exception of Winter, when the cats can’t be bothered to sail to your island (but don’t worry, they’ll add up the tax they don’t take in that time and take it as a lump sum from the survivors when Spring comes, thank-you-very-much).

The way taxes are calculated in Whiskerwood is laid out by the developer, and depends on five variables:

  • Population (a flat rate of 3 coins per day per Whisker)
  • Administrative (13 coins to begin with, increasing by 0.3 for every day survived past the first)
  • Travel (21 coins per colonist ship that turns up)
  • Production (10% of the value of all industrial production)
  • Supply (cost of goods accepted from the supply ship - more on that later).

So, in the absence of some mass die-off event (which can happen; winter is a thing after all), the tax bill is pretty much always going to climb day by day, visit by visit, becoming an increasingly difficult burden to meet as your colony grows.

The logic of colonialism has a high body count

Since the developers of Whiskerwood understand that the primary purpose of a colony in the eye of the parent state is primary sector resource extraction, that is the cats’ sole interest in your case too; although you’ll need to refine resources and manufacture goods and services to keep your whiskers happy and healthy, the cats have absolutely no interest in that nonsense and will not accept any of them as part of a tax payment; in fact, what the cats will accept doesn’t really change throughout the game (with a minor exception), they want logs, they want coal and ores, they want cotton and flax, they want raw fish and berries. Later in the game, when the naval aspect begins to kick off, the cats will add a desire for canvas sails and bronze nails to their list of acceptable payments. Everything else? No, thank you!

This adds an interesting rub to resource management in Whiskerwood. Yes, you need planks for anything more than the most basic construction, and you’ll definitely use them - but if you turn all your logs into the more useful planks, how will you pay your taxes? Do you use the cotton gin to turn your cotton into a superior fabric for making better clothing for your whiskers, or do you save the raw bales for the taxman?

A field to grow cotton

There is, however, a way to use your precious manufactured goods to pay your taxes - the Smuggler. Every once in a while, a smuggler ship will turn up at your dock and offer to buy your manufactured goods in exchange for gold bars, which the cats will accept in payment. Obviously, the smuggler’s prices are not generous (it’s a business, after all), but in a pinch it’s a good way to turn all that excess furniture you have into something you can use to fulfil your obligations to the Claws.

If you’re short on a tax payment, you can still submit it, but the cats’ opinion of you will decrease along a 5-point scale from “happy” to “hostile”, a completely skipped tax payment sees their opinion decrease by 2, a partial payment gives a penalty of 1, while overpaying tax nets a +1 opinion boost (plus a reward), and overpaying tax dramatically gives a +2 opinion boost plus a more generous reward. The rewards for excess tax payments are either one or two selections from a list of various manufactured goods, with different value based on how much you overpaid your taxes. What happens if your opinion slips too far? The tax ships that visit will open fire on your colony, bombarding it with cannonballs for a while before leaving. These bombardments will destroy buildings and pathways, injure, and even kill your Whiskers, making it even harder for your Whiskers to rebuild and be ready for the next tax ship (which is still coming in two days time!). Later in the game, when you’ve got more significant resources, you can construct walls to protect your town from the cannons (since they’re simulated via line of sight from the ship, a bombardment can’t hit a building the ship can’t see), which is also useful against pirates.

Every few days, a ship loaded with supplies will rock up at your main dock and offer you the choice of several packets of resources, typically manufactured goods. These aren’t gifts, though; the cost of whichever packet you select gets added to your next tax bill. You can, of course, refuse to purchase any supply packets, but if you do, you get an immediate -1 opinion malus with the cats for your impertinence. Critically, the Supply Ship and tax-overpayment rewards are the only way to acquire the Machinery resource, required both to build and maintain all factories, but which can’t be produced domestically until the late/end-game.

The Whisker Ship will also visit your dock and offer a range of new colonists you can take on by spending Approval points, either by selecting individuals or accepting the entire shipment at once. As the game is now, Whiskers don’t seem to reproduce, so this is the only way to replace colonists that died of cold, hunger, or bombardment.

Bold (and adorable) sentinels stand ready to face the pirate menace.

Occasionally, a pirate ship will turn up at your dock and demand a ransom, typically in the form of high-quality produced goods like tailored clothes and machinery. Failure to pay results in immediate bombardment by cannon, in the same way as the Claws bombardment. Later in the game, the player can construct anti-pirate defences, like towers armed with cannons that automatically attack approaching pirate ships, along with various quests that include immunity from pirate raids as a reward.

Whiskerwood has an Approval system, whereby the experiences of each Whisker are tallied at the end of each day and assigned a score. Positive experiences like a good meal, warm surroundings, a nice dwelling place, etc. accrue positive points, while unpleasant experiences like homelessness, hunger, unburied bodies, cold, pollution, whether their neighbour snores, bland food, worn-out clothes, an encounter with a rude Whisker, etc. impose a malus on approval. Each of these things is weighed against the expectations of each Whisker (denoted by a star rating on their character card - Whiskers with 1 or half a star will endure pretty severe hardships, eating simple raw fish and living in a hovel without complaining, while a highly skilled or educated Whisker with 4 or 5 stars won’t tolerate anything but the highest standard of living possible, requiring a stone-built house with furniture, bedding, and accessible amenities like a bathhouse and dining hall) and the outcome decides how many approval points you have to spend the next day. Approval points can be spent on a few things, from recruiting new Whiskers, asking workers to perform a double shift, sending a ship to sea, etc. Balancing Approval is one of the trickiest aspects of Whiskerwood; you can’t bank it, you get a fresh amount every day, and the previous day’s approval is lost. Having a high approval but not spending it is pointless, while having low or zero approval can significantly curtail your ability to act. For example, it’s the depths of winter, your Whiskers are all cold and hungry, but you can’t dispatch your Deep Sea Fishing vessel to bring in a large quantity of fish, because launching the ship costs 20 approval and you’ve not got any!

Summary of Day #523's production, consumption, approval, and taxes

To produce the end-game resources your high-status Whiskers crave, you’ll often need to manage considerably more complicated supply chains - for example, to produce a high-quality meal, you’ll need to catch fish, grow wheat, grow peppers, and grow mushrooms; you’ll then need to process that wheat into flour in a windmill, then turn that flour into bread in a bakery. You’ll need to combine the fish with fuel and mushrooms at a smokery to turn it into a fish skewer; you’ll also need to grind peppers into spices and combine all three things- bread, skewer, and spices at a Fine Kitchen building, staffed by a Craftmaster and two Educated Whiskers. To improve this production, you can add table salt as a catalyst, which is produced by mining rock salt and passing it through a sifting tower (which requires piped water from a pumping station). That’s a pretty complicated process to make a meal, but it’s what your higher-status Whiskers will demand!

Monarchist Whiskers are a particular pain in the arse, complaining about the presence of factories being an affront to the generosity of the Claws. Even among the oppressed, there are always those who love the oppressor.

Since this is a colony sim, your main enemy is always going to be Winter. Even though the islands look pretty warm, when the dark season rolls around, temperatures plunge into the single digits and Whiskers will start to sicken and die if they aren’t kept warm. To that end, you need to build bonfires, heating stoves, and later Steam Radiators to warm the surrounding environment and make life possible in the depths of the snowy winter (and also to prevent farm ground from freezing and being unavailable all winter), but each of these methods both uses fuel and generates pollution. Later-game heating like the aforementioned Steam Radiators generate significantly less pollution (and since the steam travels from the boiler to the radiator through pipes, it’s possible to have the pollution-emitting aspect further away from where people actually live) but have an increased material cost to build; you need a lot of copper available to build the boilers and pipes to run the radiators, and to unlock the technology in the first place. So in the earlier game, your Whiskers will either be freezing in winter or complaining bitterly about the pollution produced by the bonfires keeping them alive, typical.

The slice view enables you to excavate huge caverns for farming mushrooms and digging up ores and stone

Each Whisker is automatically assigned to a particular guild from a list of four: Miners, Engineers, Farmers, and Explorers - being assigned to a job that matches their guild affiliation gives an associated productivity boost, and having all workers in a particular workplace have the same guild affiliation adds another boost. In addition, each Whisker can have advanced vocational or educational traits enabling them to take on more advanced jobs. An Apprentice is a worker with a basic level of additional vocational education. In a lot of factories, the first worker must be an Apprentice, and if none are available, the factory won’t function at all. To begin with, the only way to get an Apprentice is if one turns up to your colony on a Whisker Ship, but once you have Whiskers with advanced educational traits, you can employ them at a School and, through doing so, give additional Whiskers their education trait or guild affiliation.

Both workers on this cotton farm are in the Farmer's Guild, so they get a speed bonus.

Beware, though, if you’re thinking “I would just set up schools to educate all of my Whiskers to Apprentice level”, Whiskers with advanced education will have a higher expectation ranking and will be much more demanding when it comes to creature comforts! Beyond Apprentice, you can train Whiskers as Craftmasters, Educated, and as Sailors. Craftmasters are the most elite of the manual workers, and are often required to fill the last position in a factory and provide a significant boost to production by their presence. In some late–game factories like the Machinist, the only acceptable employees are Craftmasters, so keeping track of where your elite labourers are assigned is key to an efficient colony - having an Engineer's Guild Craftmaster working an unskilled job growing Flax while your Machinist goes unstaffed and there are unskilled Farmer’s Guild Whiskers hanging around unemployed is no good for the collective.

At least one Whisker with the Sailor trait is required to staff a ship - that doesn’t mean all the workers on a ship must be sailors (though there’s a production boost if they are).

Educated Whiskers are required to fulfil the need for bureaucrats, researchers, and producers of science-y things like chemicals and medicines.

This institution of classes even among the subaltern colonists is interesting; even effective slaves can live a somewhat bourgeois life, living in comfortable, warm housing replete with furniture and bedding, eating nice meals and engaging in activities like visiting a bathhouse. Lower-status Whiskers are not banned from enjoying these creature comforts, and will greatly appreciate them, but your educated and skilled workers will expect them and be deeply unhappy if you fail to provide (and they'll let you know via a big Approval penalty).

Later in the game, you can research and construct adorable railway systems to improve transit around your colony

Research in Whiskerwood takes the typical form of tech-trees unlocked by the spending of resources. You build a Research Lab, staff it with educated Whiskers, choose which technology/building you want them to work on, and they consume the necessary resources until the tech is unlocked. Each tech either enables the creation of a new building or unlocks an additional recipe for an existing factory (e.g, enabling the Ore Furnace to produce Bronze as well as Copper). You can build additional Research Labs, but Whiskerwood discourages speedrunning the tech tree by introducing a production malus on every research lab for every additional one you build (called Coordination). It’s still faster to have two (or more) Research Labs than one, but it is not 2x faster, more like 1.7x faster, decreasing with every additional lab built.

As of the time of writing, the endgame - which I presume involves boosting your military capability, declaring independence, and fighting the Claws to secure it is not yet entirely implemented, but will probably include more extensive defensive buildings and naval combat. At present, you can construct naval vessels and dispatch fleets to reduce Pirate islands, but at the moment the quests seem to be bugged, in that you can just repeat the pirate quests constantly and receive so many days of immunity that pirates cease to be an issue altogether. I expect that in the full release the naval aspect of the game will be considerably tweaked and expanded, but I certainly can’t say for sure.

Whiskerwood is a great game with enough complexity to scratch the citybuilder itch, and although it peters out a bit at the endgame as your industry becomes strong enough that your whiskers produce wealth and abundance far beyond the tax bill, this is pretty normal for Early Access games that aren’t technically “finished” yet. It’s not unreasonable to frontload development into ensuring that the early and midgame are more polished as a sort of “setting out the stall” exercise to intrigue interested buyers - a lot of early access games morph significantly during their development (Timberborn is vastly more polished now than it was when the Early Access was floated), and I certainly don’t begrudge the studio for this approach; I can’t wait to play full-fat 1.0 whenever it turns up.

If you love Timberborn, but wish it was a bit more like Anno, you’ll certainly love this, and if you like citybuilders in general, the Whiskerwood experience is well worth your time.
