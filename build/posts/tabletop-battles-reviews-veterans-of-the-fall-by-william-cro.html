Tabletop Battles Reviews: Veterans of the Fall, by William Crowe

August 1st, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Games Workshop

Up until the end of last year, you had a fairly solid talent development pipeline at the Black Library. New and upcoming authors effectively 'auditioned' for larger commitments through one of three main short story paths.

The first of these is the digital eShort subscription, a pipeline which was shuttered late last year when- without warning- the customary Advent Subscription failed to materialize. Bah humbug!

The second path is through the pages of White Dwarf magazine. Not only is this path still open, but it's recently seen some excellent work from Russell Zimmerman (review) and Neil Andrew Miller (review).

Finally, while less common, you do see stories get their debut in the occasional anthology. While most recent anthologies have largely pulled from the pool of available digital shorts, the recent Death and Duty: An Astra Militarum Anthology (review) had a trio of never-before-seen tales from Zimmeman, John Sollitto, and Tom R. Pike.

While it remains to be seen how the move away from a digital-first release model will impact the talent pipeline in the Black Library, today's author is a terrific example of this model in action.

As the Crowe Flies

William Crowe's debut for the Black Library came in April of 2024, when The Shot That Kills You was included in the Space Marine Successors Week subscription. Later seeing print in the No Peace Among Stars anthology (review), the story concerned an Astartes of the Raptors matching wits with the Drukhari. A terrific little tale that highlighted the kinds of tactics the Chapter is known for, it set the table for Crowe's second offering, Tattershield, the following October.

Tattershield was released as part of Astra Militarum Week, and it too would find a home in No Peace Among Stars. But as good as The Shot that Kills You was, clearly it was the latter story that unlocked Crowe's progression as a Black Library author. The very next month, the first part of a five-part serial, Tatters of Hope, appeared in White Dwarf magazine.

Image credit: Games Workshop

After Tatters of Hope concludes in the March 2025 issue, Crowe disappears for a bit working on the next installment in the Tattershields story, the novella that gives its name to today's book. "Between Tattershield and The Shot That Kills You," I wrote last March in my review, "I hope we haven't seen the last of Crowe." That sentiment turned out to be somewhat prophetic, as Veterans of the Fall released just two months later.

While billed as "An Astra Militarum novel," Veterans is made up like an anthology: one short story, one five-part serial, and then a novella. In publishing, a "fix-up novel" is one that's constituted from a number of short stories, with the author stitching them together with a bit of bridging prose. We've seen those before in the Black Library, such as William King's Trollslayer or Ghostmaker by Dan Abnett. While Veterans of the Fall doesn't employ that tactic, it doesn't really need to; it certainly reads like a novel.

Cadian Command Squad. Credit: Rockfish

The Story

The book opens with the short story Tattershield, which introduces us to the Kasrkin unit of the same name led by Sergeant Kostantin Taikon. These are weary, ground-down Cadians who haven't had time to breathe let alone process their trauma. Crowe sets this stage right off the bat, noting the Cadians...

"...had already lived through unimaginable horrors, from the Fall of Cadia to the birth of the hellish rift that turned the night sky into a roiling, queasy storm of tortured pink unlight. They had survived the Great Retreat, falling back to landing pads and lighters, fighting off suicidal boarding actions to buy frigates and battleships time to shoulder their way clear of their home world's collapsing gravity well. In shock, they had limped towards the nearest friendly star- the cold grey light of Gavaine. And they had been there, under siege, for the next two years and nineteen days."

These aren't soldiers fresh to the fight, and that resigned weariness is an element that Crowe never lets stray too far from his narrative. They've been given an assassination mission, one targeting an enemy commander known as the Maligner.

For months, the Tattershields have been engaged in shaping operations against their target, assassinating enemy officers, destroying vox relays, and closing down the Maligner's safe havens one by one. Put your opponent in check enough times and they'll eventually run out of places to retreat to, and as the opening story unfolds some intel they've received from a questionable source sets up a final, decapitating strike that will end the Maligner once and for all.

Except, the Maligner isn't quite what he seems, and the Tattershields must risk it all to see their mission through.

That leads right into the five-part Tatters of Hope. I was particularly interested here to see how Crowe worked within the structure given how Neil Andrew Miller navigated it recently with The Chronicles of Bain. It's not just that the story has to be told in five parts, but as a serial the author must be mindful that many readers will be consuming it with a month between installments. Each needs to both stand on its own as well as connect to the greater series as a whole.

With Bain, Miller used a terrific time-jump device that followed soldier at different, consequential moments in his career. Crowe's narrative takes a more conjoined approach; these stories run right up next to each other, but Crowe ably manages to make each of them individual and distinct. For instance, the second one involves a rescue of a stranded convoy, while the third involves the Tattershields encountering a sniper in the field. This episodic approach works very well, with little info needing to be retained from one story to be able to enjoy the next.

And with those two stories completed, you're a little more than one-third of the way through the book and will have a pretty good idea of what the Tattershields are all about- just in time to dive into the concluding novella, Veterans of the Fall. That gives Crowe plenty of space to write the story he really wants to tell about his Kasrkin, and he ups the stakes by putting them on a collision course with the Emperor's Children.

Still on Gavaine, it's been a grinding, brutal conflict, but at last the enemy is on the verge of total defeat. Their last front of resistance is at the planetary voidport, so Sergent Taikon and the Tattershields are dispatched as the strike force to reclaim it.

Things go from bad to worse as they discover the presence of Traitor Guard within their ranks, a last plot by the enemy to try and destroy them from within. And if that wasn't bad enough, a trio of Astartes voidcraft have been detected on an intercept course for the planet.

Caught between the hammer and the anvil, can the Tattershields find a way to secure Gavaine once and for all, or will they truly see defeat snatched from the jaws of victory?

Kasrkin Gunner with Hotshot Volley Gun. Credit: Jack Hunter

A Question of Character

Throughout the different stories that make up the anthology/novel, Crowe has a knack for crafting engaging combat tales. Whether it's the buildup to raiding the Cathedral in Tattershield, the terrific mix of different scenarios in Tatters of Hope, or (in particular) the taking of the voidport in Veterans of the Fall I was at all times easily engaged with the action.

Indeed, his skill in coming up with different combat scenarios is one of the books real strengths. Sooner or later in reading the Black Library you'll inevitably run into 'bolter porn,' the overlong and rather formulaic combat sequences whose primary virtue seems to be increasing the word count at around the same rate as bolter shells hitting the floor. I give a lot of credit to writers who can craft imaginative battle scenes, and I never felt like Crowe's overstayed their welcome. If you enjoy a lot of Imperial Guard action, you'll have a lot to look forward to here.

But if there was a place I struggled to get into the book, it was with the characters that make up the Tattershields themselves. When an author creates their characters, they know more about them than anyone else. Their traits and inclinations, likes and dislikes. Their foibles, their valor, their backstory and sense of being. The job, then, is to translate that onto the page, bringing that character from their mind to the reader's. Even after 350 pages spent with them, I never really felt like I got to know Taikon and his Tattershields all that well.

I'm not convinced that this can be entirely placed at Crowe's feet, however. Regardless of what it says on the cover, Veterans of the Fall is not a novel, not truly. Where a writer given a novel to write can plan and plot it all the way through as one body of fiction, Crowe had to navigate a bit more start/stop. From a short story to a five-part serial to a novella, all (presumably) written at different times, I have to wonder how that structure might have impacted the final result. To be sure, the Black Library derived some added benefit from it- the short story was run in a Theme Week and later in a paper anthology, the five-part serial enriched nearly half-a-year's worth of White Dwarf, and so on. But did that flexible modularity come at a cost to the overall cohesion? It's difficult to say.

But from Abnett's Ghosts to Rhuairidh James' Death Rider (review) or Chem Dog by Callum Davis (review), what brings me back to Imperial Guard stories time and again isn't the action scenes, it's the characters. I'd have liked to have seen a little more depth given to Crowe's cast of characters, more scenes that bring them to life like the one (on page 216) where Orrich momentarily steps into the shoes of a Commissar.

Finally, while I tend not to worry too much about absolute fidelity to established canonical power levels, I'd be remiss not to mention there were some moments that even I struggled to accept. In one scene, a soldier keeps on fighting after taking a burst to the chest from an Astartes bolter. In another, a band of Kasrkin manages to dispatch multiple Emperor's Children in Terminator armor, with surprisingly light casualties. Paradoxically enough, it's the Astra Militarum's relative weakness that makes them some of the most compelling heroes in the Black Library. Moments like these- more common towards the end of the book- felt more like the acts of Astartes.

Overall, this wasn't the full-length debut I'd hoped for from William Crowe, but there wasn't anything in here that would dissuade me from grabbing his next Black Library contribution with both hands. It wasn't hard to see the promise in here, that with a little bit more character work his Tattershields could hold their own in the pantheon of terrific Astra Militarum heroes.
