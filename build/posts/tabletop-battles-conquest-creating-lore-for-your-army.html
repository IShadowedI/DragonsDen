Tabletop Battles Conquest: Creating Lore for Your Army

June 9th, 2026 via Goonhammer

People play tabletop miniature wargames for all sorts of reasons. You might be the practical hobbyist, who enjoys the craft of assembling minis, or you might be someone who enjoys painting. You might be the stats nerd, who can’t help but tie themselves in knots at some point in every game. You might be the hyper competitor, for whom asymmetric wargames provide a satisfying complexity that chess sorely lacks. You might be a fan of immersive storytelling, and the combination of agency, lore and chance strokes the imaginative part of you.

Chances are you’re some sort of combination of all of the above. But one common challenge faced by wargamers is the ability to develop lore of our own, no matter how much we might love it. Stat lines, game rules, lore for the setting and paint schemes are all given to us in various forms, but the particular contextual story for our army can be tricky.

I personally think that having some solid army lore is a really helpful way to dramatically increase pleasure in the game, but the effort required to develop it often feels misplaced. This is particularly true for Conquest: The Last Argument of Kings (TLAOK), because that elusive ‘immersion’ factor requires more personal effort than other games.

Firstly, rules around terrain in TLAOK make two-dimensional the most pragmatic option, which forces us to put conscious effort into imagination. This is made harder when our brains are taken up thinking about card order, positioning, tactical decision-making and statistical analysis. Secondly, its rank-and-file nature sometimes provokes rules that by necessity break immersion (such as the effects of failing a charge that would demand great use of the free 90-degree wheel, how monsters can hide in forests that in effect are behind them, and so on).

But it’s definitely possible to enrich our gaming experience through the development of deep, personal army lore, so I thought it might be a good idea to provide some structured support to aid in its development.

How to Use This Article

Below, I’ve laid out half a dozen aspects that could contribute to your army’s lore. Of course, this is not the only way to approach it. If other aspects occur to you as you’re going, take things in a new direction!

For each aspect, roll a D6 and consult the table, and it will give you a starting point for that aspect. Below each table I’ve expanded on the items in it. As before, if another result seems more attractive to you, just go with that. And if something that’s not on the table feels like a better fit, be my guest. I know we love rules, but when it comes to lore the only rule is internal cohesion, and even that is one that’s often better broken.

By the end of the article you should have a solid backstory that gives a proper headstart on every game, to answer why your army is there and what might guide your regiments to do what you want them to.

The Origin 

Let’s start by figuring out where your army comes from. This will help to shape the whole feel for the army and can inform everything from character’s names to paint schemes to the units you choose to field.

Result Answer
1 A single location
2 A coalition
3 A diaspora
4 A ragtag band
5 Conscripts
6 The past

Your army might hail from a single location. Get out that map of Eä and find a bit of the map that feels like it fits.

It might be a coalition of two or more locations. These might be near to one another or linked in some other way, but for some reason (what is that reason for you?) they don’t fight alone.

As a diaspora they represent their own faction that for whatever reason is no longer in their own homeland. Maybe they know that reason, or maybe it’s been lost to the mists of time.

They might be a ragtag band of small towns and mercenary groups that fight together.

Or they might be conscripts, perhaps taken regardless of willingness from a population or forced into action following conquests of other people groups.

For some, their origin may be so far in the past that the origins have been forgotten, and perhaps the reason why has its own story to tell…

Dweghom Found painted by Josh Gagel

The Catalyst

Why does anyone go to war? There must have been a call to arms at some point. What event led your army to form in the first place?

Result Answer
1 A divine call
2 A betrayal
3 A fight for survival
4 The death of a hero
5 Self-defence
6 A disaster!
A divine call might suggest a supernatural experience - perhaps everyone in your army has experienced the same vision, which explains their personal commitment to the cause. Or it might have been an evangelistic sermon that sparked action. Maybe some in the army have more faith than others…

The thing that unites them might be a betrayal. Perhaps claiming your army is the army of Riismark feels like too much of a stretch, but what if they feel like they are the true expression because the previous leader made the wrong call. Or perhaps a political leader in an important enemy people sold them out and your army arose in protest.

It might simply be a fight for survival - a natural (or supernatural) disaster, or growing military threat, means that the army feels like it has no choice but to exist.

It could have been the death of a hero, and the army now exists to avenge them or retrieve something that was lost. Who was that hero, and what was the manner of their death?

It could be self-defence, in response to an invasion or terrorist activity. What was the nature of the event?

Maybe the army exists because of a much bigger disaster, leaving no option. Their entire city collapsed under a spell, and those in the army are simply those that happened to not be there at that moment. Slavers took their entire civilisation and they have sworn to fight until they can be reunited to their families.

This part of the storytelling creates a truly compelling reason for the models on the table to be there. Why are those longbowmen charging into battle against the Chosen of War? Because, in their minds, they have no option not to.

The Objective

The opposite of the catalyst - why do they feel they don’t have the option not to - is the more positive question of the army’s mission or vision. Part of the reason they fight is because, if they achieve their objective, they will know that they have done what they set out to do, and today’s battle is one step in that direction.

Result Answer
1 Restoration
2 Redemption
3 Protection
4 To pay a debt
5 To claim a new home
6 To solve a mystery

The army might be inspired by restoration. A past event needs righting. A royal bloodline needs returning to the throne, or a historic monument or religious icon needs retrieving.

It might be for redemption, where the people they are fighting on behalf of (or maybe the army’s participants themselves) have somehow collectively committed a sin so grave that they need to make up for it.

Perhaps their own people rightly live in fear of being destroyed, and your army exists for protection. If they can wipe out every enemy, future generations will survive.

Or maybe they operate to pay a debt, which has been taken on somehow. A huge sum of money is owed, and with limited resources themselves, pillaging is the only reasonable way!

If their old home was destroyed, or even if the grass seems greener in some other location where there are valuable resources, the army might exist to claim a new home. What is it that makes their old home uninhabitable, or what’s so attractive about this new place?

Finally, what if they exist to solve a mystery, like a murder or a theft. Somehow, they have become convinced that the evidence sits just the other side of this next battle.

The Identity

Every army has a reputation, even if only within its own ranks. What is yours known for? This is an easy way to add flair to your army.

Result Answer
1 A nickname
2 A grim ‘signature’
3 A quirk on the battlefield
4 A unique ritual
5 Impenetrable codes
6 Something feels off…

Maybe your army has a nickname. Here’s one way to do this: Just flip through a dictionary! Find one adjective and one noun, it really doesn’t matter that much what they are. I just did it and my finger landed on Last and Bathing. If my army is known as The Last Bathers, it indicates a story behind it - maybe there was a holy fountain that has since dried up, and my army was the last to visit it. Or maybe their stench is so great that their reputation precedes them in the wrong way…

Perhaps they have a grim signature, meaning that when they’ve won a battle it’s visually obvious. After seeing it once, you probably wish you never had. Use your imagination, and every time your opponent removes a unit from the table take delight in explaining what their comrades are currently seeing!

Perhaps they have a quirk on the battlefield, which excites the senses. Is it a distinctive warcry, an intoxicating smell from having burned incense (or something less palatable), or even something visual that you could tie into a paint scheme?

Or your army might have a unique ritual; before or after every battle they… Come up with something utterly random, like an insistence that everyone plucks a hair from their head and feeds it to the person next to them, to remind them of their camaraderie and in memory of Hairy Wethain, for whom they are all fighting.

They might have impenetrable codes, so refer to one another using names that only your army uses. Everyone knows they’re Brute Drones, but your army refers to them as Dry Paps. Why? Maybe there’s a reason, or maybe the reason has been long forgotten.

Perhaps it’s as simple as something feels off. When the army is fighting, they just do something entirely random, seemingly without warning. One guy shouts “Hey!” and the rest of the army jumps in the air. Why does the Scion of Conquest keep on laughing manically?

Scion of Conquest - Credit GeneralCross (Jamie)

The Secret

By this point, you should have a pretty clear idea of why your army exists and what it’s like. That might be enough, so treat this point as optional, but a bit of intrigue always helps make a story more interesting. To bring it to life, what is the shadow side of your army?

Result Answer
1 A forbidden romance
2 Blackmail
3 Manipulation
4 Munity at bay
5 Conspiracy theories
6 Double agents

Maybe there’s a forbidden romance going on, maybe between people in the army, or someone in the army and a noble. Some in the army have picked up on it but others haven’t…

Perhaps there’s an epidemic of blackmail in the army. The warlord or army’s sponsor has something on every individual in the army. Each one of them is fighting because all hell would break loose for them personally if they didn’t, and none of them know that the same is true of their comrades.

Or perhaps those in the army are all victims of manipulation. The cause they’re united behind is actually just a front, being used to mislead them. Without them realising it, the people they’re protecting are actually the ones they’ve sworn to destroy. Making it more interesting, perhaps one minor character is in on the plan and is driving things from behind the scenes.

Maybe the army is in a state of mutiny at bay; the leaders are on a completely different page from the majority of the soldiers. Maybe they don’t realise it, or perhaps a minor character is on the cusp of launching a coup. It’s only a matter of time before the house of cards comes crashing down, which might explain why those Charge rolls keep coming up as 1s at critical moments…

Or maybe the entire army is steadily becoming obsessed with conspiracy theories, and we all know that a dangerous idea in the wrong hands could lead to its downfall. What if the soldiers are starting to suspect that their leader is secretly being paid by the other side, or that the battle they’re in the midst of fighting is simply entertainment for the rulers on both sides?

Finally, perhaps the army believes that they’re fighting for a cause, but the truth is that they’re actually being brainwashed. The people they’ve set out to protect for ethical reasons are actually highly corrupt, but the army just can’t see it.

Conquest

Taking Your Army Forward

Now the big picture has been set, you’re ready to get stuck into the details. Every battle tells its own story, and once you’ve led them on the table a couple of times that background information ought to be enough to catalyse some storytelling behind those moments that define a game.

After a few games you’ll begin to notice the patterns that were already there, because they become more memorable. Why is it that when the Karyatids shout the name of their shared ancestor before firing they hit so much harder? What is it that means that the Ipparchos’ own regiment of Companion Cavalry seems to start off so well and then roll a bunch of 6s?

One detail in particular that’s always worth paying attention to is your army’s characters. Giving them names and backstories can be helpful, and perhaps there are characters that sit ‘off screen’ but feel important to the army in any case.

Regardless of what you do with all of this I’d love to wish you enormous luck as you shape your army’s lore and experience its emergent narrative arc. And I’d love to hear what lore your current army has!
