The Paint Review: Monument Hobbies ProAcryl 1Step Phase Two

October 13th, 2026 via Goonhammer

Greetings, ladies and gentlemen, and welcome to another paint review. We bring you a double act this time - I, keewa, your pundit of pigment, am joined by the formidable Contemptor Kevin to discuss the second wave of the ProAcryl 1Step range from Monument Hobbies.

We’ve already discussed the general idea of 1Step in the first review, which you can find here, but I offer you a short recap or précis here: 1Step is the long-awaited entry of Monument Hobbies into the area of the market generally called “Contrast-style” paints, characterised by their fluidity and staining - paints like Army Painter Speedpaints, Vallejo Xpress Color, and so on and so on. Every man and his dog seems to have a trademark-friendly range of contrast-style paints these days, and so Monument’s very late entry into the arena raised a few eyebrows, but plenty of excitement as well.

With thanks to Monument Hobbies for providing keewa with a copy of 1Step Wave 2 for review. Contemptor Kevin purchased his copy of the paints himself. 

Scarlet Red

Keewa: The first wave of 1Step paints was lacking a “proper red”. Crimson (a lovely colour) technically counts as a red, certainly, but that’s already dark and quite desaturated - Scarlet Red is much beefier and has a lot more punch in the saturation department. It’s very impactful, though it’ll take a few coats to cover fully; bear that in mind when it goes on pink over white at first.

Contemptor Kevin: My big test with it was to slap it on a pre-highlighted/shadowed red cloak, the one I used for my Victrix Guard Ancient.  One use and I was absolutely sold on this paint.  

Rust

Keewa: Previously a colour only available to attendees of Adepticon; now in Wave 2, Rust is available to everyone. The first 1-Step set featured a very saturated reddish “hot” Orange; now the range includes Rust as a darker and more natural oxide orange with a brownish tint. As you might guess from the name of the colour, an oxide orange is the perfect colour for painting naturally weathered iron and steel, and the high flow of 1Step allows the painter to create easy streaking rust stains. Grimdark and Blanche-esque painters will like this one.

Bronze Yellow

Keewa: A similar relationship to Warm Yellow as Rust is to Orange, Bronze Yellow is a dark, warm, earthy ochre yellow with a brownish tint. It’s ideal for shading “standard” yellows into a more natural register, as well as fur and hair. 

Contemptor Kevin: I wish I had a pot of Nazdreg Yellow to compare this to Bronze Yellow, because both *I think* are similar paints.  This one might be the weakest link in the set, because it occupies a niche between Olive Yellow and Warm Yellow.  I'm happy to have it and it's a good paint to use, but it's much more subtle versus some of the other paints.  

Olive Yellow

Keewa: I think the best way to describe Olive Yellow is to call it a “dirty” yellow. It’s not necessarily super desaturated, but it has a good amount of both brown and green. It’ll probably find plenty of use shading things like Cadian khaki fatigues, stone textures, and general grime application.

Contemptor Kevin: I think Keewa hits the nail on the head.  For those looking at Contrast comparisons, it's more of an easier-to-use/more pigment-dense Plaguebearer Flesh.  My use of it suggests that it could make a great shade color for Plaguebearer Flesh for some extremely quick but nice-looking NMM-aged gold.  Here. I used the Olive Yellow to shade the PBF.

Yellow Green

Keewa: My very first thought upon seeing this paint was “Ork skin in a bottle”; this paint has everything I want from an Ork skin - the colour is punchy with a lot of depth and versatility. Since it’s transparent, you can change how it behaves by underpainting with different colours, adjusting the warmth and coolness of the highlights and shadows to achieve everything from a bright yellow-y goblinoid skin to one with bluish shadows.  Yellow Green also excels as both a colour of nature and decay, ideal for your wood-elves, moss and leaves on Sylvaneth tree-people, ooze on Death Guard and Nurgle daemons, etc.  

Contemptor Kevin: For the most part, I have been using Contrast Warp Lightning as a filter to give my Orks a very punchy skin tone.  I don't think that will change for me, because there's a bunch of underlayers that Warp Lightning filters.  But directly over a very light primer?  I think Yellow Green looks better:

Dark Turquoise

Keewa: Oh boy, oh boy, everyone knows that I love turquoise, and this one is a real beauty, sailing right up the list of Paints I Love. First things first, a quibbling nitpick: this paint is not dark, and I do not understand why it is labelled as such. The unusually unhelpful naming mirrors Dark Turquoise from the normal ProAcryl line, which is also not dark - what ProAcryl calls Dark Turquoise I would just call Turquoise. That’s by the by, though; this paint has punch in spades, a beautifully vibrant and intense colour. This paint is ideal for plasma coils + power glow, lizardmen scales, and magic spells.

Contemptor Kevin:  I suspect the reason why this is called Dark Turquoise is also to let us know that this isn't just the 1-Step version of Contrast Akhelian Green, but that this paint is much richer and has a lot more pigmentation than the venerable Warhammer paint.  Making a good looking Alpha Legion army with a metal undercoat and this paint has never been easier.  

Dark Green Blue

Keewa: In contrast to Dark Turquoise, Dark Green Blue actually is a dark turquoise colour with a strong oceanic, abyssal vibe. It’s obviously darker, but the saturation is dialled down, and the result is a paint that is greener, but also considerably colder. If Dark Turquoise is the colour of the sea at a Caribbean beach, Dark Green Blue is the colour of the raging South Atlantic. This paint is obviously an exceptional shade for both greens and blues, and also functions nicely as a good chromatic black for other applications where you need a black that’s freezing cold. 

Deep Blue

Keewa: Not the computer that gave Garry Kasparov a good shoeing. Deep Blue is more of an ultramarine than the regular phthalo–esque Blue of the first wave, with a bit more purple to it. This particular blue has a good deal of similarity to the regular ProAcryl Ultramarine in both tone and boldness. 

Contemptor Kevin: Ironically, this particular Blue is probably the best contrast-style paint for painting Ultramarines with a Warhammer Paint Macragge Blue tone.  The Contrast Ultramarines Blue is far too dark and desaturated.  This color has the same feel as Macragge Blue while remaining more vibrant than the Warhammer paint.  It won't replace how I do my blindingly blue Ultras, but for those who prefer the modern style (or want a darker color for Heresy), these will do the trick.

Black

Contemptor Kevin:  In Set #1, our darkest color was Dark Blue Black.  I noted at the time that it was a very dark blue and would work well for Ravenwing models, but it was very clearly not a true black.  Set #2 finally gives us the line’s true black, simply called “Black”.  Now, for the way I paint, Warhammer Paint Black Templar is one of my workhorse paints.  When I want to “reset” something to hide the color underneath it, such as when painting trim or details, it tends to get a coat of Black Templar. Testing these paints out on my Land Speeder, one coat of Black struggled to hide the base color.  It worked fine with blues, but over a gold it changed the color to dark gold.  Another caveat I had with it is that it tends to dry a fair bit slower than Black Templar: This means using it to try and paint the rim of the base black was more fraught than I thought it was going to be.  Sadly, I don't think this 1Step is going to replace my beloved Black Templar on the brush.  

Keewa: I disagree with my learned colleague; I find that the slow drying time of the 1Step paints makes them ideal for glazing shadows. If I compare this to Army Painter Speedpaint Grim Black, I really appreciate the slower drying time; with the AP paint, you have hardly any time on your hands before it begins to form a skin and tears with a bit of manipulation. This paint will be ideal for blacklining and separation; I think it’s going to become a favourite for me and many others.

Bright Slate Grey

Keewa: Going to start calling this one Notpothecary White. ProAcryl has a reputation for excellent whites, and although this isn’t called a white, it’s no exception, and there’s no doubt what it’s for. It’s a very similar tone to the venerable Apothecary White, but it’s better. Why? The extended drying time of the 1Step formula means that it’s a lot easier to move the paint off the flat areas where you don’t want it, deftly helping to avoid the telltale coffee stains and tide marks that plague contrast painting. If you’re painting white, you really need this.

Contemptor Kevin: Keewa is underselling this one.  This paint is better than Apothecary White (and the second attempt, Soulblight Grey) in every way.  The flow is far superior, and the bottle design (with a built-in mixer) makes actually using this paint substantially easier.  Vis-à-vis Soulblight Grey, this produced a cooler white, but a smoother and nicer white.  

Dark Brown

From the top clockwise: Dark Brown, Warhammer Paint Cygor Brown, Warhammer Paint Snakebite Leather, Warm Brown, Warhammer Paint Wyldwood, Army Painter Speedpaint Satchel Brown.

Contemptor Kevin: As you can see, this brown is very different from the last set’s Warm Brown, being, if not necessarily darker, then containing much less red and feeling much more desaturated.  This color strikes me as an obvious choice for older wood or even a muddy texture.  One interesting property I found with this paint is that it went over the GW White Scar-primed base pretty easily.  By contrast, the Warhammer Paint Contrasts were pretty quick to pull away from the surface and bead up.  Accordingly, I was able to get an even coat of Dark Brown easily, but the other browns proved more challenging.  

Keewa: Compared to Warm Brown, Dark Brown strays into a colder, more Van Dyke Brown territory, and as such, it’s a pretty good shadow colour for Warm Brown.

Dark Purple

Keewa: Compared to the Royal Purple that came with the first wave, Dark Purple occupies the other side of the purple spectrum - where Royal Purple skews towards the warmer Magenta side, Dark Purple is decidedly in the cold Indigo-Purple realm, on the cusp of being a dark blue. This colour contrast actually makes it an ideal shade for warmer purples like Royal Purple. 

Clockwise from Top: Dark Purple, Ultramarines Blue, Hive Dweller Purple, Cloudburst Blue, Royal Purple, Shyish Purple

Contemptor Kevin: Dark Purple sits in a position where it's not quite a purple, but not quite a blue.  It's also different from basically most of the other purples in the Contrast line.  I think Keewa is right - this is going to be a wonderful shadow color for Royal Purple, or as a bridge color for certain muzzle burns/heat warping for metal.  

Final Thoughts

Keewa: Rather than being another straightforward Contrast-Clone, Monument has sought to carve out a niche and establish 1Step as being a premium paint “along the same lines as Contrast, but different” - the lengthened drying time makes manipulating the paint on the model much easier. 1Step flows through the airbrush in the same manner as an ink, and unlike Army Painter Speedpaints, 1Step doesn’t foul up the needle by drying on the tip as it leaves the airbrush; honestly, with the way I tend to paint, using 1Step as airbrush filters is probably my biggest utility for these kinds of paints, so the drying time extender comes very much in handy. The Secret Sauce medium also works well as a thinner for both airbrushing and glazing. 

On the consumer side of things, at 6 euros per 22ml bottle, these paints can hardly be accused of being cheap. While not necessarily as ridiculously expensive as Contrast paint (more on that in a minute), 1Step considerably outstrips other contrast-a-likes (Xpress Color, Speedpaint etc.) both in bottle price and price per ml. Since ProAcryl positions itself as a “premium” brand, this is basically to be expected, but it’ll still sting your wallet – that’s 140-ish euros for the current range of 24 paints.

The 1Step paints are great; I find myself reaching for them all the time. They’re quite easy to recommend - even if the cost of buying in is a little steep.

Contemptor Kevin:  You get what you pay for in these paints, in my opinion.  These paints are all unique colors and extremely easy to use, both in terms of airbrushing and in terms of brush painting.  With the exception of the Black and potentially Bronze Yellow, these tones do not really overlap with Contrast, and thus having both is helpful. I'm not as keen on Black as Keewa is, but that's because I use Black Templar in a bit different manner than he does, where having the GW Pot design is a boon.  I like having sets of both, though I will readily agree that this stuff is better through the airbrush than Speedpaint.   Have any questions or feedback?
