A Three-Player Battle Report for Conquest First Blood

July 29th, 2026 via Goonhammer

The route that took me into the world of Conquest was lubricated by the friendship of two friends (Josh and Josh - I’m not Josh). Isn’t it nice to have friends! And yet Conquest, like most tabletop wargames, is designed to be played by two players, so it’s reasonably common for two of us to play while the third sits in spectator mode. Have you ever wondered if it would be possible to sensibly play with three players?

Wonder no longer.

The three of us have really been enjoying the reworked rules for First Blood. It’s unlike The Last Argument of Kings (TLAOK - the rank-and-flank version of Conquest) in many ways. One important way is that in TLAOK movement is done through rectangular blocks of models needing to wheel. This makes facing two opponents almost impossible, because often the only sensible way to manoeuvre is done by thinking about straight lines facing ‘forwards’ - which means virtually nothing when there are two directions to attack.

First Blood, on the other hand, allows for free movement, so we struck while the iron was hot and decided to give a three-way battle a go.

The Setup

Like TLAOK, the scenario setup for First Blood is designed for two players, but is inherently more flexible so lends itself to malleability. By default in First Blood the two players will roll and choose alternately a ‘mission’ (objective markers and zones, and victory conditions) and ‘territories’ (deployment areas), along with an ‘encounter’, which adjusts the rules to reflect a particular narrative focus, alongside ‘tactics’, which adjust the rules in an asymmetric way.

This was already going to be confusing enough so we ignored encounters and tactics altogether. Instead, we simply developed three-person alternatives to a Mission and Territories.

The Mission we developed was number 5 from the book, Proving Grounds. We kept all the same rules but added two more Artefacts and spread them out a bit to give us more for three people to fight over. Here was our custom Mission setup (on a 4’ x 4’ table):

The scoring is simple:

  • 2 points for each Artefact you hold at the end of the game
  • 3 points for killing an enemy warlord
  • 2 points for killing a hero
  • 1 point for killing a leader

For selecting Territories we came up with a setup that felt reasonably balanced, which is harder than it looks with a 16-square layout! We came up with the layout below. Like most Mission-Territory combinations in First Blood there are clear advantages in terms of starting position, but that makes the starting roll-off meaningful!

This means that green and red each begin with one Artefact entirely within their deployment zone. Blue shares a deployment boundary with green for one Artefact and red for another, with all three initially contesting the central Artefact. The deployment rules for First Blood allow for strategic placement of facedown cards to protect boundary Artefacts, so this isn’t as much of a disadvantage as it may first appear.

The Table

Terrain can dictate everything in a wargame. The story we told ourselves was that a caravan had been attacked but that something had startled the bandits, leading to the caravan’s valuable contents being abandoned throughout the map (the Artefacts). It was important that we had a road, therefore, with plenty of scenery to hide behind.

We clustered some rocks, buildings and trees around the road, with a water feature in one corner. As predicted, this immediately had an impact on the way things played out.

Initial table layout

I played with a Spires list, Josh played as the Hundred Kingdoms (I'll call him 100K) and the other Josh brought out his Old Dominion (OD). In an initial roll off for choosing deployment zones we decided to use an A-B-C-C-B-A scheme so that the blue zone wouldn’t be too disadvantaged. 100K won and chose the green area, I went second and opted for red, meaning that OD ended up with blue.

He placed his first card right in the centre in order to take the three-way contested Artefact.

I knew I was going to get the one deep in my own territory so went as close as I could to the one I was contesting with OD, and 100K did the same with his equivalent. Back to OD, and that piece of terrain that put me 2" further back than 100K was enough reason for OD to invest heavily in the boundary with me.

Cards placed and revealed, and models positioned, and we had our initial table setup. The Fallen Divinity took centre stage, surrounded by Moroi (using Ghols as proxies - OD’s Moroi aren’t painted yet and he couldn’t bear the shame of grey plastic gracing the pages of Tabletop Battles), threatening to swarm my Siegebreaker Behemoth, challenged in the other direction by 100K’s Crimson Tower and protected from behind by my lone Leonine Avatara.

The Longbowmen were elevated on a rock to provide covering fire for the Household Guard and a Theist Priest, while a couple of lowly Mounted Squires sat alone in one corner, and my Lineage Highborne and Avatara faced off against a Profane Sepulchre, a Karyatid and regiment of Legionnaires in the other.

The table after initial deployment

Battle Report

Turn 1, and I won the rolloff. The perfect chance for my Mimetic Assassin to leap out from the shadows and launch a surprise attack on that utterly repulsive Karyatid. My rolling was atrocious, and that set a precedent for the rest of the game.

The Mimetic Assassin pounces!

OD’s strategy was straightforward. The Moroi were there to get as much Dark Power as possible as quickly as possible, so he sent them in every possible direction, to provide a screen for his Fallen Divinity. The Legionnaires in part surrounded my Mimetic Assassin and in part went for the Avatara, while the Fallen Divinity also came in my direction. He really wanted that Artefact.

For their part, the Avatara fought on bravely and survived for a decent spell. I wish the same could have been said for the Assassin, which was killed on Turn 3, giving OD the first 2 points on the board. On the other side of the table, my Siegebreaker Behemoth, which really ought to have dived straight into the Fallen Divinity, instead saw the threat of the Crimson Tower bearing down on my vulnerable Leonine Avatara and chose to eat some horses. Cue me rolling a bunch of 6s.

A typical roll for this game

The Longbowmen tried to do some damage against the Fallen Divinity but failed, so switched their attention to the Moroi, but not soon enough for 100K to also roll a bunch of 6s and get his Household Guard essentially trapped between the building and the rock.

Turn 4 included a smart play from me, though I say it myself. My Lineage Highborne challenged the Fallen Divinity to a Duel, moving her in-between my now vulnerable Avatara and the Legionnaires, keeping them alive for one more turn. Of course, she was almost killed in the process, but managed to make the Resolve check even with the forced reroll, and in Turn 5 legged it away as fast as she could.

An unbalanced battle

Monsters in the new First Blood are incredible. They’re rightly limited to one per army at 1,000 points, and the Siegebreaker Behemoth was far and away my best-performing unit. It should have immediately killed the Crimson Tower before burrowing away, but those 6s kept on coming up for me and while I did manage to kill the leader on Turn 4 (score now 2-1-0) it was trapped there for too long to get her to where she really wanted to be.

Turn 5 saw 100K’s Longbowmen really making a dent, helping to free up the Mounted Squires from the Moroi they were stuck to, and clearing the way for the Household Guard to make progress towards the Fallen Divinity. Meanwhile, the combination of Fallen Divinity and Legionnaires proved too much for the Avatara and while my Leonine Avatara had held onto its Artefact while killing the two Moroi in front of it, that left me very firmly in last place, leaving me to rely on the Siegebreaker Behemoth to finish off the Crimson Tower with ease, pick up the Artefact they were carrying and burrow away to have a more meaningful impact next turn.

The Longbowmen clear a path for the Household Guard, while the Mounted Squires in the background fight their own battles...

Turn 6 was as exciting as a tabletop wargame will ever be. OD won the roll-off, deciding to send as much as he could against my Siegebreaker Behemoth that had just emerged from the ground behind his Karyatid, and did away with her. The Household Guard then did some real work on the Fallen Divinity, and while it was tempting for me to go after the odd 100K warrior carrying an Artefact I just wanted that thing dead, so stepped out of the way instead and enjoyed seeing the last Longbowman with its final roll of the dice deal the single additional wound just as the dark power pool had been depleted, and with that 100K went ahead, the score now sitting at 3-2-1.

The Profane Sepulchre hides

But all of that action had left the Theist Priest broken, meaning that when I won the roll-off for Turn 7 the best thing I could do was to challenge him to a duel he couldn’t decline. The Mounted Squires flew down the other side of the rock to take on the Karyatid in a fight that was only ever going to have one winner, and the Profane Sepulchre, now retreated into a corner, decided to make the most of its undead entourage. The overperforming Longbowmen finished off the Leonine Avatara, another Artefact now in the hands of the Hundred Kingdoms, and it felt like the momentum was firmly with them.

The Mounted Squires declare a charge

On Turn 8, having won the roll off again, I managed to charge at the Household Guard leader and kill it, giving me a marginal burst of dopamine but exposing my Lineage Highborne to be shot at by the entire unit of Longbowmen.

The Lineage Highborne bracing for a barrage

As the final whistle blew, the Old Dominion were holding two Artefacts, giving them a final score of 6. I held nothing, my entire army having died, a miserable 5 points putting me firmly in last place. But the victor by a country mile was 100K and his Hundred Kingdoms, holding three Artefacts and having killed both warlords, landing a total of 12 points!

What Did We Learn?

Let’s start with the rules for First Blood. This new version is a whole lot of fun. It’s seriously swingy in comparison with TLOAK so while it’s cliche to blame dice rolls for game outcomes the difference between rolling a single 1 or a 6 in this can be extremely impactful.

They do still feel like they’re in beta stage. The Moroi contributing 2 dark power with every model makes them extraordinarily more valuable than anything else in that bracket, and the Karyatid’s disgusting random handful of additional wounds when it costs the same as the Leonine Avatara simply feels unfair, as much of a sore loser I may not be.

We also got a bunch of rules wrong. Like, we’d forgotten that Monsters can’t be pushed back or broken when not injured, meaning that the Laurean Lance ability shouldn’t have worked. In fact, I should probably have won. So there.

The game really did work well though. The only rule we had to change was the supremacy roll-off, which we could have done a couple of ways but felt fair enough the way we did it. It took about four hours in the end because we were chatting a lot and drinking throughout. And it turns out that accidentally drinking a cider that’s more than 8% will make one feel a bit lightheaded, so it’s a good job we live within walking distance of each other.

All in all, this was a proper laugh. My list should have been thought through a touch more than “how can I get away with as few models as possible”, and that Siegebreaker-Crimson Tower matchup didn’t work out well for either of us, but I maintain that the Old Dominion are horrible to play against, the gods of the dice hate me, and this hobby is a lot of fun. We will do it again!
