Tabletop Battles Autopsy: The Big 10th Edition Retrospective – Chaos

May 26th, 2026 via Goonhammer
We're moving on to part three of our big 10th Edition retrospective, this time looking at the forces of Chaos. As our resident Chaos Lord, TheChirurgeon is in the driving seat for a lot of this, but the rest of the gang is here adding thoughts and comments from the sidelines.

If you want to look at parts 1 and 2 (including the details of how we assembled this in the Space Marines article), check these out from the links below:
  • Tabletop Battles Autopsy: The Big 10th Edition Retrospective - Space Marines
  • Tabletop Battles Autopsy: The Big 10th Edition Retrospective - Imperium

Chaos Factions

Chaos Daemons



Games Workshop waffled on daemons over the course of the edition, and it seemed for a while like they were going to be legitimately sunset as a faction until they finally resurfaced in the 11th edition previews on Warhammer Community. Daemons are the only faction to not get a Codex in tenth edition, and while you could count the Deathwatch with them, they at least got support in Imperial Agents.

Of course, not having a Codex didn’t necessarily mean being bad; on the contrary, Daemons were competitive through much of 10th edition thanks to the ability to drop back into the warp whenever they pleased and a number of very powerful Detachments, several of which needed nerfs throughout the edition.

Scores

  • Power: 4
  • Merits: 3.5
  • Mistakes: 4.25
Best Detachment: Daemonic Incursion (by tie-break with Legion of Excess)

Worst Detachment: Blood Legion

What Worked

Shadow of Chaos is a weird rule, requiring you to control objectives in each part of the table - your deployment zone, no man’s land, and your opponent’s deployment zone - but it works surprisingly well, enough so that it’s been duplicated successfully in other factions. The Incursion Detachment ability to go back into reserves (and deep strike back onto the table later) really made the army feel like no other army in the game.

On the topic of Daemonic Incursion: What a great Detachment. Competitively relevant all edition long, a useful toolbox of abilities, gives a play experience that really aligns with the themes of the faction.

What Didn’t

4+ Feel No Pain just generally shouldn’t exist, and we went waaaay too long having to live with a Great Unclean One sporting one in the edition. It’s also pretty clear that Legion of Excess was a mistake the moment it dropped - Full Hit and Wound re-rolls is just a stupidly strong thing to get, and the tradeoff of giving up Fights First on the charge was completely optional.

There’s also just a lot of stuff in this book (PDF, whatever) that is just kind of there, taking up space, never seeing the table. You’re so dependent on the Greater Daemons to do the heavy lifting, and so committed to spending points on them (typical lists run 5 and commit 1500pts+ to doing so, and a recently successful list ran 6), that there’s not a lot of room for anything else. Pick which 5 of the big guys you like, add around 4 units chosen from either Plaguebearers/Nurglings/Beasts of Nurgle/Flesh Hounds to give you a few bits to do an action with, and that’s your list. It makes the faction very samey, like playing off-brand Knights.

What We Learned

Stop giving out 4+ FNPs. It just never works. Same deal for full Hit and Wound re-rolls - it’s an itch you simply do not need to scratch.

What We Want to See

Better viable unit variety. There’s a lot of cool, weird stuff in Daemons, and it would be nice if any of it ever hit the table. In some ways Daemons are victims of the switch to the Leader model here, because a bunch of their roster is Leaders for one or more lesser Daemon unit, and when those are bad, so are the Leaders! This is where Upgrades could really help - create more compelling reasons to use the lesser stuff, and it will uplift the rest of the army along with it.

Chaos Knights



Chaos Knights were done pretty dirty by their Index, which paled in power next to the rules given to Imperial Knights, with their plan essentially turning out to be, “run War Dogs and just pretend you don’t have a Detachment rule.” That changed dramatically with the release of Codex: Chaos Knights last summer, when they received an updated book which at first glance seemed weaker - most Knights lost a point of toughness but gained extra wounds - but turned out to be much better, particularly when combined with big Knights and the army’s ability to take double gatling Despoilers. The result was an army that dominated an entire summer's worth of events, running three different Detachments and varying numbers of big Knights, including the Forge World Knight Atrapos.

Scores

  • Power: 4.5
  • Merits: 3.75
  • Mistakes: 4.25
Best Detachment: Infernal Lance

Worst Detachment: Helhunt Lance (by tie-break with Traitoris Lance)

What Worked

Unlike Imperial Knights, the Chaos Knights Codex offered a variety of interesting builds, with an interesting mix of big knights and war dogs across the Infernal Lance, Lords of Dread, and Houndpack Lance Detachments. Sure, the big knights were a little too busted but you had three solid ways to run them and for an army with only a dozen units, that’s a pretty impressive feat.

What Didn’t

GW never figured out the army rule for Chaos Knights; it went from irrelevant in the index to mostly irrelevant in the Codex, thanks in part to the fact that Battle-shock just doesn’t really matter in tenth edition.

Wings: The other glaring issue here was the internal balance, specifically that double gatling Despoilers with access to Lethal Hits (via Infernal Lance, the best Detachment) outclassed everything else in the book by a huge margin. The interaction between some of the Lords of Dread enhancements and the Knight Lancer was also fairly problematic, the Vect aura was just too much value on something with a flat 4++.

What We Learned

The important one here is that adding Wounds to a profile seems to consistently be better than expected - we’ll see this again later with C’tan. Big Knights got big point cuts as their durability was tweaked, and it turned out that probably wasn’t needed.

The other issue is that the Knight Lancer, specifically, is very warping for both Knight Factions. The 4+ Invulnerable save, including in melee, is too juicy of a durability boost in general to pass up, and the melee aspect neutralises what is supposed to be a faction weakness.

What We Want to See

A working army rule for Chaos Knights. The designers have done a good job making them feel distinct from Imperial Knights from a unit and flavor standpoint, but Chaos Knights have been continually saddled with army rules that are unreliable at best and just unworkable at worst.

Chaos Space Marines



Chaos Space Marines were a faction that started off stronger with their Index Detachment, then were toned down substantially when their Codex released, scaling back the power of the Pactbound Zealots Detachment and offering some interesting - but not overpowered - alternatives. For most of the edition, Chaos Space marines tended to occupy a spot firmly in the middle of faction power rankings, and they admittedly felt like the faction the rest of the game could be balanced around, rather than Space Marines, who tended to be more dominant following the update to Oaths of Moment in December 2024. That changed late in the edition, when the Red Corsairs and Defilers releases really tipped the scales and pushed them up to the rarified air among the game’s best armies.

Scores

  • Power: 3.25
  • Merits: 4.25
  • Mistakes: 2.25
Best Detachment: Renegade Raiders

Worst Detachment: Cabal of Chaos

What Worked

As a faction, Chaos Space Marines had a solid rule that gave them flexibility against different types of targets. The faction also had a mix of decent but not great units with very limited access to high-damage attacks and Command Point generation. The faction has several very good Detachments and they have clear strengths and weaknesses and while they have a large number of units, they also have a fair amount of redundancy - many of those units are melee units of some variety of another, filling slightly different roles. That said, the army is fairly fragile, often feeling like a glass cannon and requiring players to use multiple units to bring down big targets and avoid giving up free kills. At its best, CSM are a high skill army which encourage taking a variety of units.

What Didn’t

Of course, none of this mattered once Defilers were released, giving the army an insane shooting platform with high durability and enough melee power to brawl with other elite targets effectively. And Dark Pacts worked better when you lost units after making attacks, preventing situations where you’d lose an entire model or two before you even got the benefit. It’s also an example of an army rule that’s good, but comes with a drawback and as a result doesn’t stack up to most of the rest of the game’s “pure upside” rules.

Also, while it was interesting to see Chaos Cult be a playable competitive Detachment, Accursed Cultists ended up being a bit too strong. Being able to ally in Chaos Daemons was an interesting ability in theory, but in most games just meant adding a unit or two of Nurglings.

What We Learned

You can, in fact, make an army with half a dozen different melee units which are all viable and will see play in different lists and Detachments. You can make a large number of Detachments for a Codex and still have most of them be playable - the count for CSM was a whopping six out of their eight Detachments seeing real competitive success at some point or another, with only Deceptors and Dread Talons ending up duds.

What We Want to See

The Defiler needs to be toned down, but more generally we’d like to see Chaos Space Marines continue to develop a faction identity that moves them further away from loyalists - Detachments like Renegade Warband are a fun lore nod but ultimately CSM need to feel like a different army with different tools and quirks. They also need to have units that are distinct from what you see in the cult armies - that’s something they’ve been building toward, with varying levels of success. We’d also like to see more around the different legions - having an Iron Warriors character finally is awesome, and we’d love to see the Word Bearers, Alpha Legion, and Night Lords get similar treatment.

Death Guard



The poor Death Guard started 10th edition as one of the game’s worst armies, if not the absolute worst, and spent nearly a year suffering until they received an updated army rule that allowed them to tack an additional effect onto their contagion - most notably -1 to enemy saves - in order to actually do damage to enemy units. Then the Codex dropped and it was insanely pushed, with a number of units which were just too cheap for their power. The most egregious of those units were curbed by summer’s end, the mark of an army whose issues were almost entirely a result of being undercosted from a points standpoint.

Scores

  • Power: 5
  • Merits: 4
  • Mistakes: 4.25
Best Detachment: Virulent Vectorium

Worst Detachment: Tallyband Summoners (3-way tie-break with Shamblerot Vectorium and Death Lord's Chosen)

What Worked

The revised version of Contagions - one in which you can choose an additional effect at the start of the game - is good. The three modes aren’t at all equivalent, but generally speaking getting +1 AP on all of your attacks is enough most of the time and when it’s not -1 to hit will cover the rest. Other than that, the Death Guard are in most respects an ideal army for 40k. Not because they do everything well, but because they don’t - the army has some clear upsides and downsides, things it can and can’t do, and a limited but robust pool of units that offer different builds where almost every unit in the army has value.

Wings: The Datasheets in this book were also really, really good. Too good, at their starting points, but extremely well thought through to ensure that everything had a role that it could function in.

What Didn’t

Those initial point costs were also pretty crazy, and it was absurd that heavy blight launcher Bloat-drones were ever 100 points. Deathshrouds Deep Striking at 6” was probably also intended to be a bit more conditional than it ended up being, but points changes largely brought those in line.

What We Learned

You can in fact make an army of relatively slow units (5” movement) competitively viable without making them obnoxiously durable by giving them a mix of deep strike tricks, Infiltrate, and vehicles which can move 10”.

What We Want to See

We’d like to see contagion options be a little more balanced to create a real decision between the three - you almost never take -1” movement/Leadership/OC, but that could change in 11th edition… though we doubt it.

Emperor’s Children



The one truly new faction of 10th edition, the Emperor’s Children hit the ground running (and shooting and charging) with a book packed with high-powered datasheets. It had some interesting play, where a combination of speed and melee power made it an immediate threat in Teams environments.

Scores

  • Power: 3.25
  • Merits: 3.75
  • Mistakes: 2.5
Best Detachment: Coterie of the Conceited

Worst Detachment: Slaanesh's Chosen

What Worked

The idea of having an entire army which can fall back and Advance and charge, albeit with some restrictions, was really cool. The Emperor’s Children also had some very strong datasheets with some cool effects, and those worked well with the Coterie of the Conceited Detachment. Of course, any issues the faction had were solved by Defilers finally giving them a capable ranged unit.

What Didn’t

Fulgrim was just completely underwhelming as a Primarch and faction leader who did absolutely nothing for his faction. This also seems to be when Games Workshop decided to cut a bunch of units from the Emperor’s Children Codex, leaving them without Helbrutes, Tanks, or Cultists. The result was a threadbare book that has very few options and is difficult to balance. This also left the faction with no way to crack open enemy transports, making it one-dimensional and incapable of winning certain matchups.

What We Learned

Taking all of the ranged anti-vehicle options out of an army is pretty much a deal-breaker.

Isaac: Building off of this here I think is an edition wide problem. While there have sometimes been brief flashes in the pan where a mono melee army is good, such as Bully Boyz Orks or Creations of Bile CSM. To be viable this edition, more than ever you need good, preferably long range shooting. Shooting is just on balance stronger then melee since you do not need to be directly engaged to using shooting as opposed to melee, and really nothing has been done to solve this problem. Games Workshop seems to be allergic to giving out damage higher than 2 to melee units, while pretty much any unit can get damage 3 shooting. Looking into 11th, we see the hidden rule showing up, which I think is a decent first step, but I am skeptical if it will have a real impact or if guns will just walk 14.9” away and still blow you off the table.

What We Want to See

A return of some of the missing units. It never made sense for Emperor’s Children to not have tanks or Helbrutes, and not having them makes the army incapable of handling some threats and difficult to balance. Also, it’d be cool if they could make the Terminators not complete garbage.

Isaac: I would really like to see some more power placed into other Detachments, given that right now Coterie and Carnival hold basically all the faction power. If you want to play competitively you are pretty much going to be stuck with them. Emperor’s Children could be so much more than just Daemon Princes and Noise Marines, and while Defilers have helped with this some I hope 11th can bring some more units, more viable Detachments, and new playstyles.

Thousand Sons



The Thousand Sons were one of the game’s most powerful armies on the launch of 10th edition, but were overshadowed by Eldar and Wraithknights at the time. They’d soon rise to prominence in the months after Eldar nerfs, only to be tuned down substantially. Then their Codex dropped in early 2025 along with the other three Cult Legion books and they were once again an incredibly powerful faction, overshadowed by Death Guard and Chaos Knights and eventually nerfed. They’re still pretty good, though.

Scores:
  • Power: 4.25
  • Merits: 3.25
  • Mistakes: 3.75

What Worked

The Codex gave players multiple, legitimate ways to play the faction, either with Scarabs, Magnus, or vehicles. The book’s mechanics are generally solid, and Rituals give the army a solid punch against big units and singular threats that lets them be very effective in metas which focus on big threats like C’tan while having lots of flamers makes them equally effective into hordes.

Isaac: Thousand Sons players have multiple times this edition cried out in pain when they get nerfed, “This faction is dead!” “We were overnerfed!” and things of this nature. They just seem to keep winning, however. Thousand Sons have been blessed all edition with an ultra-powerful Codex and multiple different viable builds depending on the meta. Grand Coven has worked all edition long, with Rubricae Phalanx and Warpforge Cabal seeing time in the sun as well. Besides perhaps the Heldrake I have seen pretty much every Thousand Sons unit on the table at some point as a competitive choice.

Wings: It was genuinely hilarious that even after a sustained campaign of nerfs beyond anything any other Index received, I'd occasionally still get a 5-0 finish with it pop up in Competitive Innovations.

What Didn’t

Rituals were kind of needlessly complex and were too powerful on launch, causing issues and requiring errata before release. They’re also super parasitic, pushing you to taking only specific units.

Isaac: I want to briefly touch on something that has been forgotten by many players: Warpmeld Pact. This was perhaps one of the most busted Codex Detachments ever released, though it only lasted for a brief moment of time. If you do not remember how it worked, you could effectively make your units immortal due to the timing of an ability that gave you -1 to wound, allowing you to pull models after targets were declared but without a window allowing the attacking unit to retarget. Spamming twenty man bricks of Tzaangors, Chaos Spawn, and Mutalith Vortex Beast was one of the most broken things I have played with in testing. It hopefully taught Games Workshop a valuable lesson on rules timings that will carry over into 11th.

Wings: Don’t forget Tzaangor Enlightened as a serious contender for most undercosted unit in the game on launch.

What We Learned

Rituals were still overtuned even in their reduced form, where they more or less gave you Oath of Moment and several other key effects. On release players could just keep attempting each Ritual until they got it; that was later removed.

Isaac: Something I learned from this Codex is the power of core stratagems. With the granting of the Grenades keyword to many characters in the Codex release, Thousand Sons were able to use pretty much every core stratagem to extreme power. Overwatch in particular stands out with the abundance of flamer and improved overwatch rules. But they also used Rapid Ingress, Grenades, and even Heroic in powerful ways. I can point to many factions who have holes in their Codex where they simply never used a core stratagem at all. Maybe they did not have a good Deep Strike unit to rapid with, no flamer unit for overwatch, or had zero Grenade units at all. Thousand Sons having full access to every single one of these rules I think made a pretty sizable impact on why they were, and are, so strong.

What We Want to See

A little more support for strategies which don’t rely on Magnus, but otherwise the Thousand Sons are a pretty solid army.

Isaac: What I want to see from this Codex is more for the entire faction pool, not just Thousand Sons, to bring back Psychic! Do I think we need an entire Psychic table (or two) for each army, or a Psychic phase? No, not really. I do want to see more design space in this area. Grey Knight and Tyranids are two big factions that were deeply tied to Psychic powers and were deeply hurt by the removal of them from the game. I think the Thousand Sons Cabal of Sorcerers rule is a great blueprint. Maybe not every faction needs four spells, but giving Tyranids a Detachment that hands out two spells to them would be an awesome change for them.

Wings: I am deeply and personally hurt by Aeldari not being on that list.

World Eaters



World Eaters were the last Codex of 9th edition and got very little time before being reindexed. But it was clear that their book’s model of Detachments/subfactions shared a lot of the design principles of 10th edition. They sat most of tenth edition using their Index, then got an interesting boost with the release of their Codex, where they suddenly gained a solid shooting element thanks to an influx of BS4+ shots..They also had one of the only Daemon Detachments worth playing with the Khorne Daemonkin, which let you deep strike in units of Bloodletters any time something died.

Scores

  • Power: 2.75
  • Merits: 2
  • Mistakes: 2.25
Best Detachment: Berzerker Warband

Worst Detachment: Cult of Blood

What Worked

World Eaters Helbrutes are among the most fun units you can play, and almost everything in the codex is worth fielding in some form or another, from Berzerkers to Eightbound. Berzerker Warband is also a really exceptional melee Detachment.

Wings: I think the understated success of this book is that like some of the others, a lot of the changes in it were overreactions to things people hated about the Index, but unlike something like Custodes, the book was correctly re-balanced around its new capabilities, and came out pretty good. I was definitely guilty of underrating it on launch, but it’s had real staying power, and although Warband has been the default build most of the time, you’ve seen good successes with the Daemonkin and Goretrack as well.

What Didn’t

Angron’s resurrection ability eating your entire Blessings roll felt horrible and made him a unit to avoid for most of the edition - only coming in later on when you really needed heavy hitters. The idea of trading off one point of Ballistic Skill for extra shots with Rapid Fire also felt pretty lopsided, but may come back around to being too harsh in 11th edition when the benefit of cover becomes -1 to Ballistic Skill. Those extra shots at BS4+ are fine for most of the army but absolutely great on units like the Forgefiend, making them a must-take in too many lists.

Wings: I think the one really big stumble with this book was moving the base S of chainaxes to 4, and making Warband +2S. I understand the feeling that this needed to mirror the buffs Liberator got, but at only S4 Berzerkers don't consistently hit hard enough outside Warband, which is a major contributor to how dominant it has been. Making them S5 base with +1 from Warband would help open up options.

What We Learned

Do not, under any circumstances, make Deep Strike abilities that have no distance limitation. Simply do not do that.

What We Want to See

Make World Eaters Terminators worth playing and give them a character who can join them. The World Eaters roster feels pretty good but still has a few weird gaps for things like a fast jump infantry or cavalry unit. Berzerkers on Juggernauts would be an incredibly cool unit and finally give something for Invocatus and Juggalords to join that isn’t infantry. The World Eaters Leader situation is just weird right now.

Wrap Up

We'll be back later in the week to round out the factions with Xenos, and then we'll finish up with the content the internet craves - top ten lists. See you then!
