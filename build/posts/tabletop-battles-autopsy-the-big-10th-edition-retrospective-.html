Tabletop Battles Autopsy: The Big 10th Edition Retrospective - Space Marines

May 19th, 2026 via Goonhammer
As this edition winds down we’ve found ourselves looking backward on Tenth edition. It was, by any metric, the most popular and successful edition of 40k to date, growing the player base substantially and building on many of the lessons of 9th edition, streamlining a number of things.

With 11th Edition still at least a month away and the slow drip of content stretching things out to a crawl, we’re forced to look backward on what was. We know 11th Edition isn’t reindexing, but we also know a new edition means an entire cycle of new Codexes for each faction.

So with this look back we’re taking a deep dive into the factions of tenth edition and examining what worked, what didn’t, what we (and GW) learned about them, and where they might go in the future.

Methodology

Our methodology for this was to look at each Codex (ignoring Indexes wholly, except for Chaos Daemons and Deathwatch) and review it against three criteria:
  • Power: What was the power level of the Codex at launch?
  • Merits: How highly would you rate the good aspects of the Codex as initially released, from the point of view of someone who plays the faction?
  • Mistakes: How bad were any impacts on the game for everyone else from the Codex as initially released? Were there any complete disasters that needed to be rewritten?
Seven of our competitive-minded contributors scored each book on these criteria on a 1-5 scale, which we averaged out and then rounded to the nearest .25. The goal is to grade on a curve, and when we totted up the averages at the end we were hovering around a 3 on all the metrics, so there should be fairly even spreads.

To explain our thinking in scoring these, we can use Aeldari as an example, a book which (spoilers) scored 5s across the board. That means the Codex as released was very strong compared to others, that it had lots to recommend it for players of the faction (it was fun to play, a wide variety of datasheets were viable, the rules felt thematic and relevant), and that it also had severe external balance problems which needed resolving by more than just points changes.

Separately, we’ve also had our panel vote on what we think are the best and worst Detachments each faction had this edition once their Codex was released. This does take into account things like the Grotmas releases, which obviously weren’t included in the books to begin with, but we thought that it made more sense this way rather than trying to rate Ork Detachments while ignoring the Giant Squiggoth in the room that was More Dakka’s brief existence.

There are a ton of factions to cover here so we’ll be splitting this up into the classic superfaction groups of:
  • Space Marines
  • Imperium
  • Chaos
  • Xenos
We’ll then wrap up with a final part with some top tens on each metric, and final thoughts from our panel.

So sit back, grab a drink, and join us on this look at where things stand at the end of the edition.

Space Marines

Codex: Space Marines



The second Codex of the edition thanks to a heroic victory in the global campaign by Tyranid players, Codex: Space Marines more or less set the stage for the next three years by dropping the single most powerful Detachment of the edition: The Gladius Task Force. Sure, other Detachments have come out and been nerfed but between great offensive and defensive buffs and outstanding movement tricks Gladius dominated Marine Detachment discourse and created an impossibly high standard for new Detachments to be gauged against.

One of the edition’s biggest changes happened when Space Marines received a major balance pass in December 2024 (the first Grotmas), adding new rules for a number of Epic Heroes, datasheet updates, and an updated Oath of Moment rule for non-Supplement Chapters which gives attackers +1 to wound against their Oath target. Everybody owns Space Marines and as a result they tend to make up the largest proportion of players at any given event; when Marines are overpowered, it can create metas which feel oppressive.

Scores

  • Power: 3.25
  • Merits: 3
  • Mistakes: 3.5
Best Detachment: Gladius Task Force

Worst Detachment: 1st Company Task Force

What Worked

It’s hard now to think back to the Codex launch, with the extent to which its rules and datasheets have been tweaked and amended over the course of the edition, but for an early edition release the book broadly worked in a way that e.g. Tyranids didn’t. Space Marine armies played well and although Gladius Task Force remains the primus inter pares there have at least been real builds out of Stormlance Task Force, Ironstorm Spearhead, and Vanguard Spearhead at various points across the edition. As with any roster as deep as Space Marines, there’s some pretty useless datasheets in here, but a surprising number of them are good and there’s been meaningful list variety over the course of the edition.

What Didn’t

Look, with that said, Gladius Task Force is still king and represents the problem with having all-encompassing and very flexible Detachments. It just gives you every tool you need, and while it might not be as outright powerful in any one aspect as a more specialised Detachment, it’s good enough at everything that the alternative has to be really strong or really specialised to be worthwhile. The result was that even when new Codex Supplements dropped, it was common to see non-Codex chapters being run with Gladius Task Force, and while nerfs took the Ironstorm and Vanguard Spearheads out of contention GTF kept on trucking even after several rounds of rules and points changes. It’s just a really great Detachment, and while that isn’t a problem per se, at the end of 10th edition has 20 Detachments just for this book never mind all the ones in supplements too and people are still merrily playing the one that started life in the index.

Additionally, adding the +1 to wound against Oath targets was something that made a lot more sense when Ultramarines didn’t have as many bespoke units as the Codex Supplement chapters, which is what it was meant to compensate for the lack of. By edition’s end, it has become common to run into lists running three units of Victrix Guard, the Wardens of Ultramar, and 3-4 Ultramarines special characters. As something designed to entice players into taking the more “vanilla” Space Marine chapters, this didn’t quite work.

Liam: This is a difficult problem when Codex: Space Marines is really Codex: Ultramarines, Plus Some Other Guys, made much more acute by the late-edition releases of multiple new Ultramarines characters and units. Guilliman gunlines have been a thing basically since the edition started, and were the biggest beneficiary of the Oath change. I think doing something much more targeted to the four other Chapters might have worked, and eventually sort of did when the new Detachments with specific character restrictions came out.

Rob: I’ll also add that if the idea in 10th was that you can run whatever chapter you want with whatever rules you want, that idea quickly fell by the wayside late in the edition as they released updated chapter Detachments which more or less forced you to take a specific character. I was never a fan of removing subfactions; while I see the appeal – your rules aren’t tied to your paint job – the result just ended up being Bloodless Angels and Stormlance Space Wolves.

Liam: On the subject of Stormlance, that Detachment was written with rules that seemed to imagine far more bikers existing in the unit roster than actually do. It’s continued being relevant in spite of the rules not really gelling with what’s in the book, but it’s always struck me as a weird miss for that reason.

Wings: I think we have to be a little fair and say that later in the Edition, the studio got quite a bit better at writing incentives for non-Ultramarine lists, it's just that they kept releasing more good Ultramarine units as well. Shadowmark, Forgefather's and Hammer have all seen decent amounts of play (even rising to the top briefly in Shadowmark's case), with Shadowmark in particular standing out for a few weeks. In the end, however, the sheer value of Ultramarines keeps rising back to the top.

What We Learned

Not really new news, but Space Marines continue to grapple with the thorny problem of being the ‘main’ faction with the biggest player base, and getting outsized releases and support as a result, while also trying to slim the roster down to something manageable, and strike a balance between “Codex: Ultramarines” and “Codex: Space Marines.”

Also, toolbox Detachments can be great, and represent a faction really well, but somewhat defeat the object of having multiple ways to play if they’re too good - a problem which recurs in other factions too.

What We Want to See

We don’t necessarily want more Space Marine books every edition, but it would be more honest and probably easier to balance if this actually was Codex: Ultramarines, and the other First Founding Chapters were a supplement of their own.

Detachment-wise, an interesting challenge for the new approach will be to see if having smaller, more discrete building blocks leads to greater variety in lists, or whether we all just end up recreating GTF by the back door.

Codex Supplement: Black Templars



Black Templars was the final Space Marine supplement released, and a distinctly mixed bag. On the one hand, it was bold enough to actually try mixing up the Space Marine formula - no Oaths of Moment, replaced by Templar Vows, with Datasheets picking up the slack of providing re-rolls. On the other, the Detachments were a big miss - by the time this arrived, there were so damn many Marine Detachments that producing something that pushed them aside was a steep task, which this supplement wasn’t really equal to.

Scores

  • Power: 2.75
  • Merits: 1.75
  • Mistakes: 1.75
Best Detachment: Godhammer Assault Force

Worst Detachment: Companions of Vehemence

What Worked

Wings: The main achievement of this book, as alluded to in the title, was proving that with the right set of Datasheets and abilities you could have a Marine supplement without Oaths and it could still work. The +1 to Wound Vow was really good, and a couple of key units in here (Castellans and Repulsor Executioners) brought their own hit re-rolls along, which was enough to glue things together (alongside some strong Epic Heroes). The book also retained the Templars signature Big Crusader units, which created a relatively unique capability that could dominate in the right Detachments.

If you built around this stuff, the Templars had some competitive legs too. Early on, Gladius lists built around the Epic Heroes and RepExes filled with incredibly tooled-up Sword Brethren units put in serious performances, full Crusader horde broke out of Teams a few times early on, and once Bastion Task Force arrived it became a decent fringe plan. All these lists shared the drawback that they were very samey - generally the top versions had only a single unit of wiggle room around the stuff you had to include. Godhammer was fun if you happened to be specifically Campbell as well, which probably earns the book 0.25 of a point.

What Didn’t

The Detachments were a big miss, and as highlighted above, the successful builds were very one-dimensional. You were either building around a collection of over-tuned Datasheets in Gladius, a horde of Crusaders, or you weren’t bothering, and the idea of doing fun Templar Chaplain synergies just never really came up. Being primarily “more efficient Gladius” also meant the book had no real legs once points nerfs hit their best stuff; as soon as they’re not more efficient Gladius, the fun goes away.

The Vows were also very two-dimensional, as one option vastly outstripped the others in the majority of situations, taking away any fun decision making or flexibility. They also fell into the gaps of 10th’s design space; they’re a Templar-only army rule, but because all Stratagems are Detachment-linked, if you take any vanilla Marine Detachments you won’t have any Stratagems or Enhancements that interface with them at all. This could plausibly work better in 11th - honestly several of the Detachments here feel like they’d be more at home as 1DP add-ons to a main force, and you could put some Vow stuff in there.

The other issue for making the Templars interesting is that Chaplains got the short end of the stick amongst Marine Characters in 10th, lacking a really compelling identity and just being a slightly different force multiplier you could include if the numbers added up. This is only compounded in Templars by the Castellan being a must-take in most real units, leaving religious guidance on the shelf.

The Templar Codex did manage some interesting things, but in the end it couldn’t quite drive home the right feel for the army, and was mostly harvested for parts by builds that could leverage the pure efficiency on a few angles.

What We Learned

On a positive front, this book did show that with the right Datasheets, you could make a Marine army without Oaths and have it be fine. Oaths was such a big part of most Marine play in this Edition that it was genuinely good to see this attempted, and to have it mostly land. The flip side of this is that it was much harder to escape the gravitational pull of the massive number of Marine Detachments that were available, and creating an excitingly different toolset Datasheet-wise made it much better to play with existing ones than try the new stuff.

What We Want to See

Vows and Chaplains feel like they need another attempt to really land. The more modular Detachments of 11th could give these a chance to shine, but there's definitely also a risk that Templars' unique Datasheets get pulled into fairly generic shells on pure efficiency basis. Let's cross our fingers for the former.

Codex Supplement: Blood Angels



Landing in the middle of the edition, the Blood Angels Codex had some interesting impacts. It finally ripped out the free-for-all on options for jump pack Death Company, and also killed off a number of datasheets that had become pretty vestigial, but traded this for some pretty decent rules for playing the faction and some significant upgrades to datasheets like Sanguinary Guard, which quite frankly had sucked (and not in a cool vampiric way) in the Index.

Scores

  • Power: 3.5
  • Merits: 3
  • Mistakes: 2
Best Detachment: Liberator Assault Group

Worst Detachment: The Lost Brethren

What Worked

Looking at Blood Angels from a competitive perspective, they are certainly the strongest of all of the chapter expansions this edition but for some rather strange reasons. Unlike the Detachments featured in Dark Angels, Black Templars, and Space Wolves, Liberator Assault Group found use in a brand new archetype called “Bloodless Angels.” The gimmick here was that the +1 to Wound on Oath of Moment, intended to boost the generic Codex: Space Marines, checked that you didn’t have the keywords of a divergent Chapter. The response from players was to run a list featuring zero actual BA characters/units, which gave you access to the improved Oath of Moment while still running LAG. It turns out giving one of the best melee Detachments in the game +1 to wound on a stick was very very good, eventually leading to the entire archetype being killed forcing you to only run chapter Detachments as full fledged BA.

Outside of this on release there was not a whole lot of spice for players, but constant buffs throughout the edition have breathed new life into the codex, with Sanguinary Guard in particular getting multiple huge profile boosts, turning a rather mediocre unit into one of the hardest and fastest melee units in the game when considering all the buffs you can apply. Even more recently, GW updated the Sanguinary Priest to add Bladeguard as one of its Leader options, a meaningful boost to what both units can do. Pretty much every unique BA datasheet has had time in the sun, which is a decent result.

What Didn’t

Isaac: While on the datasheet side there was some big value, there was some pretty bad stuff on the Detachment side. BA faced a very similar problem to World Eaters in that they really really needed something to push them over the edge damage wise, and the only Codex Detachment that did that was LAG (Liberator Assault Group) so that was the only one you ever took. Both the other two Detachments were basically useless and I couldn’t name them without looking them up which tells you how relevant they are. Thankfully later on we received digital supplements that both hit it out of the park creating two entirely new BA lists. Angelic Inheritors and Rage Cursed Onslaught are both excellent Detachments, fun fact they both give a damage boost, but before them it was LAG or bust.

What We Learned

Isaac: The biggest thing that I pull from looking back at Blood Angels is that a codex can really get shined up when given some attention by GW. While the BA codex was just okay at release, mostly relying on bloodless angels to pull its competitive weight, consistent tweaks of datasheets and new Detachments have breathed a life into this codex that other marine supplements could only dream of. After taking index BA to Tacoma in 2024 I definitely was disappointed with the nerf to Death Company even if it was deserved. But now as a BA I can point to three different Detachments that are all worth you time and datasheets that work in each, one even bringing back Death Company and Lemartes to the lime light. Taking away the success of the BA book and sharing it with the other three chapter codex’s I hope to see in 11th, giving those underdeveloped datasheets and Detachments some new life like we see here.

What We Want to See

Late on in the edition, Black Templars showed up in not just some Detachments but a completely different army rule. That opens up a lot of design space that doesn’t exist otherwise, and lets you move power around differently - part of why Blood Angels lists were so focused on LAG for a long time is that the damage output the Detachment rule completely changes what you can expect your units to do on the table, in a way that’s hard to match. An army rule carrying that weight instead would let the Detachments compete on a more equal footing - a story mirrored by World Eaters, where Berzerker Warband has an identical Detachment rule which is similarly transformative for what the units can do. Extra-acute for Blood Angels and the other divergent Chapters, since Oath of Moment was objectively worse for them than the baseline codex in ways that didn’t really reflect the comparative performance between the books.

Codex Supplement: Dark Angels



A rocky initial release for the Dark Angels, with some nerfs to key datasheets (sometimes reflecting new kits, sometimes not) and mediocre Detachments. They at least got the rad-looking Inner Circle Companions out of it.

Scores

  • Power: 2.25
  • Merits: 1.75
  • Mistakes: 2
Best Detachment: Wrath of the Rock

Worst Detachment: Unforgiven Task Force

What Worked

Very little, at first. Readers only familiar with the Dark Angels’ later work may be baffled by how low the scores are here, because Dark Angels have been great in late 10th, but that’s because almost every aspect of their Codex has been buffed heavily since then. Initially you had Azrael as one of the best Marine Characters, and that was kind of it. At this point in time, super Oaths for Codex chapters didn’t exist, so you legitimately had Gladius and Ironstorm builds rock up that were Dark Angels solely for Azrael, but that’s a pretty dismal state of affairs.

Luckily, they’ve since had a long tail of great boosts. A big shake up of Inner Circle Companions and Deathwing Knights turned those into very playable units, the Vengeance was eventually buffed back up to higher damage, and the Lion got buffed and reworked so many times that he eventually ended up a bit over-the-top, a vicious god of war into any foes, and an absolute nightmare for melee ones to handle. After a few attempts, Wrath of the Rock also landed as an excellent Detachment for the more elite Infantry/Biker theme Dark Angels are meant to showcase, and the Ravenwing got some love from improvements to Company of Hunters.

What Didn’t

When we talk about bad books, a consistent trend is that the writers are clearly more afraid of it being too good than too weak, and Dark Angels were an early poster child for this. Every Datasheet was extremely cautious, and you had bizarre stuff like Belial’s fancy new model coming with a nerf compared to the Index Datasheet that, to be clear, no one ever used. Did you even remember that Belial had a new model in 10th? The Detachments also sucked either because they were afraid of pushing them hard enough (Ravenwing), the units that went in them were bad (Deathwing) or they hadn’t worked out how to write a non-Gladius all-rounder option yet (Unforgiven). This book was a mess, and the nicest thing that can be said about it is that they did a good job fixing it in balance passes.

Liam: One weird thing with this book is how much old stuff survived in it. Ezekiel is older than many of our readers, Sammael is resin too, the Ravenwing Black Knights are based on the 2nd edition bike model (albeit recut on a more modern frame), the Land Speeder Vengeance/Darktalon is still here when all other Land Speeders had been cut - like what are we doing here? In an edition where the cuts were otherwise pretty brutal, why is 32-year-old Ezekiel still kicking?

What We Learned

A lot of different ways not to write a Datasheet, and this clearly was taken on board given the improvements handed out later, and the fact that no book after this really landed with the Datasheets this dismal (Grey Knights was maybe more of a mess overall).

What We Want to See

More stuff with plasma weapons maybe? Most of the other Dark Angels themes are in a good place, but this has been hard to land with the more broadly applicable nature of Stratagems in 10th compared to previous Editions. Feels like something you could attack with the new Upgrades mechanic in a small, Hellblaster-focused Detachment.

Codex Supplement: Space Wolves



The Space Wolves Codex Supplement arrived fairly deep into the Edition, by which point the designers were very much live to two of the ways a Marine book could be a miss, either feeding new Datasheets into a Gladius shell or providing some busted new layer of rules on top of already good Marine units. The Space Wolves book tried to prevent that from happening and…yeah it was OK.

Scores

  • Power: 2.25
  • Merits: 2.5
  • Mistakes: 2
Best Detachment: Saga of the Beastslayer

Worst Detachment: A tie even after a tie-breaker from our wider community between Saga of the Bold and Champions of Fenris.

What Worked

The gimmick of Space Wolves was that both their Datasheets and Detachments were much less compatible with regular Marine stuff than for other Chapters. No regular Marine Leaders in Space Wolf units, and if you take a Wolf Detachment, only some of the rules will work for regular Marine units, while others are reserved for Mjod-enjoyers.

This was pretty high-risk, but worked out decently well, largely because the Datasheets were really worth it. The more constrained Leader combinations, in particular, let the power level be a bit higher without the risk of accidentally unleashing a broken combo, and as a result many of the Datasheets in the book have seen some sort of play. Even Thunderwolf Cavalry survived losing all of their Leader options thanks to some rules boosts.

The book had one very good Detachment in Saga of the Beastslayer, too. You could absolutely just build Stormlance or Gladius with the powerful Datasheets here, but Beastslayer was very real, and there were fringe Hunter builds that saw some usage elsewhere. The success of the Wolf Codex was that the incentive to go hard on using their units together meant that even the Gladius and Stormlance setups still felt distinctly like Space Wolves armies rather than just a flavour of the month Datasheet hastily painted to Battle Ready and slotted into existing lists.

What Didn’t

Several things. The incentives around doubling down on wolfing it up made armies of Space Wolves using regular Marine Detachments feel strongly themed, but the lack of cross-faction benefits bit more in their own Detachments, and the reason Beastslayer was the standout was that the Detachment Rule still gave most of its value to regular Marine imports. Lack of imported Marine Leaders was also baffling on Wolf Guard Terminators, where you had only two Leader options if you wanted them to have a boss, and no way to get Enhancements into the units (including, hilariously, from the Champions of Fenris Detachment that was meant to be partially Terminator-themed).

The book also had a couple of glaring mistakes at launch. Iron Priests, briefly, one of the most busted things in the whole game because no consideration appeared to have been given to how differently their buff would scale for various targets, while the ability to stack Logan and a Callidus Assassin and create a 12” bubble where opponents essentially couldn’t use Stratagems was definitely not great! Both got taken out at respectable pace, but the Iron Priest in particular should simply never have been able to escape from playtesting in that form.

What We Learned

As a first attempt at really playing with restrictions on compatibility, this book showed that it could have some positive impacts, and in general I'd like to see more attempts at that in 11th to stop Marines feeling like such a monolith. The risks were apparent too, however, and it's important not to leave units too much in the cold Leader-wise. Other than that, the Rune Priest was a reminder of the need to be careful of how buffs scale or stack in outlier situations, particularly when they're coming from Datasheets rather than Stratagems.

What We Want to See

Make Grey Hunters good, they’re pretty much the only unit in the Codex that has never gotten there, despite being super Intercessors on paper. Ideally give them one Leader for Thunderwolf Cavalry too. No double stacking. None of that nonsense again.

Index: Deathwatch (V2)



Deathwatch had one of the most unique journeys through 10th Edition. They had an Index at launch supporting their weird and wacky legacy Kill teams, but when the Imperial Agents Codex came round, the ability to field them as an army was whisked away in favour of them being part of that setup. This lead to some fairly substantial (and unusually justified) outcry, and later in the Edition the Deathwatch made a triumphant return, with their Detachment rocking back up largely unchanged, supported by a new set of Kill Teams leveraging all the Primaris options to extremely good effect.

Scores

  • Power: 4.5
  • Merits: 2.67
  • Mistakes: 4.25
Best Detachment: Black-Spear Task Force (by Default)

Worst Detachment: Black-Spear Task Force (by Default)

What Worked

I mean stepping back here - the decision to revive and update the Index was the right call. It is often correct to simply ignore fan outcry, but in this case the grievances were legitimate - people own Deathwatch armies, and the Imperial Agents Codex simply did not support running those. It probably could have (we'll get to some missed opportunities on that one later), but the version that landed didn't, and that was rough as hell, and going back on it was correct.

In terms of how the Index was updated, removing the old rules nightmare of the Proteus team and bringing in new Primaris options was a good choice too, and it did a good job of making all the Kill Team options pretty compelling, modernising the concept of mixed units pretty well.

What Didn’t

With all that said about it being the right call to give the tiny number of Deathwatch players their book back, it turns out to be really hard to balance a book at very low play rates. The Index landed at a pretty good power level, but it saw little play, and it felt like that was mistaken for it being bad. The army was gifted point cut after point cut until it suddenly struck top players just how powerful it had become, and the late edition win rates were spectacularly out of line (at least until we hit Defiler town, which they were only middling at handling). Fortis Kill Teams in particular are one of the more egregiously undercosted units in the whole game as the Edition closes, and the design of the Index amplifies their power - all the Kill Teams get flexibility and mobility from the Stratagems, so the second one is substantively over-pushed, the book is essentially guaranteed to be.

This also had the effect of somewhat muting the impact of what was meant to be the shiny new Kill Team that arrived from, uh, Kill Team, which has largely been ignored in favour of jamming more Fortis Teams and Veterans. Also, the Corvus Blackstar has been terrible all Edition, and its Datasheet needs to be sat down next to the Stormravens and really given a once-over.

What We Learned

Deathwatch are an army to a decent number of players, and while there are certainly versions of making them more an Inquisitorial than Marine force that would work, they need to incorporate more elements than just Kill Teams and Watch Masters to satisfy the demand. We haven't really seen anything yet about how the new Detachment system will handle allied forces, but it certainly seems like a mechanism you could use to have another go at this.

Outside of that - be really careful when doing balance adjustments for low play rate armies. If you were in the weeds of the metagame as I (Wings) usually am from reading every 4-1 list every week, it was pretty clear that Deathwatch did not, under any circumstances, need the last round of buffs they got, so something needs to be done to capture that signal more effectively.

What We Want to See

Deathwatch to continue feeling like an army. As above - you could go several ways about it, but hopefully the lesson of 10th is that this is the goal you need to centre when trying options out.
