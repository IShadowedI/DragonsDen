A Brush With Greatness 6: Kuri - Skin and Working With Resin

May 23rd, 2026 via Goonhammer
Welcome, my friends! Welcome back to Brush With Greatness, pull up a pew, take a load off - we’re moving on to the second of the busts I’m tackling on this journey, the fantastic falconer Kuri from Black Crow Miniatures, sculpted by the exceptionally talented Elina Karimova from artwork by Guillermo Gonzalez. Let me tell you, I love this miniature so much, the moment I saw her on the Black Crow website, I knew I had to paint her. She’s got a ton of character with her steppe robe and hat. The kit comes with options for two different creatures to perch on her outstretched hand, either a hawk or a fantastic dragon - after deliberation, I plumped for the hawk as it’s a bit more realistic, and I’m a sucker for a good bird.



The model is cast resin, which means the first port of call is always going to be bathtime. When a mould is prepared for casting, it’s covered in a release agent, typically some kind of grease, wax, or oil - this serves to make sure that the piece will actually come out of the mould and not get stuck, a bit like when you grease a baking tin with butter when you’re making a cake. The problem is that the release agent stays on the model and, as an unfortunate side-effect, will prevent paint from sticking to it altogether. Do not, my friends, attempt to prime a cast-resin model that hasn’t been cleaned - if you do, you’re in for a hell of a mess. So, how do we tackle this release agent? Well, fortunately, dish soap is really, really good at cutting through fats and oils, so we’re going to take a bowl of warm water, dish soap, and an old toothbrush and give our pieces a damn good cleaning, making sure to get into all the nooks and crannies where some of that grease might be hiding.

But wait! Soap will also really interfere with the ability of paint to stick to something, so you’re going to need to have a separate bowl in which to rinse all the soap off the model. Annoyingly, release agents don’t really have any kind of dye or colour to them, so it’s difficult to tell visually whether you got it all. On raised and flat surfaces, it’s not that difficult to tell. When a surface is properly cleaned, it should look flat and matt, rather than shiny. To confirm, a dry fingertip gently rubbed on the surface will usually suffice. The washed resin feels a bit rougher and more “grabby” than one with release agent still on it, but when it comes to the nooks and crannies, you’re going to have to just hope you did your best and really make sure to get into all those recesses with your toothbrush. After a good rinse under the cold tap and drying with a paper towel and hairdryer, it was time to take a look at the model and determine what might need cleaning up, either flash or gates that were left behind, or any other imperfections, like mould lines, that need to go. My weapon of choice when it comes to dealing with this stuff is the finest sandpiper I can get my hands on (The FEPA (Federation of European Producers of Abrasives) grades sandpaper in the EU on a P scale, the one I prefer to use is the P1200 ultra-fine) and very carefully work away at the unwanted material, since this sandpaper is essentially a polishing material, the result is super smooth and uniform.



Important safety information: I know I say this a lot, perhaps frequent readers will go, “Oh god is he going to say it again??” and the answer is, yes, yes I am. Working with resin is hazardous if you don’t take the proper precautions. 

Firstly: This particular model is from a reputable vendor I trust, but if you buy something from one less reputable, or someone casting resin in their garage, there’s a chance that there’s a void inside the model that’s filled with uncured resin (like the world’s worst Cadbury creme egg), ordinarily any resin models with hollow parts are safely drained through some kind of vent, but if this doesn’t happen, the uncured resin sloshing around inside the model is going to cause problems. Uncured resin causes nasty chemical burns on contact with skin and emits fumes that’ll irritate the hell out of your lungs - make sure you only buy cast resin from a reputable vendor.

Secondly: You’ll often need to file or sand resin models to clean up flash and gates, and I say this all the time, and I’m sorry, but I don’t care how tough and young you are and how invincible you feel - wear a fucking mask when you’re sanding or filing resin. Get a respirator if you can, but even a dust mask is better than nothing. Why? Because resin dust will get past all your natural defences and find its way into your lungs, and once it’s there, it’ll scratch the living shit out of them from the inside, causing permanent scarring (pulmonary fibrosis) that will stay there for the rest of your long life; these scars build up with each time you inhale a new batch of resin dust and will progressively impair your lung function, making it harder to breathe, permanently. We like to fool around and be glib here at Tabletop Battles, but don’t take any stupid risks; being able to breathe properly is nice, don’t take it for granted. 

Alright, now I’ve done my stern-dad safety presentation, let’s get back to what I was talking about before. Now that I’ve cleaned all the gates off the model and feel pretty good about how it’s fitting together, I temporarily stick the model together with UHU Patafix white-tac for priming. I chose to keep the arm and bird separate so as to retain good access to the face, which is always the most important part of any bust. You see, a bust is essentially a 3d portrait, and as humans, our brains are fundamentally hard-wired to seek faces in everything. Go and look at a painting or photograph featuring people,I guarantee that the face will be the first thing your eye is drawn to.



Anyway, onto the painting. Rather than flooding the model with the primer, I applied it carefully with several thin coats, building up to a full coverage of black. I always prime everything in black, in this case Army Painter Air black primer.

With the black primer done, I took some time to consider where I’d like the light source to be, before settling on a Rembrandt-style side light from the model’s left. White is always a pain in the neck, and even though I used an alcohol-based paint like Molotow White, it still ended up a bit speckly. It doesn’t matter that much either way, I just wish I could spray a nice, smooth non-speckly white.



Now that I have a rough idea as to where I’d like the shadows and highlights to go, I need to actively counter something that my friend Juan Hidalgo calls the “blue shift effect”. In essence, when the shadows are black, any skin tones painted directly over the top will show a bluish tint, making the figure look… well,  a bit bloodless and dead. As a way to prevent this corpse-like appearance, Juan suggests spraying all the shadows with a flesh coloured speedpaint beforehand, he uses Xpress Color Dwarf Flesh, while I’m using AP Speedpaint 2.0 Crusader Skin. 





With the shadows fully established in a skin-like colour, they looked better and more appropriate than the black shadows, so I moved on to establishing a base colour, for which I used Vallejo Game Color Athena Skin, thinned with my usual thinner and water and sprayed through the airbrush (you could also just use Game Air, but I don’t have any of that!)  



Hot on the heels of the Athena Skin, I skipped a step and went straight to Game Color Pale Skin to further define the volumes, particularly the cheeks, the nose, and the chin.She looked a little too pale after this, so I went in with both Speedpaint Crusader Skin and Goddess Glow to add more shadows and depth where I wanted it, mostly on the cheeks, beneath the brim of the hat, and underneath the chin.



I left the airbrushing of the skin there for now, come back next time to see how I airbrushed the robe and hat!
