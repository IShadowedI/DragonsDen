Tabletop Battles Reviews: For the Emperor (Illustrated and Annotated Edition), by Sandy Mitchell

August 14th, 2026 via Goonhammer
Image credit: Games Workshop

Sometimes things are best enjoyed in small doses. We all like cake, for instance, but not a lot of us would put their hand up for the opportunity to sit down and eat a whole one in one sitting. Today's book, For the Emperor, is just such a treat- but in order to best explain why we need to start on the other side of the equation here, the side that eats the whole cake.

For the Emperor originally released in 2003, following Ciaphas Cain's introduction in a couple of short stores published in the pages of Inferno! Magazine (Fight or Flight and The Beguiling). The self-interested Imperial Commissar and malodorous aide Jurgen's brand of dark comedy in a grimdark universe was a significant stylistic reach for Black Library audiences of the time, but it proved a roll of the dice that paid off.

Ultimately, author Sandy Mitchell would pen nearly a dozen novels in the series, culminating with 2024's Vainglorious after a six-year series pause. He very quickly figured out what elements resonated with readers and relentlessly delivered. When Cain's sophomore novel Caves of Ice entered the Black Library Readers' Hall of Fame, Committee member Michael Dodd of Track of Words noted:

This is really where it becomes clear that the Cain series is going to become the AC/DC of Black Library. That the whole point of the series is to hit the same beats and play the same jokes, because those beats and those jokes are just so damn good! There’s nothing wrong with wanting more of the same.

There's certainly something to say for giving the people what they want. And yet...

Meanwhile, on Gravalax

Cain's kickoff novel sees him assigned to a Valhallan split regiment, one comprised of the remnants of two different understrength ones. Cain must find a way to get both halves of his new deployment to work in unity with one another, all the while as they're stationed on a world riven by tensions between the Imperium and the tau, which have a strong trade and diplomatic presence on the planet.

That fragile detente is repeatedly threatened by attack and assassination, the pro-Imperial citizens clashing with the pro-tau separatists. Alongside Inquisitor Amberley Vail, Cain must find out what's behind all the trouble before conflict with the tau slips into a shooting war.

All the while, Cain continually seeks to preserve his own skin, continually managing to fail upwards through a combination of sheer dumb luck, keen survival instinct, and timely assistance from those around him.

Image credit: Games Workshop

Cain Fatigue

The past year has seen me increasingly enjoying Black Library's range of audiobooks. With so much of my paper reading in service to Tabletop Battles reviewing the latest releases, audio has been a fun way for me to catch up on the backlog of older books and revisit some of the classics (you can find those reviews here). But as I've worked in Mitchell's Cain catalogue, the less I've come away enjoying it. Heresy, right?

It's not that the books are bad, far from it! There's good reason that Mitchell's seen four of them end up immortalized in the Hall. They're both very popular, and were a great early example of what could happen when you were allowed to push the boundaries of the setting in directions they'd not really been before.

It's just that as Mitchell has returned to the same wellspring of inspiration, it becomes increasingly clear when read in quick succession just how repetitive they can get. In my review of Steve Lyons' The Relentless Dead I discussed the idea of "Flanderization," indicating "the gradual reduction of a character into its most oversimplified and exaggerated traits." The term was coined by TVTropes, describing the process by which Ned Flanders became more and more a caricature of himself over successive seasons of The Simpsons.

Yes, Mitchell hits the same beats, AC/DC style, across the series, but at the same time the series noticeably starts to Flanderize. The occasional mention of Jurgen's more piquant qualities, for instance, quickly loses its humor when the man can't even be in a scene without a joke at his expense. For this reason, I typically recommend enjoying Cain more as an occasional palate-cleanser than an all-in bingeworthy experience.

Or put another way- since the more you eat the less you enjoy each bite- I suggest it's best devoured as an occasional slice of cake.

Image credit: Games Workshop

Impressions

I bring all of this up to set the table for what was the most surprising realization for me as I read For the Emperor again (this time, of course, with the annotations): this book is so damn good. 

That probably sounds obvious, but as someone who has soured a bit on the repetitiveness of the series it was delightful to see it how clean a tale Mitchell wrote when he wasn't leaning in to the things that made the book popular after publication. 75% of the Jurgen body odor jokes...gone. Just a few sprinklings of the "if I'd known then what I know now, I would have [not done the thing I'm about to do]" constructions, a reasonable amount of his internal cowardice monologuing... all of those "Cain doing Cain things" or "Cain being Cain" moments in balance with the traditional narrative elements of the book instead of shading them out... if there had to be a formula, this was the one to use.

Mitchell has a wonderfully subtle and witty style of writing. It's sprinkled with lines like, "I sat for a while longer sipping water from my canteen and chewing a ration bar, the flavour of which, as usual, hovered just outside the range of identification," or "a level head is a fine asset on the battlefield, but not when it's been shaped like that by a fragment of shrapnel." It's not that this more subtle humor disappears from subsequent novels (it doesn't), so much as here it has more room to breathe making the story a delight instead of a groan.

Image credit: Wizards of the Coast

Special Edition Extras

The illustrations for the book were provided by Alex Brock who, while a newer face for Warhammer, has done a lot of work for Magic: the Gathering and other tabletop games. Brock, who once described his style as "old master style oil painting with a dark fantasy twinge," gives us an almost monochromatic style accentuated by a very muted gold tone. It's a striking style that I felt matched the book terrifically, with only the tiniest of missteps (there's a scene drawn towards the end of the story showing Cain triumphant with his Commissar's cap, but he'd lost the cap earlier during a wall collapse when underground).

While Mitchell has big shoes to fill following Robert Rath's annotation spree in The Infinite and the Divine (review), he packs a lot of insight into his 130 footnotes. Since Mitchell's Cain stories already use frequent annotations (representing the remarks of Amberly Vail), the reader very quickly gets accustomed to navigating two different sets of annotations based on the color of the ink.

Like previous authors Dan Abnett, Chris Wraight, and Rath, Mitchell takes the opportunity to both pull the curtain back on the writing process for the book as well as hold forth on other topics of interest to the reader. These include:

  • How Jurgen's pornography addiction became the series' first running gag (#44)
  • The value of briefing scenes as stealth exposition (#72)
  • Doing the work to set up the reveal of a mystery (#98)
  • The character development of Cain and other primary (and secondary) characters
  • And perhaps most candidly, how Cain was influenced by Mitchell's own struggles with autism (#47)

There's even one instance (note #68) where Mitchell takes the opportunity to correct something the original typesetter got wrong with the footnotes, likely making this the first time this 'corrected' text has seen print. In addition, Mitchell offers both an introduction and an afterword (even if the former is a bit abbreviated).

Final Thoughts

We're now six books in to the Illustrated and Annotated Series releases, and every one of them has been exceptional. They're some of the best stories in all of the Black Library's canon, wonderfully decorated and filled with additional insight from the author. All that in a hardcover book with bonus treatments (sprayed edging and ribbon bookmarks) typically reserved for higher-end editions. And all this for USD $40?

Long may it last.

This was a worthy entry in the series, and shows Cain at his very best. Don't let this one pass you by.
