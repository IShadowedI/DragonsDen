Hextoricals: Infernal Machine Dawn of Submarine Warfare

May 18th, 2026 via Goonhammer
You strain against the crank, trying to turn it as much as possible while James, the man next to you, seems to be on the verge of exhaustion. From the aft of the ship, someone is shouting, begging to be let out of this metal coffin, while the man next to him desperately tries to calm him down. You aren't sure where you are, how close the enemy ship is, or how long you will have to keep rotating the crank, when the captain finally shouts "Brace for impact, full back!". Suddenly, your world is just noise; the loudest explosion you ever heard envelopes you, and the fishboat is rocked to its core. The hull cracks and splits and leaks start to form. As the water starts filling the fishboat and you hear reports of damage to the gear box and propeller, you aren't sure if you will see the sky ever again, nevermind getting back home.

Welcome to the dawn of submarine warfare.

Infernal Machine is one of the latest offerings by GMT Games and attempts to model an often neglected part of history: The dawn of submarine warfare, focusing on the trials and tribulations of the inventors, pilots and crew of the submersibles of the American Civil War. Although submarines (or fishboats, as they are called by the rulebook) had first been developed decades earlier, the American Civil War had the unique distinction of featuring the first successful attack by a submarine against an enemy vessel, with the CSS H.L. Hunley (named posthumously after its inventor) managing to sink the sloop-of-war USS Housatonic, although the Hunley was subsequently lost with all hands.

There are several different modes of play available within the game:
  • The tactical board, which simulates the actual moment of attack by the fishboat against an enemy vessel, bridge, obstruction or wharf, and is mostly concerned with avoiding immediate detection and getting to the right place to detonate your charge, depending on your method of conveyance.
  • The mission board, which simulates the fishboat being launched and attempting to travel the coast or river of various locales in an attempt to perform a specific mission, be it training of its crew, attacking an enemy vessel, spying, blowing up a bridge or delivering men or cargo.
  • The campaign board, which simulates the fishboat actually being built, along with gaining investors, recruiting mechanics, journeymen or sailors, managing your finances as a private citizen or attempting to influence your respective government in order to be allowed to perform offensive missions against the enemy.
During the course of a game and especially during the campaign (which is the intended mode of play of Infernal Machine) you will be swapping from one mode to another depending on the situation and your own decisions, and all modes are meant to interlink together in a continuous gameplay loop. The decisions you make at the design-building stage will affect how effective the fishboat is during the mission, and the state of your submersible during the mission will affect your chances of victory on the tactical board.

The dawn of submarine warfare was a hazardous time, however; strong currents and random malfunctions will plague you at every step, and your crew will panic and become fatigued if they aren't trained well enough. Even if you do manage to destroy your target, the concussion wave from the explosion might destroy your own boat, or strain your crew enough that you can't make it back home. The game really manages to capture how dangerous and experimental this era of warfare was.

Unboxing and Materials

The box opens to a veritable cornucopia of game aids, rulebooks, counters, cards, wooden pieces and other gaming materials. What's especially welcome is that most of the boards are mounted and double-sided, with the only exception being the historical submarines used during the mission scenarios. I usually prefer mounted boards over paper because they tend to be harder to damage and provide a much sturdier platform for game pieces and counters, which are often sent flying if a paper board is even lightly jostled.

All of the game pieces, excluding the various rulebooks and player aids. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

The materials include the mission boards for the the many different locations where the submersibles were employed, a tactical board with a training mission on the other side, the campaign board that features gauges for both the USA and CSA campaign, two double-sided paper boards with pre-built historical submarines, the different parts and mechanisms used to build your own fishboat, a deck of personae for both sides, navigation cards, discovery cards, target counters, three 10-sided dice, and countless status/tracking markers for use throughout the game.

The game also has two player aids, one focused on the tactical/mission boards and the other on the campaign board. It also provides an incredible number of booklets: the rulebook, the scenario book, two campaign almanacs and a cyclopedia. The latter is especially interesting, since it details the history and use of the many components and mechanisms used in the construction of the fishboats, along with histories of the men that built, piloted and often died within these iron contraptions.

It's clear that GMT was proud of this production, as an extra player aid is provided that details every single component present in the box. However, this premium production comes at a literal cost, since Infernal Machine comes at $93 USD in retail, which is a hefty price for what is an entirely solitaire experience.

Gameplay

The main issue with learning Infernal Machine is that the game features three distinctive gameplay loops, all interconnected with each other; the campaign loop allows you to initiate the mission loop, which itself can initiate the tactical loop. In order to ease you into the game, Infernal Machine makes use of its scenarios as a sort of tutorial; first teaching you a simplified version of the tactical loop, then building up on that with a series of mission scenarios, and finally providing a mini-campaign once you are comfortable with the base concepts of the game. The scenario booklets also feature examples of play for all three loops, which are immensely helpful since they outline every single step that you have to take in the correct sequence.

The CSS H.L. Hunley attacking the USS Housatonic. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

The tactical loop represents your fishboat approaching and attacking a prospective target, be it a gunboat, sloop, ironclad, obstruction or even a bridge. The first thing you do after setting the specified visibility and elevation is determine your vector of approach; attacks in this era were preferably done perpendicular to your target in order to cause the maximum damage possible. This is done by rolling a single d10 and adding the training level of your crew: the higher the result, the better the angle of attack will be. You can modify this result after the roll by increasing your crank intensity, although this can rapidly fatigue your crew.

Once the approach vector is set, you go through a loop of detection, detonation (if you are in range) and movement. Once you are close enough to your target with your chosen conveyance system, you detonate your charge and hope that you don't blow yourself up at the same time, which is done through the drawing of fate cubes; representations of both luck and the general morale of the crew.

The three tactical scenarios go through the three main ways that fishboats attacked their targets during the American Civil War, which are all crazy and dangerous in their own respective ways. The first conveyance method is using a wooden spar with the explosive charge attached at the end; I don't need to tell you how potentially dangerous this can be. The second conveyance method is towing the explosive charge behind you: this requires you to dive underneath and past your target, which is generally safer than the spar but makes it more likely that you will be spotted, since the charge is bobbing on the surface behind you. The third and craziest conveyance is to ground your fishboat, open an airlock and let a diver walk along the seafloor or riverbed carrying the charge, attach it to the target and then walk all the way back. If you move at all, the airline to the diver is cut and there is no hope to rescue them. An incredibly terrifying prospect!

The tactical scenarios are obviously meant to be played as tutorials and are incredibly barebones, with malfunctions and fatigue only used to determine the likelihood that your submersible made it back home, and players are unlikely to return to them once they have learnt the ropes.

The Pioneer on its first training mission. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

Mission scenarios are much more compelling, and they feature both historical and semi-historical operations that happened throughout the war. They all feature historical fishboats, including the Hunley and the USS Alligator. The first mission features the Pioneer, one of the first fishboats invented by Hunley, and is a simple training mission: set out with your manually-powered submarine, submerge and then come back. Sounds easy, but even such a simple task can have hidden dangers.

The basic loop is that you first assign orders to your crew: these can take the form of steering, mechanic or repair orders; anyone not given an order is automatically assigned to cranking the engine instead, although anyone panicking can't crank the engine or receive an order. Next comes navigation; along with allowing you to move your fishboat to another area and change elevation, it also forces you to draw a Navigation card, which are the heart of the game and the crucial piece of the puzzle that makes the game interesting.

The Navigation cards force you to have enough engine power to fight the currents and the sea, although you can temporarily exert your crew further in order to boost your engine. If this is still not enough, you can potentially suffer panic amongst your crew, or your morale could drop, or your fishboat could become grounded, drift out to sea or even be lost immediately. Exerting your crew, however, makes them fatigued, which means that your engine power will decrease in any future turn. This feeds into itself like a vicious cycle; the more fatigued your crew is, the more likely it is that you will need to fatigue them further, eventually making it impossible for your fishboat to make it back home.

The above, along with the random malfunctions that were endemic to these early attempts at submarine warfare, make even a simple excursion without enemy contact potentially fatal. Sailing in enemy waters adds the additional danger that you can potentially be shot and damaged, which further saps your resources and engine power as you are forced to order crewmen to repair the malfunctions.

Other scenarios feature the historical operation where the Hunley destroyed the Housatonic and was subsequently lost, as well as a prospective operation by the Alligator against a bridge that was cancelled before it could go ahead.

The starting setup for a CSS Campaign. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

Once I had finished a couple of the mission scenarios, I decided to go to the deep end and do a full CSA campaign. The campaign loop is pretty simple: You gain income from your shop and any contracts that you might have, roll a random event for your current year and season, then perform a series of actions, which can include recruiting personae, building your fishboat, entering contracts, applying to your government for either a Letter of Marque (for the CSA) or a commission (for the USA) and electing to go on missions. Several personae in the game can increase your level of expertise, which grants you access to more sophisticated hull pieces and mechanisms. Initially you can only go on training missions, which can increment your training level, but once you have been given approval by your respective government, you can go on offensive missions as well.

My first campaign had a quite interesting narrative arc: I started out in New Orleans, which made it easy to gain funds and recruit journeymen for my crew, but before my fishboat was complete, I was forced to relocate to Charleston as New Orleans was captured by Union forces. The first winter was very tough as well, since the influenza virus claimed the life of my best mechanic. Eventually I was able to test out my creation and apply for a Letter of Marque, and after strapping a long spar tipped with a large explosive to my fishboat, I set sail to hunt the blockading Union ships. My first mission was a dismal failure, since I was unable to find a single Union vessel, but my second mission was a complete success and I was able to destroy my target and return safely, albeit with heavy damage. A year later, I was out again, bagging another sloop and returning safely. Alas, my luck ran out, and in the Summer of 1864, the CSS Neptune went out on a final mission: swept out to sea further than intended, I tried to force the issue and was unable to outrun my own explosive charge, losing the Neptune and all her crew.

The gameplay loops are tied together extremely well, and the decisions of what to build and who crews your fishboat really affects the outcome of each individual mission.

Historicity

It is clear from reading the rulebook that the designer of the game has an abiding love for both the era and the personalities involved. The rulebook is replete with flavourful snippets and rule explanations, and it's clear that a lot of research has been done not just on the technology and submersibles of the American Civil War, but also the people which risked their lives to pioneer submarine warfare. The booklets make a note that many of the people that crewed these boats were lost to time, with very little information on who they were, what they looked like or the life they lived. Many games that focus on the machines of war tend to lose the human element, and I feel that Infernal Machine goes out of its way to memorialise these brave, unknown men, and it should be commended for that.

Additionally, the rulebook has an important aside right on its very first page; stating that at the start of the war, both sides allowed the enslavement of people, and that one side actively fought to preserve this inhumane institution. It also gives advisories on the grisly nature of armed conflict, as well as the fact that for many of the investors and inventors of this new technology, there was a definite profit motive in creating machines designed to end the lives of others. I think such an advisory is more than welcome and is a real breath of fresh air in comparison to many other American Civil War games that tend to skirt around the major issues of the conflict.

Critiques & Quirks

I got the first edition of the game, and although most of the materials are of excellent quality, there are some very obvious misprints within the rulebook; there is more than one occasion where an entire paragraph is repeated wholesale, or is misaligned or overlapping other text.

The CSS Neptune on its maiden voyage. Credit: Riccardo Fabris

Even though the game has a lot of player aids, there is still information that felt a little bit hidden or hard to access. One such example is that you always have to keep the chunky cylcopedia booklet handy, since its back cover has the random malfunction chart and also details what the malfunctions actually do. Some tables are hidden deep within the rulebook or campaign almanac, and are a chore to access every time they are required. Having to hunt out for a specific chart was a definite annoyance when playing the game.

In terms of the game itself, the main problems are the length of the game; a full campaign will take you far longer than the advertised 30 to 90 minutes, with mine taking 3 to 4 hours to go to completion. The other issue is with the scenarios: although some of them are interesting from an historical context, it is clear that the intended mode of play is the full campaign, and I can't see myself trying each of the mission scenarios more than once.

The main issue I found during the couple of campaigns I played was that I'm not quite sure how much longevity the game has. Even by the end of my first campaign the game was feeling a tad repetitive, although the moment-to-moment decisions were still quite exciting.

I'm also not sure that there is enough variety in the way that the submarines are built: although the different conveyance methods are interesting historically, they aren't much different in terms of actual gameplay. It's also not especially hard to build a fishboat very similar to the Hunley and thus avoid many of the early pitfalls that plagued the inventors of submersibles. I wish that instead of just adding the expertise level amongst your crew and shop to determine what equipment you have access to, there was a system of trial and error that simulated the early attempts to build a submarine. It is also possible to create surface-running boats powered by boiler engines (or even boats powered by early electrical/magnetic engines) and I've yet to try the USA campaign, but I can't imagine that either will significantly affect the base gameplay loop.

Final Thoughts

I've had quite a lot of experience with solitaire campaign wargames in the past, with several campaigns of The Hunters (and its sequels) and B-17: Queen of the Skies under my belt. I think in comparison to them, Infernal Machine really shines. In many other campaign games, you have very limited ability to directly affect the outcome of missions, with limited options to set engagement ranges or the number of torpedoes you fire.

In comparison, the gameplay loop in Infernal Machine is much stronger; not only are you building your own machine instead of using one provided by the game, but the decisions that you make during a mission can really impact the success of the mission and if your crew get back home or not. The crew-management aspect is also a lot more complex, and you have to make some real decisions in terms of who gets fatigued or if you can afford to send someone to repair a malfunction instead of having them crank the propeller.

I found the narratives created by the game incredibly compelling, and the game itself is quite unique in both the subject matter as well as the mechanics it uses: when you are out there braving the open sea, you absolutely feel how much of a struggle it was for these fishboats to ply the waves, as underpowered as they were. The game builds an incredible level of dread; did you spend too long hunting for targets and left your crew exhausted and far from home? The enemy in the game is not just the opposing forces hunting you, but also the sea (or river) itself, and the game portrays that in a very compelling way.

Overall, I really enjoyed my time with the game, especially considering that I have a lot of interest in submarines and the American Civil War. If you want to play a narrative campaign-driven game but have been disappointed with the gameplay loop of other narrative wargames, I would recommend giving Infernal Machine a try, since it is such a unique take on the genre.
